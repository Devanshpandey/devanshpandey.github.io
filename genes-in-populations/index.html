Genes in Populations

A ground-up course in population genetics and statistical genetics: how genetic variation arises, how it moves through populations, and how we use it to find the genes behind human traits and disease.

This book assumes very little. If you know that DNA is a string of four letters and you remember what a mean and a variance are, you can start at Chapter 1 and read straight through. Each chapter builds one idea, shows it in a figure, and ends with the handful of results worth remembering.

The two halves of the subject are usually taught apart. Population genetics asks what happens to genetic variants over generations: why some become common, why most disappear, and what the pattern left behind says about history. Statistical genetics asks how to connect variants to traits in data collected today. The second depends on the first at every step. Linkage disequilibrium, the reason a genome-wide association study works at all, is a population-genetic fact. So is the confounding that can ruin one. The book is arranged so that by the time a method appears, the reason it works has already been explained.

Genomes and genetic variation

Before any mathematics, we need the objects the mathematics is about: chromosomes, the differences between them, and the way they are shuffled when parents make children.

The genome in numbers

A human genome is a sequence of about 3.1 billion DNA letters (bases: A, C, G, T), split across 23 chromosomes. You carry two copies of it, one inherited from your mother and one from your father, so each of your cells holds about 6.2 billion bases on 46 chromosomes. Organisms with two copies are called diploid. Chromosomes 1 to 22 are the autosomes; the remaining pair are the sex chromosomes, X and Y.

Roughly 20,000 stretches of the genome are protein-coding genes. Their coding portions (exons) make up only about 1–2% of the sequence. A further several percent regulates when, where and how strongly genes are switched on, and most of the rest has no known function.

Variants, alleles, genotypes

Line up two human genomes and they match at about 999 positions in every 1,000. The positions that differ are variants. The kinds you will meet most often are:

  • Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs): one base swapped for another. When an SNV is reasonably common in a population it is traditionally called a single-nucleotide polymorphism, or SNP.
  • Indels: short insertions or deletions, by convention of fewer than 50 bases.
  • Structural variants: deletions, duplications, inversions or rearrangements of 50 bases or more, often thousands.
  • Short tandem repeats: a short motif, such as CAG, repeated a variable number of times.

A position in the genome is a locus (plural loci). The alternative versions found at a locus are its alleles. Most variable sites in humans have just two alleles, so we can label them: the reference allele (whatever appears in the reference genome) and the alternate allele; or the ancestral allele (the original state) and the derived allele (the newer mutation); or the major and minor allele by frequency. The minor allele frequency (MAF) is the frequency of the rarer one.

Because you have two copies of each autosome, at each two-allele site you carry one of three genotypes: two reference alleles, one of each, or two alternate alleles. The first and last are homozygous; the middle one is heterozygous. Statistical genetics almost always codes a genotype as the count of alternate alleles: 0, 1 or 2. A study of \(n\) people at \(M\) sites is then just an \(n \times M\) table of 0s, 1s and 2s, and nearly every method in Part IV is an operation on that table.

The sequence of alleles along one physical chromosome is a haplotype. Genotypes tell you how many alternate alleles someone has at each site but not which ones travel together on the same chromosome. Working that out is called phasing (Chapter 16).

Where variants come from: mutation

Every variant began as a copying error or unrepaired damage in a single cell of a single individual. For single-base changes the human mutation rate is about \(1.2 \times 10^{-8}\) per base per generation. Multiply by the 6.2 billion bases a child inherits and you get roughly 70 new mutations in each newborn that neither parent carried. Most land in sequence where they make no difference; a few alter a protein or a regulatory element.

The mutation rate looks tiny, but there are a lot of people. With eight billion humans, every single-base change that is compatible with life arises somewhere in the world many times each generation. What is rare is a new mutation surviving and spreading, which is the subject of Part II.

How variants are transmitted: meiosis and recombination

When you make an egg or sperm, each one gets a single copy of every chromosome. That copy is not simply your maternal chromosome or your paternal one. During meiosis the two are paired up and swap segments, so the chromosome you pass on is a patchwork of the two you received. The swap points are crossovers, and the process is recombination.

Two quantities describe recombination between a pair of sites:

  • The recombination fraction \(c\) is the probability that a transmitted chromosome is recombinant for the two sites. It runs from 0 (the sites are always inherited together) to ½ (they are inherited independently, as sites on different chromosomes are).
  • Genetic distance is measured in centimorgans (cM). Two sites are 1 cM apart if a crossover falls between them in 1% of meioses. For short distances, \(c \approx\) distance in cM ÷ 100.

Averaged over the human genome, 1 cM corresponds to about one million bases (1 Mb), so two sites 10,000 bases apart are separated by recombination only about once in 10,000 meioses. An egg or sperm carries a few dozen crossovers in total. The rate is far from even: much of human recombination is concentrated in narrow hotspots one or two thousand bases wide, with long cold stretches between them.

Why this matters later

Because nearby sites are so rarely separated, alleles that sit close together on an ancestral chromosome tend to stay together for thousands of generations. This statistical association between neighbouring variants is linkage disequilibrium (Chapter 9). It is the reason we can measure half a million variants and learn about ten million, and the reason an associated variant is not necessarily the causal one.

How variants are measured

Two technologies produce most genetic data.

Genotyping arrays test a fixed list of several hundred thousand to a few million known variable sites. They are cheap, which is why biobanks with hundreds of thousands of participants used them first. The unmeasured sites are then filled in statistically by imputation (Chapter 16).

Sequencing reads the DNA directly, either the whole genome or only the protein-coding exons (the exome). Sequencing finds nearly all single-base and short variants, including those so rare they are seen in one person in a study, which arrays and imputation cannot recover. Rare variants need their own statistical methods (Chapter 20).

Key points

  • Humans are diploid: two copies of a 3.1-billion-base genome, one from each parent.
  • A variant is a position where chromosomes differ; its versions are alleles; a person's pair of alleles is a genotype, coded 0, 1 or 2.
  • New mutations arise at about \(1.2\times10^{-8}\) per base per generation, roughly 70 per child.
  • Recombination shuffles the two parental chromosomes; sites close together are rarely separated (about 1 cM per Mb).
  • A haplotype is the set of alleles on one chromosome. Most methods in this book are about haplotypes or about the 0/1/2 genotype table.

A person is heterozygous at two nearby sites. Why can their genotypes not tell you their haplotypes?

Genotypes give the count of alternate alleles at each site: 1 and 1. That is consistent with both alternate alleles lying on the same chromosome (and none on the other) or with one on each. The two arrangements are different pairs of haplotypes with identical genotypes. Resolving them is phasing.

Two sites are 2 Mb apart. Roughly how often is a chromosome passed to a child recombinant between them?

At about 1 cM per Mb they are about 2 cM apart, so a crossover falls between them in roughly 2% of meioses: \(c \approx 0.02\).

Each child carries about 70 new mutations. Why is nearly everyone healthy anyway?

Only 1–2% of the genome is coding and a further limited fraction is regulatory, so most new mutations land where they have no effect. Of those that do hit functional sequence, many are synonymous or mild, and most damaging ones are masked by the working copy on the other chromosome.

A probability and statistics toolkit

Genetics is a science of chance: which allele a parent passes on, which individuals happen to leave children, which people end up in a study. This chapter collects the few tools the rest of the book uses again and again.

Random variables, mean, variance

A random variable is a number whose value is decided by chance: the count of alternate alleles a child inherits, or a person's height. Its expectation (or mean) \(E[X]\) is its long-run average. Its variance measures spread around the mean:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(X) = E\big[(X - E[X])^2\big]. \]

The square root of the variance is the standard deviation (SD), which has the same units as \(X\). For two variables, the covariance \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])]\) measures whether they move together, and the correlation is the covariance rescaled to lie between −1 and 1:

\[ \rho = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\mathrm{SD}(X)\,\mathrm{SD}(Y)}. \]

Two rules do a great deal of work. Expectations always add: \(E[X+Y] = E[X]+E[Y]\). Variances add only with a correction for covariance:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(X+Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y) + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y). \]

When \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent the covariance is zero and variances simply add. The whole of quantitative genetics (Part III) is built on splitting the variance of a trait into pieces in this way.

Four distributions

Binomial: counting successes

Make \(n\) independent trials, each succeeding with probability \(p\). The number of successes \(X\) follows the binomial distribution, with

\[ P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad E[X] = np, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p). \]

This is the distribution of genetics. Drawing \(2N\) alleles to form the next generation, when the current allele frequency is \(p\), gives a binomial number of copies. Sampling \(n\) chromosomes from a population to estimate an allele frequency is binomial too.

Poisson: counting rare events

When \(n\) is large and \(p\) is small, the binomial is well approximated by the Poisson distribution with a single parameter \(\lambda = np\), which is both its mean and its variance: \(P(X=k) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\). The number of new mutations in a genome is close to Poisson. Crossovers on a chromosome are more evenly spaced than a Poisson process would give, because one crossover inhibits another nearby.

Exponential: waiting for a rare event

If an event happens at a constant rate \(\lambda\) per unit time, the waiting time until it first happens is exponential, with mean \(1/\lambda\). In Chapter 6 the time until two lineages find a common ancestor will be exponential.

Normal: sums of many small things

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the bell curve, fixed by its mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Its importance comes from the central limit theorem: add up many independent contributions, none of which dominates, and the total is approximately normal whatever the individual contributions look like. That is why a binomial with large \(n\) looks normal, why estimates computed from large samples are normal, and why traits shaped by thousands of variants are normal (Chapter 12).

About 95% of a normal distribution lies within 1.96 SD of its mean. A normal variable with mean 0 and SD 1 is a standard normal, often written \(Z\). The square of a standard normal follows the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom, \(\chi^2_1\), which has mean 1. Association statistics in Part IV are reported as either \(Z\) or \(\chi^2 = Z^2\).

Estimation and likelihood

We never see a population's true allele frequency \(p\). We see a sample: say \(k\) alternate alleles among \(n\) sampled chromosomes. The obvious estimate is \(\hat p = k/n\). How far from the truth is it likely to be? From the binomial variance,

\[ \mathrm{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}. \]

The standard error (SE) is the standard deviation of an estimate across imagined repeats of the study. It shrinks with the square root of the sample size: to halve your uncertainty you need four times the data. This \(\sqrt{n}\) law is behind the push to ever larger genetic studies.

A general recipe for producing estimates is maximum likelihood. The likelihood of a parameter value is the probability of the data you actually observed, computed as if that value were true. The maximum likelihood estimate is the value under which your data were most probable.

Hypothesis tests and p-values

To ask whether a variant affects a trait we set up a null hypothesis (no effect) and ask how surprising the data would be if it held. For an estimate \(\hat\beta\) that is approximately normal, the test statistic is

\[ Z = \frac{\hat\beta}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta)}, \]

which is standard normal under the null. The p-value is the probability, if the null were true, of a \(Z\) at least as extreme as the one observed. \(|Z| = 1.96\) gives \(P = 0.05\); \(|Z| = 5.45\) gives \(P = 5 \times 10^{-8}\), the threshold used in genome-wide studies.

A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. And if you run a million tests, you expect 50,000 of them to have \(P < 0.05\) by chance alone. This multiple testing problem is why genome-wide studies demand such small p-values (Chapter 14).

The power of a test is the probability that it detects an effect that is really there. Power rises with the size of the effect and with the sample size, and falls as the significance threshold becomes stricter.

Regression

Linear regression fits a straight line \(y = \alpha + \beta x + \text{error}\) through a cloud of points by minimising the squared vertical distances. The fitted slope has a simple form that you will meet in almost every chapter:

\[ \hat\beta = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(x,y)}{\mathrm{Var}(x)}. \]

A GWAS effect size is this slope with \(x\) a genotype (0, 1, 2) and \(y\) a trait. Heritability can be estimated as the slope of offspring on parents. Mendelian randomization is a ratio of two such slopes. The share of the variance of \(y\) that the line explains is \(R^2\), which for a single predictor is the squared correlation.

With several predictors, multiple regression estimates the effect of each while holding the others fixed. This is how studies adjust for covariates such as age and sex. If a variable influences both \(x\) and \(y\) and is left out, the slope absorbs its influence and no longer reflects the effect of \(x\); such a variable is a confounder. Much of Part IV is about finding and removing confounders.

For a yes/no outcome such as disease, logistic regression models the log of the odds of disease as a linear function of the predictors. Its slope is a log odds ratio: an odds ratio of 1.1 means each extra copy of the allele multiplies the odds of disease by 1.1.

Bayes' rule

Often we know how probable the data are under each hypothesis and want the reverse: how probable each hypothesis is given the data. Bayes' rule converts one into the other:

\[ P(\text{hypothesis} \mid \text{data}) = \frac{P(\text{data} \mid \text{hypothesis})\, P(\text{hypothesis})}{P(\text{data})}. \]

The prior \(P(\text{hypothesis})\) is what you believed beforehand; the posterior on the left is what you should believe after seeing the data. When comparing two hypotheses, the ratio of how well each predicts the data is the Bayes factor, and posterior odds = Bayes factor × prior odds. Fine-mapping and colocalization (Chapter 18) are applications of this one line.

Key points

  • Variances of independent quantities add. Genetics repeatedly splits a variance into components.
  • Sampling alleles is binomial: mean \(np\), variance \(np(1-p)\).
  • Sums of many small effects are normal (central limit theorem).
  • Standard errors shrink as \(1/\sqrt{n}\).
  • A regression slope is \(\mathrm{Cov}(x,y)/\mathrm{Var}(x)\); a test statistic is estimate ÷ standard error.
  • Bayes' rule: posterior odds = Bayes factor × prior odds.

Allele frequencies and the Hardy–Weinberg law

Population genetics starts by changing what we count. Instead of following individuals, we follow the frequency of each allele in the population, and ask what that frequency implies about genotypes.

From genotype counts to allele frequencies

Take a site with alleles A and a, and suppose we genotype 1,000 people and find 360 AA, 480 Aa and 160 aa. Each person carries two alleles, so there are 2,000 alleles in the sample. AA people contribute two copies of A and heterozygotes contribute one:

\[ p = \frac{2 \times 360 + 480}{2000} = 0.6, \qquad q = 1 - p = 0.4. \]

In general, if \(n_{AA}, n_{Aa}, n_{aa}\) are the genotype counts among \(n\) people, then \(p = (2n_{AA} + n_{Aa})/2n\). The allele frequency \(p\) is the basic variable of the field. Evolution, at its most stripped down, is change in \(p\) over time.

The Hardy–Weinberg law

Going the other way, from allele frequency to genotype frequencies, needs an assumption about how alleles are paired into individuals. The simplest is random mating: each child's two alleles are, in effect, two independent draws from the pool of alleles in the parental generation. Then the chance of drawing A twice is \(p \times p\), the chance of a twice is \(q \times q\), and a heterozygote can arise in two orders (A then a, or a then A):

\[ P(AA) = p^2, \qquad P(Aa) = 2pq, \qquad P(aa) = q^2. \]

These are the Hardy–Weinberg proportions, found independently by G. H. Hardy and Wilhelm Weinberg in 1908. Two things follow at once.

Equilibrium is reached in one generation. Whatever the genotype frequencies were among the parents, one round of random mating puts the children in Hardy–Weinberg proportions.

Allele frequencies do not change. The frequency of A among the children is \(p^2 + \tfrac12(2pq) = p(p+q) = p\). Inheritance by itself does not erode variation. Before Mendel, people assumed offspring were blends of their parents, which would halve the variance every generation and leave selection nothing to act on. Particulate inheritance removes that problem: variation persists unless some force removes it.

Worked example: carrier frequency

Cystic fibrosis is recessive and affects roughly 1 in 2,500 newborns of northern European ancestry. If those are the aa homozygotes, then \(q^2 = 1/2500\), so \(q = 0.02\). The carrier frequency is \(2pq = 2 \times 0.98 \times 0.02 \approx 0.039\), about 1 person in 25. A disease that looks rare is carried by a surprisingly large share of the population, because rare alleles hide in heterozygotes.

The assumptions, and why they are useful

The derivation assumed a great deal: random mating, a very large population, no mutation, no migration, and no differences in survival or fertility between genotypes. No real population satisfies all of these. The value of the Hardy–Weinberg law is as a null model. Each assumption, when broken, corresponds to one of the forces studied in the following chapters:

Departures from Hardy–Weinberg

Inbreeding

When parents are related, a child can inherit two copies of the very same ancestral allele. This raises homozygosity. If \(F\) is the inbreeding coefficient, the probability that a person's two alleles at a site are identical by descent from a recent common ancestor, the genotype frequencies become

\[ P(AA) = p^2 + Fpq, \qquad P(Aa) = 2pq(1-F), \qquad P(aa) = q^2 + Fpq. \]

Heterozygosity falls by the fraction \(F\); allele frequencies stay the same. For the child of first cousins \(F = 1/16\). The practical consequence is that rare recessive conditions become much more likely: with \(q = 0.01\), the chance of aa rises from 0.0001 to about 0.0007.

Hidden subdivision

Pool two groups that have different allele frequencies and the pooled sample has fewer heterozygotes than \(2pq\) predicts, even if each group is in Hardy–Weinberg proportions on its own. This is the Wahlund effect, and Chapter 10 turns the size of the deficit into a measure of population differentiation.

Technical error

The most common reason a site fails Hardy–Weinberg in real data is that the genotypes are wrong. A faulty assay that miscalls heterozygotes as homozygotes produces a clear deficit. For that reason every genome-wide study tests each variant for Hardy–Weinberg proportions and discards the worst offenders as a quality-control step (Chapter 14).

Testing for Hardy–Weinberg proportions

Compare observed genotype counts with those expected from the estimated allele frequency. With the counts from the start of the chapter (360, 480, 160; \(p = 0.6\)), the expected counts are \(1000 \times 0.36 = 360\), \(1000 \times 0.48 = 480\) and \(1000 \times 0.16 = 160\): a perfect fit. Suppose instead we had observed 400, 400, 200. The allele frequency is still 0.6, the expected counts are unchanged, and

\[ \chi^2 = \sum \frac{(\text{observed} - \text{expected})^2}{\text{expected}} = \frac{40^2}{360} + \frac{80^2}{480} + \frac{40^2}{160} = 27.8. \]

Under Hardy–Weinberg this statistic follows a chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom, and 27.8 corresponds to \(P \approx 10^{-7}\). There are too few heterozygotes. For rare alleles, where expected counts are small, an exact test is used in place of the chi-square approximation.

Sex chromosomes

Males carry a single X chromosome, so for X-linked sites the male "genotype" frequencies are simply the allele frequencies \(p\) and \(q\), while females follow \(p^2, 2pq, q^2\). An X-linked recessive condition with allele frequency \(q\) therefore affects a fraction \(q\) of males but only \(q^2\) of females, which is why conditions such as red–green colour blindness are so much more common in men.

Key points

  • Allele frequency: \(p = (2n_{AA} + n_{Aa})/2n\).
  • Under random mating, genotype frequencies are \(p^2, 2pq, q^2\), reached in one generation and then stable.
  • Rare alleles are found almost entirely in heterozygotes.
  • Inbreeding reduces heterozygosity by a fraction \(F\) without changing allele frequencies.
  • Hardy–Weinberg is the null model; each violated assumption is an evolutionary force, and in data a violation most often signals genotyping error.

Genetic drift

In a finite population, allele frequencies change from one generation to the next for no reason other than chance. This random wandering is genetic drift, and it never stops.

The Wright–Fisher model

Hardy–Weinberg assumed an infinitely large population. Real populations are finite, and the simplest model of a finite one is due to Sewall Wright and R. A. Fisher. Imagine \(N\) diploid individuals, so \(2N\) copies of each chromosome. Generations do not overlap. To build the next generation, draw \(2N\) alleles at random, with replacement, from the current one.

Sampling with replacement means some alleles are picked several times and others not at all, which captures the plain fact that some individuals leave many children and some leave none. If the current frequency of allele A is \(p\), the number of A copies in the next generation is a binomial draw:

\[ X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(2N,\, p), \qquad p' = \frac{X}{2N}. \]

From the binomial formulas of Chapter 2,

\[ E[p'] = p, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(p') = \frac{p(1-p)}{2N}. \]

The first equation says drift has no direction: on average the frequency stays where it is. The second says how big the random step is. It is largest at intermediate frequencies and scales as \(1/2N\), so drift is strong in small populations and weak in large ones.

Three consequences of drift

Variation is lost

Once an allele's frequency hits 0 it is lost, and once it hits 1 it is fixed. Without new mutation neither can be undone. So drift steadily removes variation. A convenient measure of variation is heterozygosity \(H\), the probability that two alleles drawn at random from the population differ; under Hardy–Weinberg \(H = 2pq\). Two alleles in the next generation are copies of the same parental allele with probability \(1/2N\), and in that case they cannot differ. Hence

\[ H_{t+1} = \left(1 - \frac{1}{2N}\right) H_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H_t = H_0\left(1 - \frac{1}{2N}\right)^t \approx H_0\, e^{-t/2N}. \]

Heterozygosity decays exponentially, losing a fraction \(1/2N\) each generation. It halves in about \(2N \ln 2 \approx 1.4N\) generations.

The probability of fixation equals the current frequency

Eventually every copy of the site in the population will descend from a single copy alive today. With no selection each of the \(2N\) present copies is equally likely to be that ancestor. If a fraction \(p\) of them are allele A, then A is the one that fixes with probability \(p\). In particular, a brand-new mutation, present as a single copy, has frequency \(1/2N\) and so fixes with probability

\[ P(\text{fixation of a new neutral mutation}) = \frac{1}{2N}. \]

Almost all new mutations are eventually lost, purely by bad luck: about half within two generations and nine in ten within twenty.

Populations diverge

Two populations that split from a common ancestor drift independently, so their allele frequencies wander apart. No selection or adaptation is needed for populations to become genetically different. Chapter 10 uses the amount of divergence to measure how long, and how completely, populations have been separated.

The timescale of drift

Everything about drift happens on a timescale proportional to \(N\). Heterozygosity decays with a time constant of \(2N\) generations. A new neutral mutation that is lucky enough to fix takes on average about \(4N\) generations to do so. This is the single most useful intuition in population genetics: if a process takes far fewer than \(N\) generations, drift has had little time to matter; if it takes many more, drift has reshuffled everything.

Effective population size

The Wright–Fisher population is an idealisation: constant size, random mating, non-overlapping generations, every individual equally likely to be a parent. Real populations break all of these. The effective population size \(N_e\) is the size of an ideal Wright–Fisher population that would drift at the same rate as the real one. It lets us keep the simple formulas and replace \(N\) with \(N_e\).

\(N_e\) is almost always smaller than the census count, sometimes by orders of magnitude. Three reasons matter most.

Fluctuating size. When population size varies over time, the long-run \(N_e\) is the harmonic mean of the sizes, which is dominated by the smallest values:

\[ \frac{1}{N_e} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{1}{N_t}. \]

A population that spends nine generations at 10,000 and one at 100 has \(N_e \approx 917\). A brief bottleneck leaves a long-lasting mark.

Unequal numbers of breeding males and females. Half of the gene pool must come from each sex, so the rarer sex is a bottleneck in every generation: \(N_e = 4N_m N_f/(N_m + N_f)\). Ten males and a thousand females give \(N_e \approx 40\).

Variance in family size. The more unequal reproductive success is, the faster drift proceeds and the lower \(N_e\).

The human number

The long-term effective size of the human population, inferred from how much genetic diversity we carry (Chapter 5), is on the order of 10,000 to 20,000, against a census size of eight billion. The gap reflects our history: for most of the last few hundred thousand years our ancestors were few, and the recent explosion in numbers has not yet had time to add much common variation. Populations descended from groups that went through additional bottlenecks, such as the migration out of Africa, have lower diversity still.

Founder effects

When a new population is started by a small group, the founders carry only a sample of the source population's variation, and their allele frequencies differ from the source by chance. This is a founder effect, a one-off burst of drift. It explains why certain recessive diseases are unusually frequent in populations with a small number of founders, such as Finns, Ashkenazi Jews, the Amish, and French Canadians. The same history makes these populations valuable for gene discovery: alleles that are vanishingly rare elsewhere can be common enough to study.

Key points

  • Wright–Fisher model: next generation's allele count is Binomial(\(2N, p\)).
  • Drift has no direction; its variance per generation is \(p(1-p)/2N\).
  • Heterozygosity decays by \(1/2N\) per generation.
  • A neutral allele at frequency \(p\) eventually fixes with probability \(p\); a new mutation with probability \(1/2N\).
  • Drift operates on a timescale of \(N_e\) generations; \(N_e\) is dominated by bottlenecks and is about \(10^4\) for humans.

Mutation and the neutral theory

Drift removes variation; mutation supplies it. The balance between the two predicts how much variation a population should carry and how fast species should diverge, with no reference to natural selection at all.

The fate of a new mutation

A new mutation appears as one copy among \(2N\). The previous chapter showed that if it has no effect on fitness it will eventually fix with probability \(1/2N\) and be lost otherwise. Loss is usually quick: the chance that a single copy leaves no descendants in the very next generation is about \(e^{-1} \approx 37\%\). The rare survivors drift upward slowly, taking around \(4N_e\) generations on average to reach fixation.

Mutation–drift balance

Each generation, mutation pushes heterozygosity up and drift pulls it down. At some level the two cancel. To find it we need a model of mutation, and the standard one for DNA is the infinite-sites model: the genome is so long, and the per-site mutation rate so low, that every new mutation lands on a site that has never mutated before.

Let \(\mu\) be the mutation rate per site per generation. Pick two chromosomes at random and ask whether they differ at a given site. They differ if a mutation has occurred on either lineage since their common ancestor. Chapter 6 will show that two lineages are separated from their common ancestor by \(2N_e\) generations on average, so the total time available for mutation is \(2 \times 2N_e\) generations, and the expected number of differences per site is

\[ \pi = 4N_e\mu \equiv \theta. \]

The compound parameter \(\theta = 4N_e\mu\), the population-scaled mutation rate, appears everywhere in population genetics. \(\pi\) is called nucleotide diversity: the average number of differences per site between two randomly chosen chromosomes.

Worked example: the human effective population size

Two human chromosomes differ at about 1 site in 1,000, so \(\pi \approx 0.001\). With \(\mu \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-8}\),

\[ N_e = \frac{\pi}{4\mu} = \frac{0.001}{4 \times 1.2 \times 10^{-8}} \approx 21{,}000. \]

Estimates between 10,000 and 20,000 are typical, depending on the population sampled and the mutation rate assumed. This is where the figure quoted in Chapter 4 comes from. Human diversity is low for a species so numerous; many fruit fly and bacterial species have diversity ten or more times higher.

For a locus where every mutation creates an allele never seen before (the infinite-alleles model), the same balance gives an equilibrium heterozygosity of

\[ H = \frac{\theta}{1+\theta}, \]

with \(\theta\) now defined per locus. When \(\theta\) is small this is approximately \(\theta\), agreeing with the per-site result.

The rate of substitution

Now look across species instead of within one. A substitution is a mutation that has gone all the way to fixation, so that it shows up as a fixed difference between species. How often does that happen?

Each generation, \(2N\mu\) new mutations arise at a site across the population. Each has probability \(1/2N\) of eventually fixing. The rate of substitution is the product:

\[ k = 2N\mu \times \frac{1}{2N} = \mu. \]

The population size cancels. For neutral mutations, the rate at which species accumulate differences equals the mutation rate, whatever the population size. A large population produces more mutations but each is less likely to fix, and the two effects offset exactly.

This result is the basis of the molecular clock. If neutral substitutions accumulate at rate \(\mu\) per generation along each lineage, two species that split \(T\) generations ago differ at a fraction \(d \approx 2\mu T\) of neutral sites. Humans and chimpanzees differ at about 1.2% of sites; with \(\mu = 1.2 \times 10^{-8}\) that gives \(T \approx 500{,}000\) generations, or more than ten million years at 25 years per generation. Careful estimates, allowing for variation already present in the ancestral population and for a faster yearly mutation rate in earlier apes, put the split at roughly 6–8 million years ago. The simple calculation gets the order of magnitude and shows where the uncertainty lies: in the mutation rate and generation time.

The neutral theory

In 1968 Motoo Kimura proposed that most of the variation we see within species, and most of the differences between species, at the molecular level are due to mutations with no meaningful effect on fitness, governed by mutation and drift alone. This is the neutral theory of molecular evolution.

The theory does not say that selection is unimportant. It says that most mutations fall into two groups: those that are harmful, which selection removes quickly so they contribute little to what we observe; and those that are effectively neutral, which make up nearly all of the polymorphism and divergence we see. Mutations that are beneficial are held to be rare enough to contribute little to the totals.

Its main predictions have held up well:

  • Sequences with less functional constraint evolve faster. Synonymous sites, introns and pseudogenes diverge more quickly than amino-acid-changing sites, because a larger share of mutations there are neutral.
  • Within-species diversity and between-species divergence should be correlated across regions, since both scale with the neutral mutation rate.
  • Substitutions should accumulate at a roughly steady rate.

Nearly neutral mutations

Tomoko Ohta refined the theory by pointing out that "neutral" depends on population size. Chapter 8 will show that selection only overpowers drift when \(|4N_e s|\) is well above 1, where \(s\) is the selection coefficient. A slightly harmful mutation with \(s = -10^{-5}\) is efficiently removed in a species with \(N_e = 10^6\) but drifts as if neutral in one with \(N_e = 10^4\). Species with small effective sizes, humans among them, are therefore expected to accumulate more slightly harmful variants. This idea returns in Chapter 8, which makes the contest between selection and drift exact.

The neutral model as a baseline

Whatever the truth about how much evolution is adaptive, the neutral model's lasting role is as the null hypothesis for molecular data. It makes sharp, quantitative predictions about how many variable sites a sample should contain, how their frequencies should be distributed, and how diversity should relate to divergence. The next two chapters derive those predictions. Departures from them are how we detect both demographic events and natural selection (Chapter 11).

Key points

  • A new neutral mutation fixes with probability \(1/2N\); most are lost almost immediately.
  • At mutation–drift balance, nucleotide diversity is \(\pi = \theta = 4N_e\mu\).
  • Human \(\pi \approx 0.001\) implies \(N_e\) on the order of \(10^4\).
  • The neutral substitution rate equals the mutation rate \(\mu\), independent of population size: the molecular clock.
  • Whether a mutation behaves as neutral depends on \(N_e s\), so the same mutation can be visible to selection in a large population and invisible in a small one.

The coalescent

The models so far run forward in time and track a whole population. The coalescent runs backward and tracks only the chromosomes we actually sampled. It is simpler, faster to simulate, and it is how modern population genetics thinks.

Looking backward

Sample a handful of chromosomes from a population today. Each had a parent chromosome in the previous generation, a grandparent before that, and so on. Follow these lines of descent back and every so often two of them land on the same ancestral chromosome. At that point the two lineages coalesce into one. Keep going and eventually all the lineages merge into a single ancestor, the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of the sample.

The tree traced out this way is the genealogy of the sample. It exists whether or not there is any genetic variation. Mutations are sprinkled onto it afterwards, and the pattern of variation we observe in data is the result. So the programme is: first understand the random tree, then add mutations.

Two lineages

Start with two chromosomes in a Wright–Fisher population of \(2N\) chromosomes. Going back one generation, the first picks its parent; the second picks the same parent with probability \(1/2N\). So in each generation the two coalesce with probability \(1/2N\), and the number of generations until they do is geometric with mean

\[ E[T_2] = 2N \text{ generations}. \]

Because \(1/2N\) is small, the geometric distribution is well approximated by an exponential. It is convenient to measure time in units of \(2N\) generations, in which case \(T_2\) is exponential with mean 1.

This immediately gives the diversity result promised in Chapter 5. Two chromosomes are separated by \(2T_2\) generations of evolution in total. Mutations occur at rate \(\mu\) per generation, so the expected number of differences per site is \(2\mu \cdot E[T_2] = 4N\mu = \theta\).

Many lineages

With \(k\) lineages, any of the \(\binom{k}{2} = k(k-1)/2\) pairs can coalesce, each with probability \(1/2N\) per generation. The chance of two events in one generation is negligible, so the total rate is \(\binom{k}{2}/2N\), and the waiting time while there are \(k\) lineages has mean

\[ E[T_k] = \frac{2N}{\binom{k}{2}} = \frac{4N}{k(k-1)} \text{ generations}. \]

When a coalescence happens, the pair involved is chosen at random. Then there are \(k-1\) lineages and the process repeats. This is Kingman's coalescent, and that short description is the whole model.

What the tree looks like

Because the rate grows roughly as \(k^2\), the recent part of the tree is crowded with coalescences and the ancient part is sparse. Summing the waiting times gives the expected age of the MRCA of \(n\) chromosomes:

\[ E[T_{MRCA}] = \sum_{k=2}^{n} \frac{4N}{k(k-1)} = 4N\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right). \]

Two chromosomes take \(2N\) generations on average to find their ancestor; a sample of a thousand takes barely under \(4N\). Adding more samples adds short twigs near the present and hardly changes the deep structure. A practical consequence is that a modest sample already captures most of a population's deep history, and common variants are discovered quickly, while ever-larger samples keep revealing new rare, recent ones.

Adding mutations

Under the infinite-sites model each mutation on the tree creates one variable site in the sample. Mutations fall on branches at rate \(\mu\) per generation, so the number of variable (or segregating) sites \(S\) is proportional to the total length of all branches in the tree. While there are \(k\) lineages the tree has \(k\) branches, each of expected length \(E[T_k]\), so

\[ E[\text{total length}] = \sum_{k=2}^{n} k \cdot \frac{4N}{k(k-1)} = 4N \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{1}{i}, \qquad E[S] = \theta \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{1}{i}. \]

The sum \(a_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} 1/i\) grows only logarithmically. Going from 10 chromosomes to 100 raises it from 2.83 to 5.18, less than a doubling of segregating sites for ten times the data.

A mutation on a branch is carried by all the sampled chromosomes descended from that branch. A mutation on a short twig near the bottom of the tree appears in one sample (a singleton); a mutation on a deep branch appears in many. The shape of the tree therefore fixes the distribution of allele frequencies in the sample, which is the subject of the next chapter.

Changing population size

The coalescence rate \(1/2N\) depends on the population size at that moment in the past. When the population was small, lineages found common ancestors quickly; when it was large, slowly. A varying population size stretches and squeezes the tree.

This is the principle behind demographic inference (Chapter 11): the distribution of coalescence times in genetic data is a record of past population sizes. Many closely spaced coalescences at a certain time in the past mean the population was small then.

Recombination: a tree for every position

A single tree describes a stretch of chromosome with no recombination. With recombination, a chromosome's left part and right part can come from different parental chromosomes, so going backward a lineage splits in two and the two parts follow separate ancestries. Neighbouring positions in the genome have similar but not identical trees, and positions far apart have essentially independent ones.

The complete structure, all the trees along a chromosome and the recombination events that link them, is the ancestral recombination graph (ARG). In human data each tree typically spans only a few thousand bases before a recombination event in the sample's history changes it. This is a statistical gift: the genome provides hundreds of thousands of nearly independent draws from the coalescent process, which is why so much history can be recovered from so few individuals, even from one (Chapter 11).

Why the backward view won

To simulate data forward in time you must track every individual in the population for thousands of generations. To simulate backward you track only the ancestors of your sample: draw exponential waiting times, merge random pairs, and drop mutations on the result. The backward view also makes it plain which features of data are informative. Everything we can learn from a sample is contained in its genealogy, and mutations are our only, noisy, view of that genealogy.

Key points

  • Two lineages coalesce at rate \(1/2N\) per generation; mean time \(2N\) generations.
  • With \(k\) lineages the rate is \(\binom{k}{2}/2N\), so recent coalescences are fast and the last one is slow.
  • \(E[T_{MRCA}] = 4N(1 - 1/n)\); expected number of segregating sites \(E[S] = \theta \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} 1/i\).
  • Past population size sets the coalescence rate, so tree shapes record demographic history.
  • With recombination, the genealogy changes along the genome; the full structure is the ancestral recombination graph.

The site frequency spectrum and summaries of diversity

A sample of genomes contains millions of variable sites. The most useful single summary of them is a histogram: how many variants are seen once, twice, three times, and so on.

The site frequency spectrum

Take \(n\) sampled chromosomes. At each variable site, count how many carry the derived (new) allele; the answer is a number from 1 to \(n-1\). The site frequency spectrum (SFS) is the tally \(\xi_1, \xi_2, \ldots, \xi_{n-1}\), where \(\xi_i\) is the number of sites at which the derived allele appears exactly \(i\) times. \(\xi_1\) counts singletons, \(\xi_2\) doubletons, and so on.

Knowing which allele is derived requires an outgroup such as the chimpanzee sequence. Without one we cannot tell a derived allele seen \(i\) times from one seen \(n-i\) times, so the two classes are merged to give the folded spectrum, indexed by the count of the minor allele.

What the neutral model predicts

Under the standard neutral coalescent with constant population size, the expected spectrum has a strikingly simple form:

\[ E[\xi_i] = \frac{\theta}{i}, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n-1. \]

There are twice as many singletons as doubletons, three times as many as tripletons. Rare variants dominate. The reason is visible in : a mutation is seen \(i\) times if it lands on a branch with \(i\) sampled descendants, and the tree has far more total branch length near its tips, where each branch has few descendants, than near its root.

Two checks. Summing over \(i\) gives \(E[S] = \theta \sum 1/i\), matching Chapter 6. And the result does not depend on \(n\): the expected number of singletons is \(\theta\) whether you sample 10 chromosomes or 10,000.

How demography and selection distort the spectrum

The \(1/i\) law is the fingerprint of a constant-size neutral population. Depart from those assumptions and the tree changes shape, and the spectrum with it.

So one fits a demographic model to sites presumed neutral and then looks for regions, or functional categories, that stand out against it.

The human spectrum

Humans show a vast excess of rare variants. In sequencing studies of tens of thousands of people, more than half of all variable sites are seen in a single person. This is the signature of explosive recent growth: the census population has grown by several orders of magnitude over the past few hundred generations, leaving genealogies with enormously long external branches. Those very rare variants are the raw material for the methods of Chapter 20.

Three estimators of θ

Under the neutral model, several different summaries of a sample all have expectation proportional to \(\theta\). Each can therefore be turned into an estimator.

Watterson's estimator uses the number of segregating sites:

\[ \hat\theta_W = \frac{S}{a_n}, \qquad a_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{i}. \]

Nucleotide diversity \(\hat\theta_\pi = \pi\) is the average number of differences between pairs of chromosomes. A site where the derived allele is in \(i\) of \(n\) chromosomes contributes to \(i(n-i)\) of the \(\binom{n}{2}\) pairs:

\[ \pi = \frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}} \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} i\,(n-i)\, \xi_i. \]

The two weight the spectrum differently. \(S\) counts every variable site once, however rare. \(\pi\) gives most weight to intermediate-frequency sites, because a singleton differs in only \(n-1\) pairs while a site at 50% differs in about \(n^2/4\). Under the neutral model with constant size the two estimates agree on average. When they disagree, the direction tells you how the spectrum is skewed.

Tajima's D

Fumio Tajima turned that comparison into a test statistic:

\[ D = \frac{\pi - S/a_n}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\pi - S/a_n)}}. \]

The denominator scales the difference so that \(D\) has roughly mean 0 and variance 1 under the null.

  • \(D < 0\): too many rare variants relative to common ones. Consistent with population growth, a recent sweep, or purifying selection.
  • \(D > 0\): too many intermediate-frequency variants. Consistent with contraction, structure, or balancing selection.
  • \(D \approx 0\): no evidence against the constant-size neutral model.
Worked example

Ten chromosomes are sequenced over a region and 12 segregating sites are found: 9 singletons and 3 sites where the derived allele appears 5 times. Here \(a_{10} = 2.83\), so \(\hat\theta_W = 12/2.83 = 4.24\). For \(\pi\): a singleton contributes \(1 \times 9 = 9\) pairwise differences and a 5-of-10 site contributes \(5 \times 5 = 25\), out of \(\binom{10}{2} = 45\) pairs, so \(\pi = (9 \times 9 + 3 \times 25)/45 = 3.47\). Since \(\pi < \hat\theta_W\), Tajima's D is negative: this region has more rare variants than the neutral constant-size model expects.

Related statistics weight the spectrum in other ways. Fay and Wu's \(H\) emphasises high-frequency derived alleles, which are a specific signature of a recent sweep dragging linked variants toward fixation. Fu and Li's statistics focus on singletons. All are different contrasts on the same histogram.

Beyond one population

With samples from two populations, one can tabulate the joint spectrum: the number of sites where the derived allele is seen \(i\) times in the first sample and \(j\) times in the second. If the populations split recently or exchange many migrants, sites cluster along the diagonal, with similar frequencies in both. Long separation spreads them out and adds variants private to one population. Fitting split times, migration rates and size changes to the joint spectrum is one of the standard routes to demographic inference (Chapter 11).

Key points

  • The SFS counts variable sites by the number of sampled copies of the derived allele.
  • Neutral, constant-size expectation: \(E[\xi_i] = \theta/i\). Rare variants are the norm.
  • Growth and sweeps add rare variants; contraction, structure and balancing selection add intermediate-frequency ones.
  • \(\hat\theta_W = S/a_n\) and \(\pi\) both estimate \(\theta\); Tajima's D measures their disagreement.
  • Demography and selection leave similar marks; demography is genome-wide, selection is local.

Natural selection

Selection is the one force that changes allele frequencies in a consistent direction. This chapter works out how fast it acts, when it can overcome drift, and how it keeps harmful alleles rare without eliminating them.

Fitness

The fitness of a genotype is the expected number of offspring its carriers contribute to the next generation. Only relative values matter, so we fix one genotype at 1 and measure the others against it. For a site with a favoured allele A:

GenotypeaaAaAA
Frequency (random mating)\(q^2\)\(2pq\)\(p^2\)

The selection coefficient \(s\) is the fitness advantage of the AA homozygote (negative \(s\) means A is harmful). The dominance coefficient \(h\) says where the heterozygote falls: \(h = 0\) if A is recessive, \(h = 1\) if dominant, \(h = \tfrac12\) if each copy adds the same amount (additive).

How allele frequency changes

After selection, each genotype's share of the population is its frequency times its fitness, divided by the population's mean fitness \(\bar w = p^2(1+s) + 2pq(1+hs) + q^2\). Counting A alleles among the survivors gives the new frequency:

\[ p' = \frac{p^2(1+s) + pq(1+hs)}{\bar w}. \]

For the additive case with small \(s\), this simplifies to

\[ \Delta p \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\, s\, p\,(1-p). \]

The change is proportional to \(p(1-p)\), the genetic variance at the site. Selection is slow when the allele is rare (few carriers to favour), fastest at intermediate frequency, and slow again near fixation (few non-carriers left to displace). The result is an S-shaped trajectory.

How long does a sweep take? In the additive case the allele's odds \(p/(1-p)\) grow by a factor of about \(1 + s/2\) per generation. Going from one copy to near fixation takes roughly \((4/s)\ln(2N)\) generations. With \(s = 0.01\) and \(N = 10^4\) that is about 4,000 generations, some 100,000 years for humans: fast by evolutionary standards, and far faster than the \(4N = 40{,}000\) generations a neutral allele would need.

Selection against drift

In a finite population a beneficial mutation is not guaranteed to spread. While it exists as one or a few copies, chance dominates and it will probably be lost. Motoo Kimura derived the probability that a new mutation ultimately fixes. Writing \(s_h\) for its advantage in heterozygotes (so \(s_h = hs\)), and assuming additive effects:

\[ P_{\text{fix}} = \frac{1 - e^{-2 s_h}}{1 - e^{-4N_e s_h}}. \]

Three regimes follow.

  • Clearly beneficial (\(4N_e s_h \gg 1\)): \(P_{\text{fix}} \approx 2s_h\). A mutation with a 1% advantage in heterozygotes still has a 98% chance of being lost. This is Haldane's classic result.
  • Effectively neutral (\(|4N_e s_h| \ll 1\)): \(P_{\text{fix}} \approx 1/2N\), the neutral value. Selection is too weak to register.
  • Clearly harmful (\(4N_e s_h \ll -1\)): the probability falls off exponentially. Fixation is practically impossible.
The rule of thumb

Selection beats drift when \(|4N_e s| \gg 1\). In humans, with \(N_e \approx 10^4\), selection coefficients much smaller than about \(10^{-4}\) are largely invisible to selection. In fruit flies, with \(N_e \approx 10^6\), the cut-off is a hundred times lower. Large populations adapt more precisely and tolerate fewer slightly harmful variants.

Mutation–selection balance

Most new mutations that do anything are harmful. Selection removes them, but mutation keeps making more, and the allele settles at a low equilibrium frequency where the two rates match. Let \(\mu\) be the mutation rate to the harmful allele and let \(s\) now be the fitness cost to homozygotes, with heterozygotes losing \(hs\).

If the allele has any effect in heterozygotes, almost all selection acts there because that is where rare alleles are. Each generation selection removes a fraction \(hs\) of copies and mutation adds \(\mu\), giving

\[ \hat q \approx \frac{\mu}{hs} \qquad \text{(partly dominant)}. \]

If the allele is fully recessive, only the rare \(q^2\) homozygotes are exposed to selection, and it rises much higher before the balance is struck:

\[ \hat q \approx \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{s}} \qquad \text{(recessive)}. \]

For a gene with a combined loss-of-function mutation rate of \(10^{-6}\) per generation and a lethal recessive effect (\(s = 1\)), \(\hat q \approx 0.001\). If instead heterozygotes lose 10% of their fitness (\(hs = 0.1\)), \(\hat q \approx 10^{-5}\). These formulas explain two observations that come back in Chapter 20: variants with large harmful effects are rare, and the frequency of loss-of-function variants in a gene measures how strongly selection is acting on that gene.

Selection that maintains variation

Selection usually removes variation, but some forms preserve it. Collectively they are balancing selection.

The textbook case is heterozygote advantage. If the fitnesses of AA, Aa and aa are \(1 - s_1\), \(1\) and \(1 - s_2\), neither allele can take over, and the population settles at

\[ \hat p = \frac{s_2}{s_1 + s_2}. \]

The sickle-cell allele of the β-globin gene is the standard example. Heterozygotes are protected against severe malaria; homozygotes have sickle-cell disease. Where malaria has been most intense, the allele is held at frequencies of about 10–18% even though it is devastating in two copies.

Other forms include negative frequency-dependent selection, where an allele is favoured when rare, as with immune-system genes such as HLA under pressure from pathogens that adapt to common host types.

Selection on traits

Selection acts on organisms, and so on traits, rather than directly on variants. Three patterns are distinguished by what they do to a trait's distribution: directional selection favours one extreme and shifts the mean; stabilizing selection favours intermediate values and trims both tails; disruptive selection favours both extremes.

Most human quantitative traits appear to be under stabilizing selection. That has a subtle consequence for the variants underneath: under stabilizing selection, any variant that shifts the trait is slightly disfavoured in proportion to the square of its effect, whichever direction it pushes. Variants with large effects are therefore kept rare, which is exactly the pattern genetic studies find (Chapter 20).

Terms you will meet

  • Positive selection: selection favouring a new beneficial allele. A beneficial allele rising rapidly to fixation is a selective sweep.
  • Purifying (negative) selection: removal of harmful alleles. By far the most common form.
  • Background selection: loss of neutral variation because it is linked to harmful alleles being removed (Chapter 11).
  • Genetic load: the reduction in a population's mean fitness due to harmful alleles. For partly dominant harmful mutations at equilibrium it is about \(2\mu\) per locus, surprisingly independent of how harmful each one is.

Key points

  • Additive selection changes allele frequency by about \(\tfrac12 s\,p(1-p)\) per generation: slow, fast, slow.
  • A new beneficial mutation fixes with probability of only about \(2s_h\).
  • Selection dominates drift when \(|4N_e s| \gg 1\); otherwise the allele behaves as neutral.
  • Harmful alleles sit at mutation–selection balance: \(\mu/hs\) if partly dominant, \(\sqrt{\mu/s}\) if recessive.
  • Heterozygote advantage and frequency dependence can hold variation in place indefinitely.

Linkage disequilibrium and recombination

So far each site has been treated alone. But sites sit next to each other on chromosomes, and alleles at neighbouring sites are inherited together. The resulting correlation, linkage disequilibrium, is the fact on which all of statistical genetics rests.

Defining linkage disequilibrium

Take two sites, one with alleles A/a and one with alleles B/b, and let \(p_A\) and \(p_B\) be the frequencies of A and B. There are four possible haplotypes: AB, Ab, aB, ab. If the alleles at the two sites were independent, the frequency of the AB haplotype would be \(p_A p_B\). Linkage disequilibrium (LD) is any departure from that:

\[ D = p_{AB} - p_A\, p_B. \]

\(D\) is exactly the covariance between the two sites when each allele is coded 0/1. If \(D > 0\), A and B are found together more often than chance predicts. The name is unfortunate. LD does not require the sites to be linked, and it is not a disequilibrium in any important sense. "Allelic association" would be clearer, but the historical term has stuck.

The possible range of \(D\) depends on the allele frequencies, which makes raw values hard to compare. Two rescalings are standard.

\(r^2\), the squared correlation between the two sites:

\[ r^2 = \frac{D^2}{p_A(1-p_A)\,p_B(1-p_B)}. \]

It runs from 0 to 1 and equals 1 only when the two sites carry the same information: knowing the allele at one tells you the allele at the other. This is the measure that matters for association studies.

\(D'\), which is \(D\) divided by the largest value it could take given the allele frequencies. \(|D'| = 1\) whenever at least one of the four haplotypes is absent, which is the signature of no recombination between the sites since the younger mutation arose. \(D'\) can be 1 while \(r^2\) is small, if the two alleles have very different frequencies.

Worked example

Among 1,000 chromosomes we count AB 500, Ab 100, aB 50, ab 350. Then \(p_A = 0.6\), \(p_B = 0.55\), \(p_{AB} = 0.5\), so \(D = 0.5 - 0.33 = 0.17\) and

\[ r^2 = \frac{0.17^2}{0.6 \times 0.4 \times 0.55 \times 0.45} = 0.49. \]

The two sites are substantially but not perfectly correlated: about half the variance at one is predictable from the other.

Where LD comes from

Mutation. A new mutation arises on one particular chromosome. At that moment it is perfectly associated with every allele that chromosome happens to carry. All LD between a variant and its neighbours starts this way, as an accident of the haplotype background on which the variant was born.

Drift. In a finite population, haplotype frequencies fluctuate by chance, continually generating random associations even between sites that began in equilibrium.

Population structure and admixture. If two populations differ in allele frequencies at both sites, mixing them creates LD between the sites, even ones on different chromosomes (Chapter 10).

Selection. A selected allele rising in frequency drags its linked neighbours with it (Chapter 11).

How recombination destroys LD

Recombination breaks up haplotypes. Each generation, a fraction \(c\) of chromosomes are recombinants between the two sites, and in a recombinant the alleles at the two sites come from different parental chromosomes, so they are independent. The remaining \(1-c\) carry forward the existing association. Therefore

\[ D_{t} = (1-c)^{t}\, D_0. \]

For sites on different chromosomes \(c = \tfrac12\) and LD halves every generation. For sites 10,000 bases apart, \(c \approx 10^{-4}\), and it takes about 7,000 generations (roughly 175,000 years in humans) to halve. Tight linkage preserves ancient associations.

The balance between drift and recombination

Drift creates LD at a rate that scales with \(1/N_e\); recombination removes it at rate \(c\). At equilibrium the expected squared correlation is approximately

\[ E[r^2] \approx \frac{1}{1 + 4N_e c}. \]

The controlling quantity is \(\rho = 4N_e c\), the population-scaled recombination rate, the exact counterpart of \(\theta = 4N_e\mu\). With \(N_e = 10^4\) and 1 cM per Mb, \(4N_e c\) reaches 1 at a separation of only 2,500 bases, so on average strong LD in humans extends for thousands to tens of thousands of bases, and little remains beyond a few hundred thousand.

The dependence on \(N_e\) explains a robust pattern in human data. Populations of African ancestry, which have the largest long-term effective size, have the shortest-range LD. Populations descended from the out-of-Africa bottleneck have longer-range LD, and recently founded or isolated populations longer still.

Haplotype blocks

Because human recombination is concentrated in hotspots (Chapter 1), LD does not decay smoothly with distance. The genome is divided into haplotype blocks: stretches typically a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of bases long, occasionally over a hundred thousand, within which recombination has been rare, where only a handful of distinct haplotypes account for most chromosomes, separated by hotspots across which LD drops sharply.

Why LD matters for finding disease genes

It makes genome-wide association studies possible. Within a block, many SNPs are near-perfect proxies for one another, so genotyping one tag SNP captures the rest. In populations of non-African ancestry, a few hundred thousand well-chosen SNPs carry most of the information about the roughly ten million common variants in the genome; African-ancestry samples, with their shorter blocks, need more. This is why arrays work, and it is what imputation exploits (Chapter 16).

It sets the price of indirect testing. Suppose the causal variant is not genotyped, but a tag SNP with correlation \(r^2\) to it is. The association statistic at the tag is, on average, \(r^2\) times what it would be at the causal variant. Equivalently, to keep the same power you need a sample \(1/r^2\) times larger. A tag with \(r^2 = 0.8\) costs 25% more samples; one with \(r^2 = 0.2\) costs five times as many.

It limits resolution. The same correlation that lets a tag stand in for a causal variant means that when a signal is found, every variant in strong LD with it shows nearly the same evidence. A GWAS hit identifies a region, not a variant. Untangling which variant in the block is responsible is the job of fine-mapping (Chapter 18). Populations with shorter LD give finer resolution, one of several scientific reasons to study genetically diverse samples.

It carries information about history. The rate at which LD decays with distance reflects \(N_e\); long shared haplotypes reveal recent common ancestry or recent selection (Chapter 11).

Key points

  • \(D = p_{AB} - p_A p_B\); \(r^2\) is the squared correlation between two sites.
  • LD is created by mutation, drift, admixture and selection, and destroyed by recombination at rate \(c\) per generation.
  • At equilibrium \(E[r^2] \approx 1/(1 + 4N_e c)\); smaller populations have more extensive LD.
  • Human LD is organised into blocks separated by recombination hotspots.
  • LD lets a tag SNP detect an untyped causal variant, at a sample-size cost of \(1/r^2\), and prevents GWAS from pinpointing the causal variant directly.

Population structure and admixture

No species is a single randomly mating pool. People tend to have children with people who live nearby, so allele frequencies vary from place to place. That variation is small, but it is enough to reconstruct history and, if ignored, enough to wreck an association study.

Subdivision reduces heterozygosity

Imagine two isolated groups of equal size. In the first an allele has frequency 0.2; in the second, 0.8. Each mates randomly within itself, so heterozygotes make up \(2 \times 0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.32\) of each group. Now pool them and treat them as one population. The overall allele frequency is 0.5, and Hardy–Weinberg would predict 0.5 heterozygotes. We observe 0.32. The shortfall is the Wahlund effect: whenever subpopulations differ in allele frequency, the pooled population has fewer heterozygotes than its allele frequency implies.

The deficit equals twice the variance of the allele frequency across subpopulations: \(H_T - H_S = 2\,\mathrm{Var}(p)\), where \(H_S\) is the average heterozygosity within subpopulations and \(H_T = 2\bar p(1 - \bar p)\) is what the total would have if it mated at random.

FST

Sewall Wright turned the size of that deficit into the standard measure of population differentiation:

\[ F_{ST} = \frac{H_T - H_S}{H_T} = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(p)}{\bar p\,(1 - \bar p)}. \]

\(F_{ST}\) is the proportion of the total genetic variance that is due to differences in allele frequency between groups. It is 0 when all groups have identical frequencies and 1 when they are fixed for different alleles. In the example above, \(F_{ST} = (0.5 - 0.32)/0.5 = 0.36\).

It also has a coalescent meaning: \(F_{ST}\) measures how much more quickly two chromosomes from the same group find a common ancestor than two chromosomes drawn from the population as a whole.

The human numbers

Between populations from different continents, \(F_{ST}\) averaged over common variants is roughly 0.10 to 0.15. Between populations within Europe it is typically well under 0.01. So about 85–90% of human genetic variance is found among individuals within any one population and only 10–15% between continental groups. Humans are a young species that expanded recently and has exchanged migrants ever since. Differences between groups are real and statistically detectable with enough markers, but they are differences in frequency, with very few variants fixed in one group and absent in another.

What sets FST: drift against migration

Drift pushes subpopulations apart; migration pulls them together. In Wright's island model, with many subpopulations of size \(N\) each receiving a fraction \(m\) of migrants per generation, the balance is

\[ F_{ST} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 4Nm}. \]

Only the product \(Nm\), the number of migrants per generation, matters. Remarkably little migration is needed to prevent strong divergence.

If instead two populations split and never exchange migrants, they diverge steadily with time: \(F_{ST} \approx 1 - e^{-t/2N_e}\) after \(t\) generations. Read in reverse, an observed \(F_{ST}\) gives a rough estimate of \(t/2N_e\), the split time measured in units of population size.

Real populations are neither islands nor clean splits. Migration falls off with distance, so genetic similarity declines smoothly with geography. This pattern, isolation by distance, describes most human variation better than any division into discrete groups.

Seeing structure: principal components analysis

With genome-wide data we can find structure without being told who belongs to which group. Standardise each SNP so that it has mean 0 and variance 1 across individuals, and compute, for each pair of individuals, their average genotype similarity across all SNPs. This gives an \(n \times n\) genetic relationship matrix. Its leading eigenvectors are the principal components (PCs): the axes along which individuals differ most.

If the sample contains groups with different allele frequencies, individuals from the same group are slightly more similar to each other across thousands of SNPs, and the top PCs separate the groups. Each SNP contributes a tiny amount of information, but half a million of them together resolve even \(F_{ST}\) values below 0.001.

A famous application showed that the first two PCs of European genotype data reproduce the geographic map of Europe to within a few hundred kilometres. PCs are a description of the data and need care in interpretation: they are sensitive to which populations were sampled and in what numbers, and continuous gradients can appear as curved arcs that have no historical meaning. Their most important practical use is as covariates that absorb ancestry differences in association studies (Chapter 15).

Model-based ancestry estimation

An alternative is to posit \(K\) ancestral populations, each with its own allele frequencies, and model each person's genome as a mixture: a fraction \(q_{i1}\) from population 1, \(q_{i2}\) from population 2, and so on. Both the ancestry proportions and the allele frequencies are estimated from the data. This is the model behind the programs STRUCTURE and ADMIXTURE, whose output is the familiar bar plot with one column per person.

The clusters are a statistical summary. The value of \(K\) is chosen by the analyst, and the "ancestral populations" need not correspond to any group that ever existed.

Admixture

When previously separated populations meet and have children, the result is an admixed population. Most human populations are admixed on some timescale. African Americans, for example, have on average roughly 75–80% African and 20–25% European ancestry, with wide variation between individuals; Latino populations combine Indigenous American, European and African ancestry in proportions that vary by region.

In the first generation after admixture, each chromosome comes entirely from one source. Recombination then breaks chromosomes into a mosaic of segments from each source, and the segments get shorter with each generation: after \(t\) generations their average length is about \(100/t\) cM. Two things follow.

  • Admixture LD. Sites far apart on a chromosome are correlated if the source populations differed in frequency at both, since they tend to sit on the same ancestry segment. This long-range LD decays with time since admixture, so its extent dates the admixture event.
  • Local ancestry. The ancestry of each segment of a person's genome can be inferred. If a disease is more common in one source population for genetic reasons, affected people will carry extra ancestry from that population around the responsible loci. Admixture mapping searches for such regions.

Testing for admixture: f- and D-statistics

A simple and robust family of tests asks whether allele frequencies fit a tree. Take four groups with the hypothesised relationship ((P1, P2), P3) and a distant outgroup. Count sites where P3 shares a derived allele with P1 but not P2 ("BABA" sites) and sites where it shares with P2 but not P1 ("ABBA" sites). If the tree is right and there has been no gene flow, both patterns arise only from the random sorting of ancestral lineages and are equally frequent. The D-statistic

\[ D = \frac{n_{ABBA} - n_{BABA}}{n_{ABBA} + n_{BABA}} \]

should then be zero. A significant excess of one pattern means P3 exchanged genes with one of the pair.

This test delivered one of the best-known results in human genetics. With P1 an African genome, P2 a non-African genome and P3 the Neanderthal genome, there is a consistent excess of sharing between Neanderthals and non-Africans. People of non-African ancestry carry about 2% Neanderthal DNA, the result of interbreeding roughly 45,000 to 50,000 years ago. Related statistics (\(f_3\), \(f_4\)) underpin most of what ancient DNA has revealed about human prehistory.

Key points

  • Subdivision lowers heterozygosity; \(F_{ST} = \mathrm{Var}(p)/\bar p(1-\bar p)\) measures the share of variance between groups.
  • Human continental \(F_{ST}\) is about 0.1: most variation is within populations.
  • At migration–drift balance \(F_{ST} \approx 1/(1+4Nm)\); one migrant per generation prevents strong divergence.
  • PCA and model-based clustering recover ancestry from genome-wide genotypes without labels.
  • Admixture creates long-range LD and mosaic chromosomes; allele-sharing tests such as the D-statistic detect past gene flow.

Reading history and selection from genomes

The previous chapters ran from process to pattern: given a history, what data should we see? This chapter runs the other way. Given genomes sampled today, what can we say about past population sizes, splits, and episodes of natural selection?

Inferring demographic history

From the site frequency spectrum

Chapter 7 showed that growth, contraction and subdivision each bend the frequency spectrum in a characteristic way. Demographic inference from the SFS turns this around. Write down a model with parameters (sizes, times of size change, split times, migration rates), compute the spectrum it predicts, and adjust the parameters until the prediction matches the observed spectrum as closely as possible. With samples from several populations the joint spectrum is used. The approach scales to large samples and is the main source of information about very recent history, because recent events show up in the rarest variants.

Its weakness is that the spectrum is a blunt summary. Quite different histories can produce nearly the same spectrum, so results depend on the family of models considered.

From a single genome: PSMC

A diploid genome is two haplotypes. At each position they share a common ancestor at some time in the past, and Chapter 6 showed that this time changes along the genome as recombination switches the genealogy. Where the two haplotypes share a recent ancestor there has been little time for mutation, so heterozygous sites are sparse; where the ancestor is ancient, heterozygous sites are dense. The local density of heterozygous sites is therefore a noisy readout of the local coalescence time.

The pairwise sequentially Markovian coalescent (PSMC) uses a hidden Markov model to infer the distribution of coalescence times across the genome from this signal. The distribution converts directly into population size over time: under the coalescent, the rate of coalescence at time \(t\) is \(1/2N(t)\), so periods in which many segments coalesce were periods of small population size.

PSMC has blind spots at both ends. Few segments of a single genome coalesce in the last ten thousand years or so, leaving recent history unresolved, and beyond a few million years nearly everything has already coalesced. Extensions that use several genomes (MSMC, SMC++) push the resolution toward the present.

From shared segments: recent history

Two people who share a common ancestor a few dozen generations ago may both have inherited the same long, unbroken stretch of that ancestor's chromosome. Such a segment is identical by descent (IBD). Each generation adds two more meioses, one on each line of descent, in which a crossover can cut the segment, so a segment inherited from an ancestor \(g\) generations back has an expected length of about \(100/(2g)\) cM. Long segments therefore indicate recent common ancestry. The number and length distribution of IBD segments in a sample reveals effective population size over the last hundred generations, the period that PSMC cannot see. The same idea, applied to pairs, is how relatives are detected in biobanks and consumer genetic tests.

From inferred genealogies

The most complete approach is to estimate the ancestral recombination graph itself: the sequence of trees along the genome for all sampled chromosomes. Methods such as Relate and tsinfer do this for thousands of genomes. With the trees in hand, population sizes, split times, the ages of individual variants and the history of selection at a locus can all be read off the same object.

Detecting natural selection

Selection at one site affects neutral variation at linked sites, and those side effects are what we detect.

Selective sweeps

When a new beneficial mutation rises quickly to fixation, the stretch of chromosome on which it arose rises with it. Linked neutral variants on that original haplotype are carried to high frequency, while the variation that was present on other haplotypes is lost. This is genetic hitchhiking, and the resulting local loss of diversity is a selective sweep.

The sweep is not felt equally everywhere. Far from the selected site, recombination has time to move the beneficial allele onto other backgrounds before the sweep completes, so diversity there escapes. A sweep lasting about \(\tau \approx (2/s)\ln(2N)\) generations, where \(s\) is the advantage in heterozygotes, reduces diversity at a site a recombination fraction \(c\) away to roughly

\[ \frac{\pi}{\pi_0} \approx 1 - e^{-c\tau}. \]

The haplotype signal deserves a closer look, because it is the most sensitive to recent selection. An allele that has reached a given frequency by drift alone is old, and recombination has had time to whittle down the haplotype on which it arose. An allele that reached the same frequency through selection is young, and its carriers still share a long stretch of the original chromosome. Extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) measures this: the probability that two chromosomes carrying the allele are identical all the way out to a given distance.

Because demography can mimic each of these, the usual practice is to scan the whole genome and treat the most extreme regions as candidates, with the genome-wide distribution standing in for the null. Well-supported human examples include the lactase persistence variant upstream of LCT, which lets adults digest milk and swept in dairying populations within the last several thousand years; variants in SLC24A5 affecting skin pigmentation; and a haplotype of EPAS1 that helps Tibetans tolerate low oxygen, which was acquired from Denisovans, an archaic human group, and then rose in frequency on the plateau.

Classic "hard" sweeps of this kind turn out to be uncommon in recent human history. Much adaptation probably proceeds by soft sweeps, in which selection acts on an allele already present on several backgrounds, and by polygenic adaptation, small coordinated shifts at many loci. Both leave much weaker footprints.

Background selection

Harmful mutations arise constantly and are removed by selection. Each removal takes with it the neutral variants on the same chromosome segment. In regions packed with functional sequence and with little recombination, this steady culling noticeably reduces neutral diversity. Background selection is probably the main reason diversity across the human genome correlates with recombination rate and with distance from genes. It also means there is very little truly neutral, unlinked sequence against which to calibrate anything.

Comparing species

On longer timescales, selection is detected by comparing kinds of change within genes. Synonymous changes are close to neutral; amino-acid-changing (nonsynonymous) ones are exposed to selection. The ratio of their substitution rates, \(d_N/d_S\), is below 1 for the great majority of genes, which shows that most amino-acid changes are harmful and removed. A ratio above 1 signals repeated adaptive change, as seen in some immune and reproductive genes.

The McDonald–Kreitman test sharpens this by comparing the nonsynonymous-to-synonymous ratio among fixed differences between species with the same ratio among polymorphisms within a species. Under neutrality the two ratios should match. An excess of nonsynonymous fixed differences indicates adaptive substitutions.

A caution about polygenic adaptation

Testing whether selection has shifted a polygenic trait, such as height, seems simple: take the alleles that GWAS associates with the trait and ask whether trait-increasing alleles are systematically more common in one population. Early studies reported strong signals of selection for height differences across Europe. When the analyses were repeated with effect sizes estimated in UK Biobank, a larger and less structured sample, the signals shrank dramatically. The original effect estimates had been slightly biased by residual population structure, and the bias pointed in the same direction as the population difference being tested. The episode is a standard warning: tiny confounding in GWAS effect sizes, summed over thousands of variants, can produce a convincing false signal. Chapters 15 and 23 return to it.

Key points

  • Demography is inferred from the frequency spectrum, from coalescence times within a genome (PSMC), from IBD segments (recent history), or from inferred genealogies.
  • The coalescence rate at a time in the past is \(1/2N\) then, so the distribution of coalescence times records population size.
  • A selective sweep removes linked diversity over a region of width roughly proportional to \(s/c\), skews the frequency spectrum, and leaves long haplotypes.
  • Background selection reduces diversity in gene-dense, low-recombination regions.
  • \(d_N/d_S\) and the McDonald–Kreitman test detect selection on protein-coding sequence over long timescales.

From genes to continuous traits

Mendel's peas were round or wrinkled. Height, blood pressure and cholesterol come in every value in between. This chapter shows how discrete genes produce continuous traits and sets up the vocabulary of effects and variances used in the rest of the book.

Many genes make a bell curve

In the early 1900s biologists argued over whether Mendel's discrete factors could explain the smooth variation and family resemblance seen in traits like height. R. A. Fisher settled it in 1918: if a trait is influenced by many genes, each of small effect, Mendelian inheritance produces continuous variation and exactly the correlations between relatives that had been observed.

The argument is the central limit theorem of Chapter 2. Suppose a trait is determined by \(L\) sites, and each "+" allele adds one unit. With one site there are three trait values. With four sites there are nine. With forty there are eighty-one, tightly packed, and their distribution is indistinguishable from a normal curve.

Traits shaped by many variants are polygenic. Traits with a simple, single-gene cause are Mendelian (or monogenic). Human height is influenced by many thousands of variants. So, it turns out, is nearly every common disease.

The basic model

Write a person's phenotype \(P\) (the measured trait) as the sum of a genetic part and everything else:

\[ P = G + E. \]

\(G\) is the genotypic value, the average phenotype that people with this genotype would have. \(E\) is the environmental deviation, which lumps together diet, upbringing, chance events in development, measurement error and anything else non-genetic. If \(G\) and \(E\) are independent, variances add:

\[ V_P = V_G + V_E. \]

Quantitative genetics is mostly about variances. The reason is practical: when thousands of unknown variants contribute, we cannot hope to know each one's effect, but we can measure how much variation they jointly create and how that variation is shared between relatives.

One locus in detail

To see what makes up \(V_G\), take a single site with alleles A (frequency \(p\)) and a (frequency \(q\)). Assign genotypic values

Here \(a\) is half the difference between the two homozygotes, and \(d\) measures dominance: how far the heterozygote departs from the midpoint. If \(d = 0\) the locus is purely additive and each copy of A adds exactly \(a\).

The average effect of an allele

Parents pass on alleles, not genotypes. So the useful question is: on average, how much does the trait change when one a allele is replaced by A? The answer is the slope of the regression of genotypic value on the number of A alleles, the average effect of allele substitution:

\[ \alpha = a + d\,(q - p). \]

When there is no dominance, \(\alpha = a\). With dominance, \(\alpha\) depends on allele frequency, because the effect of swapping in an A allele depends on which allele it is likely to be paired with.

The fitted value for each genotype is its breeding value (or additive genetic value) \(A\): the sum of the average effects of the alleles it carries. What is left over is the dominance deviation \(D\). So \(G = A + D\) at one locus, and by construction \(A\) and \(D\) are uncorrelated.

The regression slope should look familiar. It is what a GWAS estimates: the effect size \(\beta\) reported for a SNP is the average effect of allele substitution, estimated from data.

Additive and dominance variance

The variance of the breeding values is the additive genetic variance, and the variance of the dominance deviations is the dominance variance:

\[ V_A = 2pq\,\alpha^2, \qquad V_D = (2pq\,d)^2. \]
Worked example

With the values in : \(\alpha = 1 + 0.8(0.6 - 0.4) = 1.16\). Then \(V_A = 2(0.4)(0.6)(1.16)^2 = 0.646\) and \(V_D = (2 \times 0.4 \times 0.6 \times 0.8)^2 = 0.147\). Even with strong dominance (the heterozygote is 90% of the way to the AA homozygote), 81% of the genetic variance at this locus is additive.

That example illustrates a general and somewhat counter-intuitive fact. Additive variance is not the same as additive gene action. A locus can be strongly dominant and still generate mostly additive variance, because the straight line captures most of what the genotypes do.

How much it captures depends on allele frequency. Take the extreme case of a fully recessive allele: only people with two copies are affected.

Summed over many loci with a realistic spread of allele frequencies, the additive part dominates. This is a statement about variance in a population. It does not say that genes combine additively inside cells.

Many loci

Across loci, breeding values add up. If loci are in linkage equilibrium, so do their variances:

\[ V_A = \sum_{j} 2 p_j q_j\, \alpha_j^2. \]

Each variant's contribution depends on its effect and its frequency. A common variant with a small effect can contribute as much variance as a rare variant with a large one. This formula is the bridge between GWAS results and heritability.

Interactions between loci, where the effect of one genotype depends on the genotype at another, are called epistasis and create an additional component, \(V_I\). The full decomposition is

\[ V_P = \underbrace{V_A + V_D + V_I}_{V_G} + V_E. \]

As with dominance, epistasis at the level of biology is common, but most of its effect on variance is absorbed into \(V_A\). In human data, estimates of \(V_D\) and \(V_I\) for most complex traits are small compared with \(V_A\). This is why additive models work so well in practice, and why the rest of this book mostly uses them. It does not mean genes act independently inside cells.

The infinitesimal model

Pushing the many-genes idea to its limit gives Fisher's infinitesimal model: infinitely many loci, each with an infinitesimally small effect. Under it, breeding values in a population are normally distributed, and a child's breeding value is normally distributed around the average of the parents' with a fixed variance of \(\tfrac12 V_A\), reflecting the randomness of which alleles each parent passes on. That random half is why siblings differ, and it is put to work as a natural experiment in Chapter 23. The model is obviously not literally true, but for traits like height it is close enough that methods built on it predict very well.

Genes and environment together

Two complications to \(P = G + E\) deserve names.

Gene–environment interaction (G×E): the effect of a genotype depends on the environment. A variant that raises body weight only when food is plentiful is an example. The standard illustration is phenylketonuria, where a genotype causes severe intellectual disability on a normal diet and almost none on a diet low in phenylalanine.

Gene–environment correlation: genotypes are not randomly distributed across environments. Parents pass on both genes and a home; people choose environments that suit their dispositions; ancestry correlates with geography and so with diet and climate. When \(G\) and \(E\) are correlated, their variances no longer simply add, and genetic effects estimated from population data can include environmental effects. Much of Chapters 15 and 23 concerns this problem.

Key points

  • Many loci of small effect produce a normally distributed trait.
  • \(P = G + E\), and with independence \(V_P = V_G + V_E\).
  • The average effect \(\alpha = a + d(q-p)\) is the regression of trait on allele count; it is what GWAS estimates.
  • \(V_A = \sum 2p_jq_j\alpha_j^2\): variance contributed depends on both effect size and allele frequency.
  • Non-additive gene action still yields mostly additive variance, which is why additive models work.

Heritability and resemblance between relatives

Heritability is one of the most used and most misunderstood numbers in science. It has a precise definition, a classical way of being measured from families, and a list of things it does not mean.

Definitions

Heritability is the proportion of a trait's variance in a population that is due to genetic differences between individuals. There are two versions.

\[ H^2 = \frac{V_G}{V_P} \quad\text{(broad sense)}, \qquad\qquad h^2 = \frac{V_A}{V_P} \quad\text{(narrow sense)}. \]

Broad-sense heritability counts all genetic variance. Narrow-sense heritability counts only the additive part. The narrow-sense version is the important one: it determines how much relatives resemble each other, how a population responds to selection, and how well a trait can be predicted from DNA. From here on, "heritability" means \(h^2\).

Why relatives resemble each other

Relatives share alleles inherited from common ancestors, so they share part of their breeding values. For a pair of relatives, the covariance between their phenotypes due to additive effects is

\[ \mathrm{Cov}(P_1, P_2) = r\, V_A, \]

where \(r\) is the coefficient of relatedness, the expected fraction of the genome the two share identical by descent. Dividing by \(V_P\), the expected phenotypic correlation is \(r h^2\), provided nothing else makes them similar.

The catch is in the phrase "nothing else". Relatives also share environments, and separating shared genes from shared surroundings is the entire difficulty of the subject.

Estimating heritability from families

Parents and offspring

Regress children's trait values on the average of their two parents (the midparent value). The slope of the line estimates \(h^2\) directly.

Twins

Monozygotic (MZ) twins share all of their DNA; dizygotic (DZ) twins share half on average, like ordinary siblings. Both kinds are the same age and grow up in the same home. If the shared environment makes MZ and DZ pairs alike to the same extent (the equal environments assumption), then the extra similarity of MZ twins must be genetic. Writing \(c^2\) for the share of variance due to shared environment:

\[ r_{MZ} = h^2 + c^2, \qquad r_{DZ} = \tfrac12 h^2 + c^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h^2 = 2\,(r_{MZ} - r_{DZ}). \]

This is Falconer's formula. The fuller version fits three components, additive genetic (A), common environment (C) and unique environment (E), and is known as the ACE model.

Worked example

Suppose adult height has correlations \(r_{MZ} = 0.90\) and \(r_{DZ} = 0.50\). Then \(h^2 = 2(0.90 - 0.50) = 0.80\), shared environment \(c^2 = r_{MZ} - h^2 = 0.10\), and the remainder, 0.10, is environment unique to each twin plus measurement error.

Twin studies have been run on thousands of traits. A meta-analysis covering nearly every published twin study found an average heritability across all traits of about one half. Typical twin estimates: around 0.8 for height, 0.5–0.9 for body-mass index, 0.5–0.8 for blood lipids such as LDL cholesterol, and 0.4–0.6 for liability to coronary artery disease.

Twin estimates can be biased upward if MZ twins share more similar environments than DZ twins, or if non-additive genetic effects are large (MZ twins share all of them, DZ twins only a fraction). Estimates from molecular data (Chapter 17) and from within-family designs (Chapter 23) tend to come out somewhat lower, and reconciling them is an active area of research.

What heritability does not mean

It is not about individuals. A heritability of 0.8 for height does not mean 80% of your height comes from your genes. It is a statement about why people in a population differ from one another.

It is not a constant. It is a ratio, and it changes if either the genetic or the environmental variance changes. Make the environment more uniform and heritability rises, though no gene has changed.

It does not measure whether a trait can be changed. Short-sightedness is highly heritable and fully corrected by glasses. Phenylketonuria is entirely genetic and largely prevented by diet. LDL cholesterol is substantially heritable and lowered effectively by statins.

It says nothing about differences between groups. Heritability describes variation within a population. A trait can be highly heritable within each of two groups while the difference between the groups' averages is entirely environmental. Average height has risen by several centimetres in many countries over the last century, through nutrition and health, while remaining about 80% heritable within each generation.

Response to selection

Heritability was defined by Fisher and Wright and first put to work by breeders, whose question it answers exactly. If the individuals chosen as parents differ from the population mean by \(S\) (the selection differential), the mean of the next generation shifts by

\[ R = h^2 S. \]

This is the breeder's equation, and it is the same regression as . Only the additive part of the parents' superiority is transmitted. A century of selection on milk yield, crop yield and growth rate has confirmed the equation countless times.

Diseases: the liability threshold model

A disease is either present or absent, but most common diseases are as polygenic as height. The standard way to reconcile these is the liability threshold model. Suppose each person has an unobserved, normally distributed liability, the sum of many genetic and environmental risk factors. Those whose liability exceeds a threshold develop the disease. The threshold is set so that the fraction above it equals the disease prevalence \(K\).

Under this model, heritability is defined on the liability scale, and it can be estimated from how much more common the disease is in relatives of patients than in the general population. The ratio of the two risks is the recurrence risk ratio; for siblings it is written \(\lambda_s\). Rare diseases with high heritability have large \(\lambda_s\): for schizophrenia (prevalence around 1%, heritability around 0.8) it is roughly 8 to 10.

A technical point matters in practice. Heritability computed directly on the 0/1 disease scale depends on prevalence and is smaller than heritability of liability. They are related by

\[ h^2_{\text{liability}} = h^2_{\text{observed}}\; \frac{K(1-K)}{z^2}, \]

where \(z\) is the height of the standard normal density at the threshold. Studies that deliberately recruit more cases than the population would supply need a further correction for that ascertainment. Heritabilities of diseases are always quoted on the liability scale so that they can be compared across diseases of different prevalence.

The missing heritability problem

When genome-wide association studies arrived (the first in 2005, the first large wave in 2007), the variants they found explained only a few percent of the variance of traits whose twin heritabilities were 50% or more. This gap became known as missing heritability. Much of Part IV is, in one way or another, the explanation. Most of the heritability was not missing but hidden: it is spread across thousands of variants with effects too small to reach significance individually (Chapter 17), with a further part in rare variants that arrays do not capture (Chapter 20), and some of the gap comes from family-based estimates being a little too high (Chapter 23).

Key points

  • Narrow-sense heritability \(h^2 = V_A/V_P\) is the fraction of trait variance due to additive genetic differences.
  • Relatives with relatedness \(r\) have expected phenotypic correlation \(r h^2\), plus any shared environment.
  • Midparent–offspring slope estimates \(h^2\); twins give \(h^2 = 2(r_{MZ} - r_{DZ})\) under the equal environments assumption.
  • Heritability is specific to a population and environment, and says nothing about individuals, about treatability, or about group differences.
  • Response to selection is \(R = h^2 S\); diseases are handled with a normally distributed liability and a threshold.

Genome-wide association studies

A genome-wide association study tests every measured variant in the genome, one at a time, for association with a trait. The idea is almost embarrassingly simple. Doing it well is a matter of sample size, careful quality control, and honest handling of several million statistical tests.

The basic test

Collect genotypes and a trait for \(n\) people. For each variant, code the genotype \(g\) as 0, 1 or 2 copies of one allele and fit a linear regression:

\[ y_i = \mu + \beta\, g_i + \gamma^\top \mathbf{c}_i + \varepsilon_i . \]

Here \(y_i\) is the trait, \(\mathbf{c}_i\) holds covariates such as age, sex, genotyping batch and ancestry principal components, and \(\beta\) is the effect per copy of the allele. The output for each variant is an estimate \(\hat\beta\), its standard error, and a p-value for the null hypothesis \(\beta = 0\). For a yes/no disease outcome, logistic regression is used and \(\hat\beta\) is a log odds ratio.

This model is additive: it assumes two copies have twice the effect of one. Chapter 12 explained why this is the right default. \(\hat\beta\) estimates the average effect of allele substitution, which captures most of what a variant does to the trait's variance whatever its true mode of action.

The complete set of \(\hat\beta\), standard errors and p-values for every variant is the study's summary statistics. Because they contain no individual-level data they can usually be shared openly (though for a small cohort, a person’s participation can sometimes be inferred from them), and most of the methods in the remaining chapters take summary statistics as input.

Millions of tests

A modern GWAS tests around ten million variants. At the usual threshold of \(P < 0.05\), half a million would pass by chance. The standard remedy is to demand

\[ P < 5 \times 10^{-8}, \]

known as genome-wide significance. The number comes from a Bonferroni correction. Because of LD, the common variants in a European-ancestry genome behave like roughly one million independent tests, and \(0.05 / 10^6 = 5 \times 10^{-8}\). With this threshold, the chance of even one false positive across the genome is held near 5%. Populations with less LD, and studies that include rare variants, have more independent tests and warrant a somewhat stricter threshold.

Reading the results

The Manhattan plot shows where the signals are. Each tower is a locus. The variant with the smallest p-value in a tower is the lead variant, which, because of LD, is not necessarily the causal one (Chapter 18). To count independent loci, results are clumped: take the most significant variant, remove everything nearby that is correlated with it above some \(r^2\) threshold, and repeat.

The second standard picture is the quantile–quantile (QQ) plot, which checks whether the bulk of test statistics behaves as the null predicts. It is the main diagnostic for confounding and is discussed in the next chapter.

Power: why sample sizes are so large

How big a study is needed to detect a variant? For a quantitative trait measured in standard-deviation units, the expected chi-square statistic at a causal variant exceeds its null value of 1 by the non-centrality parameter

\[ \lambda = n \cdot 2p(1-p)\,\beta^2 = n\, q^2, \]

where \(q^2 = 2p(1-p)\beta^2\) is the fraction of trait variance the variant explains. Power depends on the product of sample size and variance explained, and nothing else. To reach \(5\times10^{-8}\) with 80% power requires \(\lambda \approx 40\).

Worked example

A variant with frequency 0.3 changes LDL cholesterol by 0.02 standard deviations per allele. It explains \(q^2 = 2(0.3)(0.7)(0.02)^2 = 0.000168\) of the variance, less than two hundredths of a percent. With 100,000 people \(\lambda = 16.8\) and power is about 9%. With 500,000 people \(\lambda = 84\) and power is essentially 100%. Effects of this size are typical for common variants, which is why discovery took off only when studies reached hundreds of thousands of participants.

For diseases, power depends on the number of cases more than the total, and rare diseases need deliberate over-sampling of cases (a case–control design). Three more points follow from the formula.

  • A tag SNP with correlation \(r^2\) to the causal variant has its \(\lambda\) multiplied by \(r^2\) (Chapter 9).
  • Measurement error in the trait inflates \(V_P\), shrinks standardised effects, and costs power.
  • Significance is not importance. With a million people, a variant explaining 0.004% of variance is detectable.

The winner's curse

Effect estimates are noisy, and variants are reported only if their estimate happened to clear a stringent threshold. Among variants with modest true effects, the ones that pass are disproportionately those whose estimates were pushed upward by chance. So the published effect sizes of newly discovered variants are, on average, too large. This is the winner's curse. It is strongest for signals just past the threshold, and it is why discoveries are checked in an independent replication sample, where the estimate is unbiased and typically smaller.

What GWAS has found

Two decades of studies across thousands of traits have produced a consistent picture.

Complex traits are extremely polygenic. A study of height in 5.4 million people identified about 12,000 independent signals, clustered in regions that cover roughly a fifth of the genome. Diseases such as coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes and schizophrenia each have hundreds of known loci, and the count keeps rising with sample size.

Effects are small. Typical odds ratios for common disease variants are 1.05 to 1.2. A few exceptions with large effects exist, such as APOE for Alzheimer's disease.

Most signals are non-coding. Around 90% of lead variants fall outside protein-coding sequence, concentrated in regulatory elements active in tissues relevant to the trait. Identifying the genes they act on takes additional data (Chapter 19).

Small effects can mark important biology. A common variant near HMGCR shifts LDL cholesterol only slightly, yet the protein that gene encodes is the target of statins. The size of a natural variant's effect reflects how much that variant perturbs the gene, not how much a drug could. Loci for LDL cholesterol include LDLR, APOB, PCSK9 and SORT1, all of them central to how the liver handles lipoproteins.

Variants are shared across traits. A single locus is often associated with many traits, a phenomenon called pleiotropy, which complicates causal interpretation and is taken up in Chapters 17 and 22.

What a GWAS hit does not tell you

An association at a locus says that some variant in the region, correlated with the lead variant, influences the trait in the population studied, provided confounding has been dealt with. It does not say which variant, which gene, in which tissue, or through what mechanism. Each of those is a separate inferential step, and the remaining chapters take them in turn.

Key points

  • GWAS regresses the trait on each variant's allele count, adjusting for covariates; the output is summary statistics.
  • Genome-wide significance is \(P < 5\times10^{-8}\), a correction for about a million independent common-variant tests.
  • Power depends on \(n \times 2p(1-p)\beta^2\); typical effects need hundreds of thousands of samples.
  • Newly discovered effects are overestimated (winner's curse); replication corrects this.
  • Complex traits are highly polygenic, with small, mostly non-coding effects. A hit identifies a region, not a causal variant or gene.

Confounding, relatedness and mixed models

An association between a variant and a trait can arise without the variant, or anything near it, affecting the trait. The main cause is ancestry. This chapter explains the problem using the population genetics of Part II and describes the tools that control it.

Population stratification

Chapter 10 showed that allele frequencies differ slightly between populations at nearly every variant, simply through drift. Traits and diseases also differ between populations, for reasons that are often environmental: diet, climate, wealth, access to health care. If a study sample mixes people of different ancestries, then any variant whose frequency differs between the groups will be correlated with any trait whose average differs between the groups. This is population stratification.

A classic illustration imagines a study of chopstick use in a sample drawn from San Francisco. Any variant more common in people of East Asian ancestry would be "associated" with using chopsticks. None of them has anything to do with dexterity. The association is real in the data and meaningless biologically.

The effect on any one variant is tiny, but it touches every variant at once. And its statistical weight grows with sample size: a bias that is negligible in a study of 5,000 can be highly significant in one of 500,000.

Diagnosing the problem

Since most variants do not affect the trait, the bulk of test statistics should follow the null distribution. The QQ plot checks this by plotting observed p-values, sorted, against the values expected under the null.

The single-number summary is the genomic inflation factor:

\[ \lambda_{GC} = \frac{\text{median of observed } \chi^2 \text{ statistics}}{0.455}, \]

where 0.455 is the median of a \(\chi^2_1\) distribution. A value near 1 means the typical test behaves as the null predicts. An early correction, genomic control, simply divided all statistics by \(\lambda_{GC}\). It is rarely used now, because in a large study of a polygenic trait \(\lambda_{GC}\) is well above 1 for a good reason: a large fraction of variants are in LD with something causal. Dividing would throw away real signal.

Correcting with principal components

The principal components of the genotype matrix capture the main axes of ancestry in the sample (Chapter 10). Including the top PCs as covariates in the regression removes the part of both genotype and trait that is predictable from ancestry, so only variation within ancestry groups contributes to the test. Ten to twenty PCs are typical.

PCs handle broad ancestry differences well. They do less well with fine-scale structure (differences between neighbouring regions or recent local ancestry), with family relatedness, and with sharply localised environmental effects, all of which may not align with any of the top components.

Relatedness

Standard regression assumes the people in the sample are independent. Relatives are not: they share alleles and they share environments, so their traits and genotypes are both correlated. Treating them as independent overstates the effective sample size, makes standard errors too small, and inflates the test statistics. In a biobank a substantial share of participants have a close relative who is also enrolled; in UK Biobank it is about 30% with a relative of third degree or closer. Discarding them wastes data. And even among "unrelated" people there is a continuum of distant relatedness, called cryptic relatedness, that shades into population structure.

Linear mixed models

The linear mixed model (LMM) handles ancestry and relatedness of every degree in one framework. The idea is to model the trait as the tested variant's effect plus a polygenic background that makes genetically similar people phenotypically similar:

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\gamma + \beta\,\mathbf{g} + \mathbf{u} + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \mathbf{u} \sim N(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma_g^2 \mathbf{K}), \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim N(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma_e^2 \mathbf{I}). \]

\(\mathbf{K}\) is the genetic relationship matrix (GRM). Its entry for persons \(i\) and \(k\) is their average similarity across all standardised SNPs:

\[ K_{ik} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \frac{(g_{ij} - 2p_j)(g_{kj} - 2p_j)}{2p_j(1-p_j)}. \]

For unrelated people from the same population \(K_{ik}\) is close to 0; for siblings about 0.5; for members of the same ancestry group in a mixed sample, slightly positive. The term \(\mathbf{u}\) says that phenotypes covary in proportion to \(\mathbf{K}\). So the model expects relatives, and people of similar ancestry, to have similar traits, and it does not attribute that similarity to the SNP being tested.

This is the same matrix whose eigenvectors are the principal components. Adjusting for the top PCs corrects for the few largest axes of \(\mathbf{K}\); the mixed model accounts for all of it.

Mixed models have a second benefit: power. The polygenic term soaks up trait variance due to all the other causal variants in the genome, which reduces residual noise and sharpens the test of each individual SNP. For highly heritable traits in large samples the gain is equivalent to a noticeably larger study.

Making it work at biobank scale

A naive mixed model needs operations on an \(n \times n\) matrix, which is impossible for half a million people. Three ideas made it practical.

  • Leave one chromosome out (LOCO). If the SNP being tested is also included in the polygenic background, the model partly explains away the very signal it is testing. So the background is built from all chromosomes except the one carrying the tested SNP.
  • Two-step fitting. First, fit a whole-genome model once to obtain each person's polygenic prediction. Second, test each variant against the trait after subtracting that prediction. BOLT-LMM, SAIGE and REGENIE all follow this pattern. REGENIE builds its first-step predictor from ridge regressions on blocks of SNPs, avoiding the relationship matrix entirely.
  • Calibrated tests for unbalanced binary traits. For a disease with few cases and many controls, and especially for rare variants, the usual normal approximation to the test statistic fails badly and produces false positives. SAIGE and REGENIE use a saddlepoint approximation or Firth's penalised regression to keep p-values accurate.

What remains uncorrected

PCs and mixed models work by assuming that confounding follows genome-wide genetic similarity. They can fall short when the confounder is an environmental factor with sharp geographic boundaries that genetic similarity only loosely tracks, and when the confounding is spread thinly across so many variants that it is invisible for any one of them but adds up in analyses that sum over the genome: heritability estimates, polygenic scores, tests of polygenic adaptation (Chapter 11).

There are two further safeguards. LD score regression (Chapter 17) separates inflation due to confounding from inflation due to polygenicity. And family-based designs (Chapter 23) remove confounding by construction: which alleles a child inherits from its parents is random, so comparisons within families are immune to stratification. They are the standard against which population-based estimates are checked.

Other sources of bias worth knowing

Batch effects. If cases and controls were genotyped at different times, on different arrays, or in different laboratories, technical differences masquerade as genetic ones. Batch should be a covariate, and ideally cases and controls are processed together.

Adjusting for heritable covariates. Adjusting a trait for a covariate that is itself influenced by the variant and shares causes with the trait can create associations that do not exist. This is collider bias. Adjusting waist circumference for body-mass index, for example, induces associations at BMI loci.

Selection into the study. Volunteers for biobanks are healthier, wealthier and better educated than the general population. If both a trait and a genetic predisposition influence who takes part, they become correlated among participants even if unrelated in the population, another case of collider bias.

Treatment. Medication changes the trait being measured. People with high LDL cholesterol are put on statins, which lower it; analysing measured LDL without accounting for this shrinks genetic effects. A common fix is to estimate the untreated value, for instance by dividing the measured LDL of statin users by about 0.7.

Key points

  • Stratification: ancestry affects both genotype and trait, creating associations with no causal basis.
  • QQ plots and \(\lambda_{GC}\) diagnose inflation, but polygenic signal inflates them too.
  • Principal components adjust for the main axes of ancestry; linear mixed models account for all genetic similarity through the relationship matrix, and gain power.
  • Biobank-scale methods use two steps and leave-one-chromosome-out predictions, with special tests for unbalanced binary traits.
  • Residual confounding, collider bias, participation bias and treatment effects survive standard corrections and need separate thought.

Phasing, imputation and meta-analysis

An array measures about half a million variants per person. Published GWAS report results for ten million or more, from samples no single study could assemble. Two techniques bridge the gap: imputation fills in unmeasured variants, and meta-analysis combines studies.

The shared-haplotype idea

Both phasing and imputation rest on one fact from Chapters 6 and 9. Any two people, even "unrelated" ones, share common ancestors, and over short stretches of the genome they often carry identical copies of the same ancestral haplotype, unbroken by recombination. So over a short window, your chromosome looks like a copy of someone else's; over the next window, like a copy of a different person's. Each chromosome is a mosaic of haplotypes that also exist in other people.

Na Li and Matthew Stephens turned this picture into a statistical model. A new haplotype is generated by copying from a set of known haplotypes, moving left to right, occasionally switching which one it copies (representing historical recombination) and occasionally miscopying an allele (representing mutation or error). Because which haplotype is being copied at each position is unobserved, and depends only on the previous position, this is a hidden Markov model (HMM), and standard algorithms compute the probability of every possible copying path efficiently. Nearly all modern phasing and imputation software is an elaboration of the Li–Stephens model.

Phasing

Genotyping reports, for each site, how many alternate alleles a person carries. For a person heterozygous at two sites, it does not say whether the two alternate alleles lie on the same chromosome or on opposite ones. Phasing resolves this, reconstructing the two haplotypes.

Statistical phasing uses the mosaic idea: find the pair of haplotypes that explains the person's genotypes and that both look like plausible mosaics of haplotypes seen in other people. In large samples the method becomes remarkably accurate, because for almost every stretch of your genome somebody else in the sample carries the same haplotype. In biobank-scale data, phase is correct over stretches of millions of bases between switch errors. When parents are genotyped too, phase follows directly from Mendel's laws at most sites.

Imputation

Imputation predicts genotypes at variants that were never measured. It needs a reference panel: a set of people whose genomes have been sequenced, so that their haplotypes are known at every variant.

  1. Phase the study sample at the variants on the array.
  2. For each study haplotype, use the HMM to find which reference haplotypes it matches along each stretch, using only the array variants.
  3. Copy the alleles at the unmeasured variants from the matching reference haplotypes.

Because the match is uncertain, the output for each variant is a probability for each genotype. These are usually summarised as a dosage, the expected number of alternate alleles, a number between 0 and 2 such as 1.93. The dosage goes into the GWAS regression in place of the 0/1/2 genotype.

How well does it work?

Imputation software reports a quality score for each variant (called INFO or \(R^2\) depending on the program) that estimates the squared correlation between imputed dosages and the true genotypes. It has a direct interpretation from Chapter 9: testing an imputed variant with quality \(r^2\) is equivalent to testing the true genotype in a sample \(r^2\) times as large. Variants below a cut-off such as 0.3 or 0.8 are discarded.

Accuracy depends on three things.

  • Allele frequency. Common variants sit on haplotypes that appear many times in the reference panel and are imputed almost perfectly. Rare variants are harder, because the panel may contain few or no carriers.
  • Reference panel size. Larger panels contain more of the rare haplotypes. The 1000 Genomes Project (about 2,500 people) imputes variants down to around 1% frequency well. The Haplotype Reference Consortium (about 32,000) and TOPMed (about 97,000 sequenced genomes) extend this to frequencies of 0.1% and below.
  • Ancestry match. A study haplotype can only be copied if something like it is in the panel. Imputation is less accurate for people whose ancestry is poorly represented in reference data, one of the concrete ways in which the under-representation of most of the world's populations in genomics degrades results for them.

Variants seen in only a handful of people cannot be imputed reliably from any current panel and must be found by sequencing (Chapter 20).

Meta-analysis

Chapter 14 showed that detecting typical effects takes hundreds of thousands of people. For most traits no single cohort is that large, and data-protection rules usually prevent pooling individual-level data. The solution is for each study to run its own GWAS and share only summary statistics, which are then combined by meta-analysis. Imputation is what makes this possible, since it puts studies genotyped on different arrays onto a common set of variants.

Fixed-effect, inverse-variance weighting

Suppose study \(k\) reports estimate \(\hat\beta_k\) with standard error \(s_k\) for a variant. If all studies are estimating the same true effect, the best combined estimate is a weighted average in which each study's weight is the inverse of its variance:

\[ \hat\beta = \frac{\sum_k w_k \hat\beta_k}{\sum_k w_k}, \qquad w_k = \frac{1}{s_k^2}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_k w_k}}. \]

Precise studies count for more. The combined standard error is smaller than any single study's. If the studies are independent, this is essentially as precise as analysing all the individuals together, which is why sharing summary statistics loses almost nothing.

Heterogeneity

Do the studies agree with each other more or less than chance would predict? Cochran's Q statistic tests this, and \(I^2\) expresses the share of the variation between estimates that exceeds sampling error. Real heterogeneity can arise from different ancestries (different LD between the tested and causal variants), different ways of measuring the trait, different environments, or errors in one study. A random-effects meta-analysis allows the true effect to vary between studies and gives wider intervals.

Practical hazards

Most errors in meta-analysis are bookkeeping errors, and they are common.

  • Allele alignment. Each study must report effects for the same allele. If one study's effect refers to allele A and another's to allele G, one sign must be flipped. Variants whose two alleles are complementary bases (A/T or C/G) are ambiguous about which DNA strand was reported, and are often dropped unless allele frequencies settle the matter.
  • Genome build. Positions must refer to the same version of the reference genome.
  • Trait definition and units. Effects in mg/dL cannot be averaged with effects in standard deviations.
  • Overlapping samples. If the same people appear in two studies, the estimates are correlated and the combined standard error is too small.

When only p-values and directions are comparable across studies, an alternative is to combine Z-scores weighted by the square root of sample size.

Combining ancestries

Meta-analysis across ancestries increases sample size and, because LD patterns differ between populations, narrows down which variant in a region is responsible: the causal variant should be associated in every population, while its mere neighbours will be strongly associated only where LD with it is high. Most causal effects appear to be similar across ancestries, though allele frequencies, and therefore power and variance explained, can differ greatly.

Key points

  • Every chromosome is approximately a mosaic of haplotypes carried by other people; the Li–Stephens HMM formalises this.
  • Phasing reconstructs the two haplotypes from unphased genotypes; imputation copies unmeasured alleles from a sequenced reference panel.
  • Imputation quality \(r^2\) scales the effective sample size; it is high for common variants and falls for rare ones and for ancestries missing from the panel.
  • Fixed-effect meta-analysis weights each study by \(1/\mathrm{SE}^2\) and is nearly as efficient as pooling raw data.
  • Allele alignment, genome build, units and sample overlap are the routine sources of error.

SNP heritability and genetic correlation

Genome-wide significant variants explain a small part of heritability. But a GWAS contains information in all of its variants, including the millions that fall short of significance. This chapter shows how to add that information up, for one trait and for pairs of traits.

Heritability from unrelated people

Classical heritability estimates compare close relatives, who share long stretches of genome and also share environments. In 2010 Jian Yang, Peter Visscher and colleagues asked a different question: among people with no known relationship, do those who happen to be slightly more similar genetically also have slightly more similar traits?

Among unrelated people from one population, the off-diagonal entries of the genetic relationship matrix (Chapter 15) are all near zero, varying by perhaps ±0.005. That is tiny, but with tens of thousands of people there are hundreds of millions of pairs. The mixed model of Chapter 15,

\[ \mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y}) = \sigma_g^2\,\mathbf{K} + \sigma_e^2\,\mathbf{I}, \]

is fitted to find the \(\sigma_g^2\) that best explains how phenotypic similarity tracks genetic similarity. The ratio

\[ h^2_{SNP} = \frac{\sigma_g^2}{\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_e^2} \]

is the SNP heritability: the share of trait variance explained by all the SNPs used to build \(\mathbf{K}\), taken together. The method is called GREML (genomic relatedness restricted maximum likelihood) and is implemented in the software GCTA.

A simpler version makes the logic transparent. Standardise the trait. For every pair of people, multiply their two trait values, and regress those products on the pair's genetic relatedness \(K_{ik}\). The slope estimates \(h^2_{SNP}\). This is Haseman–Elston regression.

Applied to height, the method found that common SNPs explain about 45% of the variance, when genome-wide significant hits at the time explained about 5%. Most of the "missing" heritability was there all along, in variants with effects too small to detect one at a time.

What SNP heritability does and does not include

\(h^2_{SNP}\) captures causal variants that are either among the SNPs used or well tagged by them. With array or imputed data that means mostly common variants. Rare causal variants are poorly tagged and are left out, so \(h^2_{SNP}\) is a lower bound on \(h^2\). When the calculation is repeated with whole-genome sequence data, so that rare variants are included directly, the estimate for height rises to roughly 0.7, approaching the family-based figure.

Because distant relatives do not share households, the estimate is much less exposed to shared-environment bias than twin studies. It is not immune to confounding: population structure that survives adjustment will inflate it.

LD score regression

GREML needs individual-level data. LD score regression (LDSC) gets at the same quantity from summary statistics alone, and at the same time solves the puzzle left open by : how to distinguish inflation caused by polygenicity from inflation caused by confounding.

The key observation is that a SNP's test statistic reflects not only its own effect but the effects of every causal variant it is in LD with. Define the LD score of SNP \(j\) as the sum of its squared correlations with all SNPs around it:

\[ \ell_j = \sum_{k} r_{jk}^2 . \]

A SNP with a high LD score tags many neighbours. If causal variants are scattered across the genome, a SNP that tags more of the genome is more likely to tag something causal, so it should have a larger test statistic on average. Confounding from stratification or relatedness, by contrast, inflates all SNPs regardless of how much LD they have. Working through the expectation gives

\[ E[\chi^2_j] = \frac{N h^2_{SNP}}{M}\,\ell_j + N a + 1, \]

where \(N\) is the sample size, \(M\) the number of SNPs, and \(a\) measures confounding. So regress the chi-square statistics on LD scores. The slope gives the heritability. The intercept, minus 1, gives the confounding.

LD scores are computed once from a reference panel matching the ancestry of the GWAS. The method needs nothing else but summary statistics, which made it possible to analyse hundreds of published GWAS in a uniform way. Its estimates are noisier than GREML's and rest on assumptions about how effect sizes relate to LD and allele frequency, which have been debated and refined.

An intercept near 1 alongside a \(\lambda_{GC}\) of, say, 1.5 is the typical result for a large well-conducted GWAS: the inflation is real signal. An intercept clearly above 1 is a warning of residual stratification or cryptic relatedness.

Partitioning heritability

The same regression can ask where in the genome heritability lies. Classify SNPs by functional annotation (coding sequence, enhancers active in liver, regions of open chromatin in a particular cell type) and compute a separate LD score for each category. Fitting them jointly estimates the heritability contributed by each. This is stratified LD score regression.

The enrichment of a category is its share of heritability divided by its share of SNPs. Regions conserved across mammals make up a few percent of the genome and account for a far larger fraction of heritability for most traits. Enrichments in tissue-specific regulatory regions identify the tissues and cell types through which a trait's genetics act: liver for LDL cholesterol, brain for schizophrenia, immune cells for autoimmune disease. This is one of the most direct routes from GWAS to biology.

Genetic correlation

Two traits can share genetic causes. The genetic correlation \(r_g\) is the correlation between the genetic effects on trait 1 and the genetic effects on trait 2, across the genome:

\[ r_g = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}_g(1,2)}{\sqrt{h_1^2\, h_2^2}}. \]

It ranges from −1 to 1. A positive value means alleles that raise one trait tend to raise the other. It is estimated by cross-trait LD score regression: replace \(\chi^2_j\) with the product of the two traits' Z-scores, \(z_{1j} z_{2j}\), and regress it on LD score. The slope is proportional to the genetic covariance.

The method has a very convenient property. If the two GWAS share participants, the shared environmental noise shifts the intercept but not the slope. So genetic correlations can be estimated from any two sets of summary statistics without knowing how much the samples overlap. That allowed genetic correlations to be computed between hundreds of traits measured in different studies.

Interpreting genetic correlations

The results map relationships among traits: LDL cholesterol and triglycerides are genetically correlated with coronary artery disease; many psychiatric disorders are substantially correlated with one another; educational attainment is correlated with a wide range of health outcomes. A genetic correlation by itself cannot tell you why. There are several possible reasons:

  • One trait causes the other. Variants that raise LDL cholesterol raise coronary risk because LDL causes atherosclerosis. This is sometimes called vertical pleiotropy.
  • Shared biology. The same variants affect both traits through separate pathways (horizontal pleiotropy), or both traits depend on a common upstream process.
  • Artefacts. Assortative mating across traits, shared confounding, or misdiagnosis between two conditions can all induce genetic correlation.

Separating the first from the others is the purpose of Mendelian randomization (Chapter 22). A genetic correlation is a genome-wide average and can be near zero even when individual loci are shared, if their effects on the two traits point in inconsistent directions.

How polygenic are complex traits?

Partitioning results show that heritability is spread remarkably evenly: each chromosome contributes roughly in proportion to its length, and most 100-kb windows in the genome contain variants affecting height. This led to the omnigenic hypothesis. It proposes that gene regulatory networks are so interconnected that essentially any gene expressed in a relevant cell type has some small effect on the trait, by nudging a smaller set of "core" genes with direct roles. On this view the thousands of weak GWAS signals are an expected consequence of how cells are wired, and the biologically informative genes are a small subset of the hits.

Key points

  • SNP heritability asks whether genetically more similar unrelated people have more similar traits; for height it is 40–50% from common SNPs.
  • LD score regression: \(E[\chi^2_j] = N h^2 \ell_j/M + Na + 1\). The slope is heritability and the intercept is confounding.
  • Stratified LDSC assigns heritability to functional categories and points to relevant tissues.
  • Genetic correlation measures shared genetic effects between traits and can be estimated from summary statistics even with overlapping samples.
  • A genetic correlation does not establish causation between traits.

Fine-mapping and colocalization

A GWAS locus is a cluster of correlated variants, any of which might be responsible. Fine-mapping asks which variant is causal. Colocalization asks whether two traits that share a locus share a causal variant. Both are exercises in Bayes' rule.

Why the lead variant is not the answer

Within an LD block, variants are so correlated that their test statistics are nearly interchangeable. Which one has the smallest p-value is partly luck: sampling noise can easily push a non-causal neighbour ahead of the true causal variant. If two variants have \(r^2 = 0.95\), the data can barely tell them apart, and no amount of staring at p-values will help. The honest answer is a probability for each variant.

Fine-mapping with one causal variant

Start with the simplest assumption: exactly one variant in the region is causal. Then there are as many hypotheses as variants, and we want the posterior probability of each.

For each variant, compare how well the data are explained if that variant has a real effect versus no effect. Jon Wakefield's approximate Bayes factor does this using only the GWAS estimate and its standard error. Let \(V = \mathrm{SE}^2\) be the sampling variance of \(\hat\beta\), and assume that true effects, when they exist, come from a normal distribution with variance \(W\). Then with \(z = \hat\beta/\mathrm{SE}\),

\[ \mathrm{BF} = \sqrt{\frac{V}{V+W}}\; \exp\!\left( \frac{z^2}{2} \cdot \frac{W}{V+W} \right). \]

(Wakefield wrote the ratio the other way up, as no effect over effect. The form above is the one used in fine-mapping and colocalization.) The Bayes factor grows very fast with \(z\). If every variant is equally likely to be causal beforehand, the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) of variant \(j\) is its share of the total:

\[ \mathrm{PIP}_j = \frac{\mathrm{BF}_j}{\sum_k \mathrm{BF}_k}. \]

A 95% credible set is then formed by sorting variants by PIP and adding them until their PIPs sum to at least 0.95. Under the model's assumptions the set contains the causal variant with 95% probability. The size of the credible set is the practical measure of resolution: a set of one or two variants is a result that an experimentalist can take to the bench, while a set of two hundred means the data cannot resolve the signal.

Resolution improves with three things: sample size (larger \(z\) values separate correlated variants more sharply), weaker LD (the reason multi-ancestry data help, as noted in Chapter 16), and complete coverage of variants. That last point is easily overlooked. Fine-mapping can only assign probability to variants in the data. If the causal variant was not genotyped or imputed, the credible set will confidently contain the wrong ones.

More than one causal variant

Many loci contain more than one independent signal, and well-studied lipid genes such as PCSK9 and LDLR contain several. The single-variant assumption then fails, sometimes badly. A non-causal variant in LD with two causal ones can show a stronger association than either.

Conditional analysis is the classical remedy: add the lead variant to the regression as a covariate and retest the others. Any that remain significant represent further signals. Repeat until nothing is left. This can be done approximately from summary statistics with an LD reference.

Modern methods model multiple causal variants jointly. SuSiE (the "sum of single effects" model) writes the region's genetic effect as a sum of several components, each of which is a single-causal-variant model of the kind above. It fits them in turn, each to the residual left by the others. The output is a separate credible set for each independent signal, and a PIP for each variant. FINEMAP takes a related approach, searching over combinations of causal variants.

The LD matrix must be right

Methods that allow multiple causal variants need the correlations among all variants in the region. When working from summary statistics these come from an LD reference panel, and the results are extremely sensitive to mismatches between the panel and the GWAS sample. Differences in ancestry, a small reference panel, or a meta-analysis in which variants have different sample sizes can produce confident, wrong credible sets. LD computed from the GWAS sample itself is strongly preferred.

Using functional information

Not all variants are equally plausible beforehand. A variant in a liver enhancer is a better candidate for a cholesterol locus than one in unannotated sequence. Functionally informed fine-mapping replaces the flat prior with one based on annotations, weighted by how enriched each annotation is for heritability (Chapter 17). This can break ties between variants that LD alone cannot separate.

Colocalization

Suppose a locus is associated with LDL cholesterol, and the same region is associated with the expression level of a nearby gene in liver. It is tempting to conclude that the variant affects LDL by changing that gene's expression. But regulatory variants are everywhere: most genes have at least one variant affecting their expression. Two signals in the same region may be driven by two different variants that merely happen to be near each other and in partial LD.

Colocalization tests whether two association signals are consistent with a single shared causal variant. The widely used method coloc considers five mutually exclusive hypotheses about a region:

HypothesisMeaning
H0No causal variant for either trait
H1A causal variant for trait 1 only
H2A causal variant for trait 2 only
H3Both traits have a causal variant, and they are different variants
H4Both traits share one causal variant

For each hypothesis, the method sums the evidence over all the variant configurations consistent with it, using the same approximate Bayes factors as fine-mapping, computed separately for each trait at each variant. Combined with prior probabilities, this yields a posterior probability for each hypothesis. The quantity of interest is PP.H4, and a common threshold for declaring colocalization is PP.H4 above 0.8.

The intuition is visible in the figure. If one variant drives both signals, then the whole pattern of association across the region, which is set by each variant's LD with the causal one, should look the same for both traits.

Using coloc sensibly

  • The priors matter. The defaults assume each variant has a 1 in 10,000 chance of being causal for each trait and a 1 in 100,000 chance of being causal for both. The last number is influential, and results should be checked for sensitivity to it.
  • Low PP.H4 is not evidence of distinct variants. If one study is underpowered, the posterior piles up on H1 or H2. Only a high PP.H3 supports distinct causal variants.
  • Standard coloc assumes at most one causal variant per trait. Where there are several signals, the assumption fails. The current practice is to fine-map each trait first with SuSiE and then test colocalization between each pair of credible sets.
  • Both datasets should come from populations with similar LD.
  • Colocalization is not causation. A shared variant affecting both gene expression and disease is what we would see if expression mediates the disease effect. It is also what we would see if the variant affects the two through separate mechanisms.

Where this leaves us

After fine-mapping, a locus is described by one or more credible sets, each a short list of candidate variants with probabilities. After colocalization, some of those signals are linked to molecular traits such as the expression of particular genes in particular tissues. This is the raw material for the next question: which gene does each signal act through?

Key points

  • LD makes the lead variant an unreliable guide; fine-mapping assigns each variant a posterior inclusion probability.
  • With one causal variant, \(\mathrm{PIP}_j = \mathrm{BF}_j / \sum_k \mathrm{BF}_k\); the 95% credible set is the smallest set of variants whose PIPs sum to 0.95.
  • Multiple signals need joint models such as SuSiE, and these need an accurate LD matrix.
  • Colocalization (coloc) computes the posterior probability that two traits share a single causal variant (PP.H4), as opposed to distinct variants in the same region (PP.H3).
  • A missing causal variant, a mismatched LD panel, or unmodelled multiple signals all produce confident wrong answers.

From variants to genes

Most trait-associated variants lie outside protein-coding sequence and act by changing how much of some gene is made, somewhere, at some time. Working out which gene, in which tissue, is the step that turns a statistical association into biology, and it remains the hardest part of the field.

Why the nearest gene is only a guess

Regulatory elements such as enhancers can sit tens or hundreds of thousands of bases from the gene they control, with other genes lying in between. The DNA loops in three dimensions to bring an enhancer into contact with its target promoter. So the gene closest to a GWAS signal is a reasonable first guess, and is right more often than not, but it is wrong often enough that it cannot be relied on.

The most cited cautionary case is the strongest obesity locus in the genome, which lies within an intron of the gene FTO. For years FTO was assumed to be the obesity gene. Later work showed that the associated variants sit in an enhancer that regulates IRX3 and IRX5, genes half a million to more than a million bases away, in fat-cell precursors.

Expression quantitative trait loci

If a variant acts by changing a gene's expression, that should be measurable. Collect a tissue from many genotyped people, measure the RNA level of every gene, and run a GWAS with each gene's expression as the trait. A variant associated with the expression of a gene is an expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL).

  • cis-eQTLs affect a nearby gene, conventionally within one million bases, usually by altering a regulatory element on the same chromosome. They have large effects and are easy to detect: with a few hundred samples, most genes show at least one.
  • trans-eQTLs affect distant genes, typically through an intermediate such as a transcription factor. Their effects are small, and tens of thousands of samples are needed.

The GTEx project measured expression in about fifty tissues from more than 800 deceased donors, and is the standard eQTL reference. eQTL effects are often specific to tissues, cell types, developmental stages or stimulated states, which is central to the difficulties described below.

The same design applies to other molecular traits: protein levels (pQTLs), RNA splicing (sQTLs), DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility. Large plasma proteomics studies in biobanks have made pQTLs especially useful, since protein levels are closer to function and to drug mechanisms than RNA is.

Linking eQTLs to GWAS signals

Because nearly every gene has an eQTL, a GWAS lead variant that is also an eQTL for some gene means little. The required test is colocalization (Chapter 18): do the trait signal and the expression signal share a causal variant? A colocalized eQTL in a relevant tissue is good evidence that the gene is the one through which the variant acts, and its direction tells you whether more or less of the gene raises the trait.

Transcriptome-wide association studies

A transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) tests genes directly. It proceeds in three steps.

  1. In a reference dataset with both genotypes and expression, build a model for each gene that predicts its expression from nearby variants.
  2. Apply the model to the genotypes of a large GWAS cohort to obtain each person's genetically predicted expression. No expression is measured in the GWAS cohort.
  3. Test whether predicted expression is associated with the trait.

The test can be computed from GWAS summary statistics, as a weighted combination of variant Z-scores. TWAS reduces the number of tests from millions of variants to about 20,000 genes, combines several weak variant signals into one gene-level test, and gives the direction of effect. PrediXcan and FUSION are the standard implementations.

A TWAS association does not show that the gene is causal. Predicted expression is a function of variants, and those variants are in LD with others that may affect the trait through a different gene. Neighbouring genes are frequently regulated by the same variants, so a single causal gene produces a cluster of TWAS hits around it. TWAS associations are best treated as a way of prioritising genes at known loci, to be followed by colocalization and gene-level fine-mapping.

Summary-data Mendelian randomization (SMR) is closely related: it uses a gene's top eQTL as an instrument to estimate the effect of expression on the trait (Chapter 22 explains the logic), with an accompanying test for whether one variant or two underlies the signals.

The puzzle of missing eQTLs

If most GWAS variants are regulatory, they should be eQTLs. Yet even with large eQTL resources, only a minority of GWAS signals convincingly colocalize with an eQTL for a plausible gene. Several explanations have support.

  • Wrong context. The relevant regulatory effect may occur only in a rare cell type, a developmental window, or a stimulated state that bulk adult tissue does not capture.
  • Selection. Genes that matter most for disease tend to be under strong selective constraint, which removes variants with large effects on their expression. Variants affecting such genes have small regulatory effects that eQTL studies are underpowered to see. The eQTLs that are easy to find are, for the same reason, disproportionately in genes that tolerate variation.
  • Other mechanisms. Splicing, translation, and protein function are not visible in expression levels.

Single-cell eQTL mapping, which measures expression cell by cell and can therefore resolve cell types and states, is the main current response to the first of these.

Other lines of evidence

Coding variants. If the credible set contains a missense or loss-of-function variant, the affected gene is immediately the leading candidate. Coding variants are a small minority of GWAS signals but account for many of the most secure gene assignments.

Rare-variant associations. If rare protein-altering variants in a gene near a GWAS signal are themselves associated with the same trait, that gene is very probably causal (Chapter 20). This convergence is among the strongest evidence available.

Regulatory maps. Does a credible-set variant fall in open chromatin or an enhancer in a relevant cell type? Does that element physically contact a gene's promoter in chromosome-conformation data? Models combining enhancer activity with contact frequency predict enhancer–gene pairs.

Gene-set and pathway analysis. If the genes implicated across many loci cluster in a pathway or are jointly expressed in a cell type, a candidate gene belonging to that pathway gains support. Methods of this kind also reveal the biology of a trait as a whole.

Experiment. Editing the variant in cells, or perturbing the candidate gene with CRISPR, and observing the predicted change is the final arbiter.

In practice these are combined into a score for each gene at each locus. Machine-learning approaches trained on loci where the causal gene is known do this formally.

A locus solved end to end: SORT1

A region on chromosome 1 is among the strongest associations with both LDL cholesterol and heart attack. The signal lies in non-coding sequence between several genes. Follow-up showed that a single variant creates a binding site for a liver transcription factor, which increases expression of SORT1 specifically in liver. SORT1 encodes sortilin, which alters how the liver secretes and takes up lipoprotein particles. Changing Sort1 expression in mouse liver changed plasma LDL in the direction predicted. Every link in was addressed, for a gene that had not previously been known to influence LDL cholesterol in blood. A second group’s mouse experiments, published the same year, pointed the opposite way, and the mechanism was debated for years afterwards.

Key points

  • Most GWAS variants are regulatory; the nearest gene is a useful prior and not a conclusion.
  • eQTLs link variants to gene expression; colocalization tests whether an eQTL and a trait signal share a causal variant.
  • TWAS tests genetically predicted expression against a trait; hits can reflect LD and co-regulation, so they prioritise genes without proving causation.
  • Many GWAS signals lack a matching eQTL, because of cell-type and context specificity and because selection limits regulatory variation at important genes.
  • Convincing gene assignments rest on convergent evidence: coding variants, rare-variant burden, regulatory maps and experiments.

Rare variants

Common variants have small effects. Variants with large effects exist, but natural selection keeps them rare, so they are missed by arrays and hard to test one at a time. Sequencing and gene-level tests bring them into view, and they often point straight at a gene.

Why large effects are rare

Chapter 8 showed that a harmful allele settles at a frequency of roughly \(\mu/hs\): the stronger the selection against it, the rarer it stays. Most traits and diseases are tied to fitness, directly or through stabilizing selection on underlying physiology, so a variant that shifts a trait strongly is selected against and kept at low frequency. Variants that are common today are, almost by definition, ones selection has not objected to, which means their effects are small.

The result is a consistent inverse relationship between allele frequency and effect size, seen for virtually every complex trait.

Rare variants are also numerous. Chapter 7 noted that most variable sites in a large sequenced sample are seen in only one or a few people. And they are young, so they have had little time to recombine onto different backgrounds and are poorly tagged by common SNPs. Arrays and imputation miss them; finding them requires sequencing, of the exome or the whole genome.

Why single-variant tests fail

The power formula of Chapter 14 depends on \(2p(1-p)\beta^2\). A variant carried by 10 people in a study of 100,000 has \(p = 0.00005\). Even with an effect of a full standard deviation, the non-centrality parameter is only about 10, well short of the 40 needed. For variants seen once or twice, a single-variant test is hopeless whatever the effect.

Aggregating variants within a gene

The solution is to change the unit of analysis from the variant to the gene. If a gene matters for a trait, then many different rare variants that damage it should each affect the trait, and although each is too rare to test, together they may be carried by hundreds of people.

Burden tests

A burden test collapses the qualifying rare variants in a gene into a single score per person:

\[ B_i = \sum_{j \in \text{gene}} w_j\, g_{ij}, \]

where \(g_{ij}\) is person \(i\)'s genotype at variant \(j\) and \(w_j\) is a weight. In the simplest version \(B_i\) just records whether the person carries any qualifying variant. The trait is then regressed on \(B\), with the same covariates and mixed-model machinery as a GWAS.

A burden test is powerful when most of the included variants are causal and push the trait in the same direction. It loses power when many neutral variants are mixed in, diluting the signal, or when some variants raise the trait and others lower it, cancelling out.

Variance-component tests: SKAT

The sequence kernel association test (SKAT) avoids assuming a common direction. It asks whether the variants' effects are, as a group, more variable than zero. In effect it computes a score statistic for each variant, squares it, and adds:

\[ Q = \sum_{j} w_j^2\, S_j^2, \qquad S_j = \sum_i g_{ij}\,(y_i - \hat\mu_i), \]

where \(\hat\mu_i\) is the trait value predicted from covariates alone. Because the scores are squared, effects in opposite directions add up. SKAT is robust to mixed directions and to a moderate proportion of neutral variants, and is less powerful than a burden test when all effects do align.

Since the true architecture of a gene is not known beforehand, SKAT-O combines the two tests and adapts to the data. A common current practice is to run several tests and several variant sets for each gene and combine the resulting p-values with a method such as the Cauchy combination test (ACAT), which stays valid when the tests are correlated.

Choosing which variants to include

The choice of variants, called the mask, matters more than the choice of test. Including neutral variants costs power, so masks are built from predicted functional consequence and frequency:

  • Predicted loss-of-function (pLoF): stop-gain, frameshift and essential splice-site variants. These are the cleanest class, since nearly all of them abolish one copy of the gene and so act in the same direction.
  • Missense variants predicted to be damaging, according to computational scores built from evolutionary conservation and protein structure.
  • Frequency thresholds, such as below 1%, 0.1% or 0.01%, on the reasoning that rarer variants are more likely to be damaging.

With about 20,000 genes and a handful of masks, the significance threshold for gene-based tests is typically around \(2.5 \times 10^{-6}\) or somewhat stricter.

What rare variants offer

They identify the gene directly. A burden of loss-of-function variants in a gene implicates that gene, without the variant-to-gene problem of Chapter 19.

They show the direction of effect. Loss-of-function variants reveal what happens when a gene's activity is reduced. If carriers have lower disease risk and are otherwise healthy, a drug that inhibits the gene's product is predicted to be both effective and safe.

They have large, interpretable effects. Effects of half a standard deviation or more are common among significant rare-variant associations, compared with a few hundredths for typical GWAS hits.

The model case: PCSK9

PCSK9 was first connected to cholesterol through rare gain-of-function mutations that cause very high LDL in families. Researchers then found that about 2–3% of African Americans carry a loss-of-function variant in the gene. Carriers had LDL cholesterol about 28% lower than non-carriers and a risk of coronary heart disease lower by nearly 90% (an imprecise estimate, from few events), with no evident ill effects. Lifelong reduction of the protein was therefore protective and safe. Antibody drugs that inhibit PCSK9 reached patients within about a decade. The same reasoning has since guided drugs against ANGPTL3, APOC3 and LPA.

Drug targets supported by human genetic evidence are, across the industry, roughly twice as likely to succeed in clinical development as those without it. This has made rare-variant association studies in large sequenced biobanks a core part of drug discovery. Exome sequencing of roughly half a million UK Biobank participants is the best-known example.

Allelic series

The most informative genes show an allelic series: common regulatory variants with small effects, rare missense variants with moderate effects, and loss-of-function variants with large effects, all moving the trait in directions consistent with how much they change the gene's activity. Such a series is a natural dose–response curve, and it predicts what a given degree of drug inhibition should do.

Constraint: learning from variants that are missing

Selection removes damaging variants from the population, so genes in which loss of function is harmful contain fewer such variants than the mutation rate predicts. With sequence data from hundreds of thousands of people, one can count the loss-of-function variants observed in each gene and compare the count with the expected number. Genes with a large deficit are constrained: losing even one copy reduces fitness. The gnomAD database publishes such scores for every gene.

Constraint is a direct application of mutation–selection balance, and it is useful in three ways. It helps in diagnosing rare disease, since a new loss-of-function mutation in a highly constrained gene is a strong candidate. It marks genes in which inhibition by a drug might be dangerous. And constrained genes are enriched for heritability of complex traits, which shows that selection has shaped the common-variant architecture too.

De novo mutations

For severe conditions that prevent reproduction, inherited dominant variants cannot be the main cause, because they would be eliminated at once. (Recessive variants can persist in healthy carriers.) Many cases arise from de novo mutations, present in the child and in neither parent. Sequencing parent–child trios finds them directly. Since each person has only one or two new mutations in coding sequence, finding several unrelated patients with new damaging mutations in the same gene is extremely unlikely by chance. This design has identified hundreds of genes for severe developmental disorders and has contributed substantially to the genetics of autism.

Cautions specific to rare variants

  • Stratification is harder to control. Rare variants are recent and so geographically localised. Their structure is finer than principal components built from common variants can capture.
  • Test statistics misbehave. With few carriers and unbalanced case–control ratios, normal approximations fail (Chapter 15).
  • Annotation is imperfect. Some predicted loss-of-function variants do not cause loss of function, because of sequencing artefacts or because they fall in parts of the gene that are dispensable.
  • A rare-variant signal can be a shadow of a common one. Rare variants can be in LD with a nearby common causal variant, so burden results should be conditioned on known GWAS signals at the locus.

How much heritability is rare?

For most complex traits, the bulk of heritability comes from common variants, simply because a variant's contribution to variance is \(2p(1-p)\beta^2\) and rare variants have small \(2p(1-p)\). Rare coding variants found by burden tests explain only around a percent or two of trait variance. Rare non-coding variants contribute more, and whole-genome sequence estimates suggest that rare variants together account for much of the gap between SNP heritability and family-based heritability (Chapter 17). Rare variants matter a great deal to the individuals who carry them and to biological insight, and less to population-level variance.

Key points

  • Selection keeps large-effect variants rare, producing an inverse relation between frequency and effect size.
  • Rare variants must be found by sequencing and tested in aggregate, gene by gene.
  • Burden tests assume a shared direction; SKAT allows mixed directions; SKAT-O and combination tests hedge between them. The variant mask is the most consequential choice.
  • Loss-of-function associations name the gene and the direction of effect, which makes them valuable for drug target selection.
  • Constraint scores measure selection against loss of each gene from the deficit of observed damaging variants.

Polygenic scores

If a trait depends on thousands of variants of small effect, no single one says much about a person. Their sum can. A polygenic score adds up a person's trait-associated alleles, weighted by effect, into a single number that estimates their genetic predisposition.

The definition

A polygenic score (PGS), also called a polygenic risk score (PRS) when the trait is a disease, is a weighted sum of allele counts:

\[ S_i = \sum_{j=1}^{M} \hat w_j\, g_{ij}, \]

where \(g_{ij}\) is person \(i\)'s genotype at variant \(j\) and \(\hat w_j\) is a weight derived from a GWAS. It is an estimate of the breeding value of Chapter 12. If we knew every causal variant and its exact effect, the score would capture all of the additive genetic variance, and its squared correlation with the trait would equal \(h^2\). Real scores fall short for two reasons: effects are estimated with error, and causal variants are not known, only tagged.

Choosing the weights

The obvious choice, using the GWAS \(\hat\beta\) for every variant, works poorly. GWAS effects are marginal: each \(\hat\beta_j\) includes the effects of every causal variant in LD with SNP \(j\). In a block of fifty correlated SNPs tagging one causal variant, all fifty carry roughly the same estimate, and adding them counts one signal fifty times. And most of the millions of estimates are almost pure noise.

Clumping and thresholding

The simplest fix handles the two problems separately. Clumping keeps one variant per LD block, the most significant, and discards its correlated neighbours. Thresholding keeps only variants with a p-value below some cut-off. The cut-off is chosen by trying several and seeing which predicts best in a separate dataset. For highly polygenic traits the best threshold is often very lenient, far above genome-wide significance, because thousands of variants with real but undetected effects add more signal than noise.

Bayesian shrinkage

A better approach uses all variants and models LD directly. Assume the true effects come from some prior distribution, which says most effects are zero or tiny and a few are larger. Then compute each variant's expected true effect given the GWAS statistics and the LD among variants. The effect of this is to shrink noisy estimates toward zero, strongly for weak signals and hardly at all for strong ones, and to share out a signal among correlated variants instead of counting it repeatedly.

The methods differ mainly in the prior. LDpred uses a mixture of zero and normally distributed effects. PRS-CS uses a continuous shrinkage prior that adapts to each variant. SBayesR uses a mixture of several normal distributions. All need only summary statistics and an LD reference panel, and all usually outperform clumping and thresholding, particularly for highly polygenic traits.

Keeping the evaluation honest

Three separate sets of people are needed: a discovery sample (the GWAS), a tuning sample to choose settings such as the p-value threshold, and a test sample used once to report accuracy. If the test individuals were part of the GWAS, or the settings were tuned on the test set, the reported accuracy is inflated. Overlap between discovery and test samples, sometimes hidden inside a large meta-analysis, is the most common error in this literature.

How well can a score predict?

For a quantitative trait, accuracy is the \(R^2\) between score and trait in the test sample, usually as the gain over a model with covariates such as age, sex and principal components. Two things limit it.

The ceiling is the SNP heritability. A score built from common variants cannot explain more variance than those variants do (Chapter 17).

The approach to the ceiling depends on GWAS sample size. A useful approximation is

\[ R^2 \approx \frac{h^2_{SNP}}{1 + \dfrac{M_e}{N\, h^2_{SNP}}}, \]

where \(N\) is the GWAS sample size and \(M_e\) is the effective number of independent segments of the genome, on the order of 50,000 to 100,000 in a European-ancestry population. Accuracy reaches half the ceiling when \(N \approx M_e/h^2_{SNP}\).

For height, with a GWAS of several million people, scores now explain around 40% of the variance in European-ancestry samples, close to the common-variant ceiling. For most diseases the figure is far lower, typically a few percent to about 10% of variance on the liability scale.

Scores for disease risk

For a disease, a score is usually summarised by the odds ratio per standard deviation of score, or by the area under the ROC curve (AUC): the probability that a randomly chosen case has a higher score than a randomly chosen control. An AUC of 0.5 is a coin toss and 1.0 is perfect discrimination. Polygenic scores alone typically reach 0.55 to 0.70 for common diseases.

The two panels of are the same data and explain most disagreements about the usefulness of polygenic scores. A score does not sort people into those who will and will not become ill. Most people who develop the disease have unremarkable scores, because most people have unremarkable scores. What the score does is shift risk, and people in the tails differ substantially from the average.

For coronary artery disease, about 8% of people of European ancestry have a polygenic score that confers at least three times the average risk. That is the level of risk associated with the single-gene condition familial hypercholesterolaemia, and it applies to perhaps twenty times as many people. This comparison is the main argument for clinical use: identifying a sizeable group at high inherited risk who would not be found by family history or cholesterol testing, early enough to act.

Several points keep expectations realistic.

  • Relative and absolute risk differ. Tripling a 1% risk gives 3%. Whether that changes a decision depends on the disease, the intervention and the person's age.
  • Scores add to established predictors, modestly. When combined with age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol and smoking, a polygenic score typically improves discrimination by a small amount. Its particular value is that it is available from birth, before other risk factors appear.
  • High genetic risk can be offset. For coronary disease, people with high scores who have a healthy lifestyle or take statins show large reductions in risk. A score is information for acting on, not a prognosis.
  • A score is a population-level average. It ignores rare variants of large effect, which for a carrier may outweigh the whole score.

The portability problem

The most serious limitation of current polygenic scores is that they work best in people genetically similar to the GWAS participants, and the great majority of GWAS participants to date have been of European ancestry. Applied to people of other ancestries, the same scores are less accurate, and the loss grows with genetic distance. For many traits accuracy in people of African ancestry is only about a fifth to a quarter of that in Europeans. The decline is continuous: it is visible even within a single continental group as individuals become more distant from the training sample.

The reasons come directly from Part II.

  • LD differs. A tag SNP that marks a causal variant well in one population may mark it poorly in another, so its weight is wrong there.
  • Allele frequencies differ. Variants common enough to be discovered in Europeans may be rare elsewhere, and variants common elsewhere may never have been tested.
  • Effects may differ with environment or genetic background, although the evidence suggests causal effects are mostly similar across populations.
  • Confounding differs. Any residual stratification in the weights reflects the structure of the discovery population.

The consequence is a matter of equity. If polygenic scores enter medical practice in their current form, they will benefit most the populations that are already best served. The remedy is larger and more diverse studies, together with methods that combine GWAS from several ancestries and model their LD separately, which improve accuracy in under-represented groups but do not close the gap.

A direct corollary: polygenic scores cannot be compared between ancestry groups. A difference in the average score of two populations reflects differences in LD, allele frequency and ascertainment of the variants at least as much as any difference in genetic predisposition, and tells you nothing reliable about the latter.

What a score actually captures

A score built from a population GWAS predicts through every pathway by which the weighted variants correlate with the trait in that population. That includes direct biological effects in the person, and it can also include effects of parents' genotypes acting through the family environment, the consequences of assortative mating, and residual population structure. For most physiological traits such as lipids these extra components are small. For behavioural and social traits such as educational attainment they are large: the score’s effect roughly halves, and the variance it explains falls to about a quarter, when it is used to compare siblings within a family, where those components cancel. Chapter 23 explains why.

So predictive accuracy and causal interpretation are different things. A score can predict well in the population in which it was trained for reasons that are only partly biological, and for that reason it may not transfer to another setting.

Uses in research

Whatever their clinical future, polygenic scores are standard research tools: as covariates that absorb genetic risk and so sharpen estimates of other effects; for testing whether genetic risk interacts with environment or treatment; for stratifying participants in clinical trials; for asking whether genetic risk for one trait predicts another; and as instruments in Mendelian randomization, the subject of the next chapter.

Key points

  • A polygenic score is \(\sum_j \hat w_j g_{ij}\), an estimate of an individual's additive genetic value.
  • Weights must account for LD and noise: clumping and thresholding, or Bayesian shrinkage methods such as LDpred and PRS-CS.
  • Accuracy is capped by SNP heritability and approaches the cap as GWAS sample size passes \(M_e/h^2\).
  • Scores stratify risk across a population but discriminate poorly between individuals.
  • Accuracy falls with genetic distance from the discovery sample; scores are not comparable across ancestry groups.

Mendelian randomization

Does high LDL cholesterol cause heart disease, or merely accompany it? Observational data struggle to answer questions like this. Mendelian randomization uses the random inheritance of alleles as a natural experiment to answer them.

The problem with observational data

People with higher levels of HDL cholesterol have fewer heart attacks. For decades this was read as protection, and drugs were developed to raise HDL. They raised it and, with one modest exception that also lowered LDL, did not prevent heart attacks. People who take vitamin supplements have less cancer and heart disease; randomised trials of the supplements found no benefit.

The reasons are the two standing weaknesses of observational studies. Confounding: people with high HDL also tend to be leaner, more active, and less often diabetic. Reverse causation: early disease can change the supposed risk factor instead of the other way round. The cure is a randomised controlled trial, in which chance alone decides who gets the exposure, so no confounder can be associated with it. But trials are slow, expensive, and often impossible.

Nature's randomised trial

At conception, each person receives a random one of each parent's two alleles at every site. Which alleles you inherit is not influenced by your later lifestyle, income or illness, and it is fixed before any disease begins. So if a genetic variant reliably raises LDL cholesterol, the people who inherited it form something like the treatment arm of a lifelong trial of higher LDL. If they also have more heart disease, that is evidence LDL is a cause.

This is Mendelian randomization (MR). In statistical terms the variant is an instrumental variable for the exposure.

The estimate

Suppose one more copy of an allele raises the exposure by \(\beta_{GX}\) and changes the outcome by \(\beta_{GY}\). If the allele acts on the outcome only through the exposure, then \(\beta_{GY} = \theta \cdot \beta_{GX}\), where \(\theta\) is the causal effect of exposure on outcome. Rearranging gives the Wald ratio:

\[ \hat\theta = \frac{\hat\beta_{GY}}{\hat\beta_{GX}}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat\theta) \approx \frac{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_{GY})}{|\hat\beta_{GX}|}. \]
Worked example

An allele raises LDL cholesterol by 0.10 SD and raises the log odds of coronary disease by 0.04. The Wald ratio is \(0.04/0.10 = 0.4\) log odds per SD of LDL, an odds ratio of \(e^{0.4} \approx 1.5\) per SD of genetically higher LDL.

The two numbers need not come from the same people. \(\beta_{GX}\) can be taken from a GWAS of the exposure and \(\beta_{GY}\) from a separate GWAS of the outcome. This two-sample MR, using published summary statistics, is why the method became so widely used: any pair of GWAS can be combined.

Many instruments

A single variant gives an imprecise estimate. With many independent variants associated with the exposure, each gives its own ratio, and they can be combined. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) estimate is the meta-analysis of the ratios, or equivalently the slope of a weighted regression of the outcome effects on the exposure effects, forced through the origin:

\[ \hat\theta_{IVW} = \frac{\sum_j \hat\beta_{GX,j}\,\hat\beta_{GY,j}\,/\,s_j^2}{\sum_j \hat\beta_{GX,j}^2\,/\,s_j^2}, \qquad s_j = \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_{GY,j}). \]

The line passes through the origin because a variant with no effect on the exposure should have no effect on the outcome.

The central problem: pleiotropy

Assumption 1 can be checked in data. Assumption 2 is made plausible by Mendelian inheritance and good control of population structure. Assumption 3, the exclusion restriction, is the one that fails in practice. Variants commonly affect more than one trait. If a variant used as an instrument for LDL also influences blood pressure, its association with heart disease reflects both pathways and the ratio is biased. This is horizontal pleiotropy.

(When a variant affects a second trait because that trait lies downstream of the exposure, that is vertical pleiotropy. It is the causal pathway under study and does no harm.)

IVW remains unbiased if pleiotropic effects are scattered in both directions and average to zero, though the scatter increases uncertainty. It is biased when they tend in one direction. Because the assumption cannot be verified, the standard practice is to apply several estimators, each valid under a different assumption about pleiotropy, and to trust a conclusion only when they agree.

Heterogeneity: if all instruments are valid, their ratios should agree within sampling error; Cochran's Q tests this, and methods such as MR-PRESSO identify and remove outliers. An MR-Egger intercept significantly different from zero indicates directional pleiotropy.

Other threats

Weak instruments. If the variants explain little variance in the exposure, the estimate is unstable and biased. Strength is measured by the F-statistic of the variant–exposure regression, with \(F > 10\) the conventional minimum. In two-sample MR with separate samples, weak instruments bias the estimate toward zero. When the samples overlap, the bias is toward the confounded observational association, which is the more dangerous direction.

Winner's curse. Selecting instruments and estimating their exposure effects in the same GWAS overstates \(\beta_{GX}\) (Chapter 14) and biases the ratio toward zero. Using separate datasets for selection and estimation avoids this.

Reverse causation at the level of instruments. If the outcome affects the exposure, variants that really act on the outcome will appear in the exposure GWAS. Steiger filtering removes variants that explain more variance in the outcome than in the exposure, and bidirectional MR runs the analysis both ways.

Population-level confounding. Stratification, assortative mating and the effects of parental genotype on the family environment all violate assumption 2, since they associate a person's genotype with their environment. For biomarkers this is usually negligible. For behavioural and social exposures it can be large, and the remedy is MR within families (Chapter 23).

Selection bias. If the exposure or outcome influences who is in the study, or who survives long enough to be studied, collider bias (Chapter 15) can induce an association.

Extensions

Multivariable MR

Many exposures are genetically entangled. Variants that affect LDL cholesterol often affect triglycerides and HDL as well, so single-exposure MR cannot say which lipid matters. Multivariable MR regresses the outcome effects on the effects for several exposures at once and estimates the direct effect of each, holding the others fixed. Applied to lipids, it finds that the apparent protective effect of HDL largely vanishes once LDL and triglycerides are accounted for, and that apolipoprotein B, which counts atherogenic particles, carries most of the causal signal attributed to LDL and triglycerides. The method needs instruments that distinguish the exposures; when exposures are too highly correlated genetically, conditional instrument strength collapses and they cannot be separated.

Drug-target (cis) MR

Variants within or near the gene encoding a drug's target, which alter that protein's level or activity, are natural models of the drug. Their biology is well understood, so the exclusion restriction is more believable than for a variant chosen genome-wide. Variants in HMGCR mimic statins, and variants in PCSK9 mimic PCSK9 inhibitors. Such cis-MR predicts whether a drug will work, and also its on-target side effects: HMGCR variants are associated with slightly higher body weight and type 2 diabetes risk, which showed that the small excess of diabetes seen in statin trials is an effect of inhibiting the target itself. Because the variants in a single gene region are in LD, these analyses must model their correlation and should be paired with colocalization (Chapter 18).

What MR has shown

  • LDL cholesterol causes coronary artery disease. Dozens of independent variants, acting through different genes, all raise risk in proportion to how much they raise LDL.
  • HDL cholesterol, as measured, probably does not protect. Variants that specifically raise HDL do not lower risk, in agreement with the failed drug trials.
  • Lipoprotein(a) is causal for coronary disease over a lifetime, which motivated drugs to lower it. The first large outcome trial of one, reported in 2026, found no benefit in patients with established disease (Chapter 28).
  • C-reactive protein is a marker and not a cause of heart disease, while signalling through the interleukin-6 receptor upstream of it is causal.
  • Higher body-mass index causes type 2 diabetes, coronary disease and many other conditions.

Interpreting the size of an MR estimate

An MR estimate is the effect of a small, lifelong difference in the exposure. That is not the same as the effect of changing the exposure with a drug for five years in middle age. For LDL and coronary disease, the genetic estimate per unit of LDL is about three times larger than the estimate from statin trials, consistent with atherosclerosis reflecting cumulative lifetime exposure. MR is best at establishing whether an effect exists and its direction. Its estimates of magnitude should be translated to interventions with care.

Because two-sample MR is so easy to run, the literature contains many analyses whose assumptions were never seriously examined. A credible study justifies each assumption for its particular instruments, reports several estimators and their heterogeneity, checks instrument strength and direction, and looks for supporting evidence from other study designs.

Key points

  • Alleles are assigned at random at conception, so a variant affecting an exposure can serve as an instrument for it.
  • Three assumptions: relevance, independence from confounders, and no path to the outcome except through the exposure.
  • Wald ratio \(\hat\theta = \hat\beta_{GY}/\hat\beta_{GX}\); IVW combines many instruments.
  • Horizontal pleiotropy is the main threat; MR-Egger, weighted median and mode estimators relax the assumption in different ways and should agree.
  • Multivariable MR separates correlated exposures; cis-MR models drug targets. MR estimates lifelong effects.

Families, assortative mating and what genetic associations mean

A GWAS effect is a correlation between a variant and a trait across a population. This chapter, the last of Part IV, asks what that correlation is made of, shows how data from families take it apart, and closes with the questions worth asking of any genetic finding.

Three things inside a population association

When a variant is associated with a trait in a sample of unrelated people, after the usual adjustments, the estimated effect can contain three components.

  1. Direct genetic effects. The variant, or one in LD with it, changes the trait in the person who carries it. This is what "genetic effect" is usually taken to mean.
  2. Indirect genetic effects. The genotypes of relatives, parents above all, affect the person's trait through the environment those relatives provide. A child shares half of each parent's alleles, so the child's genotype is correlated with the parents' genotypes and therefore with the environment the parents create.
  3. Confounding. Residual population stratification and the consequences of assortative mating, both of which correlate a person's genotype with other causes of the trait.

The randomness within families

Given the parents' genotypes, which alleles a child receives is decided by the coin toss of meiosis. Two siblings have the same parents, grow up in the same home and have the same ancestry. They differ genetically only because each received a different random draw. So the association between differences in sibling genotypes and differences in sibling traits cannot be due to stratification, to parental genotype, or to anything else the siblings share. It estimates the direct effect alone.

Three designs use this.

  • Sibling GWAS. Regress the trait on each person's genotype expressed as a deviation from the average of their sibship.
  • Trio designs. With both parents genotyped, include the parental genotypes as covariates. The coefficient on the child's genotype is the direct effect, and the coefficients on the parents estimate indirect effects.
  • Non-transmitted alleles. Build a polygenic score from the alleles each parent did not pass on. The child does not carry them, so any association between that score and the child's trait must run through the parents. This is sometimes called genetic nurture.

The cost is power. Differences between siblings have half the genetic variance of the population, and family data are scarcer, so a within-family study needs several times as many people as a population study for the same precision.

What within-family studies have found

The largest sibling GWAS to date compared population and within-sibship effect estimates across many traits. For most physiological and biochemical traits, including LDL cholesterol, blood pressure and body-mass index, the two agree closely: population estimates for these traits are essentially direct effects. For height the within-family estimates are slightly smaller. For educational attainment, age at first birth and depressive symptoms, the within-family estimates are about half as large. For cognitive ability and smoking they are about a fifth smaller.

The same pattern appears in every downstream quantity. For socially patterned traits, within-family analyses give lower heritability, weaker polygenic prediction, smaller genetic correlations with other traits, and smaller or absent Mendelian randomization effects. The general lesson is that the further a trait lies from molecular biology and the more it depends on family and social circumstances, the larger the share of its population-level genetic signal that is not direct.

Assortative mating

People choose partners who resemble them. The correlation between spouses is about 0.2–0.3 for height and higher for educational attainment. When the trait is heritable, this assortative mating has genetic consequences that build up over generations.

Tall people tend to have children with tall people, so their children inherit height-increasing alleles from both sides. Across the population, height-increasing alleles at different loci, even on different chromosomes, become positively correlated with one another. This is linkage disequilibrium without linkage, created by mate choice rather than by drift or physical proximity. Three consequences follow.

Genetic variance increases. When trait-increasing alleles cluster in the same individuals, the extremes become more extreme. At equilibrium the additive variance is inflated by a factor of roughly \(1/(1 - \rho h^2)\), where \(\rho\) is the correlation between mates and \(h^2\) the equilibrium heritability. For height that is an increase of about 20–25%.

Estimates built on random-mating assumptions are biased. Each variant's GWAS effect absorbs a little of the effects of all the other variants it has become correlated with. Polygenic scores then predict better than direct effects alone would allow, and heritability estimates shift in directions that depend on the method. In twin studies, assortative mating makes fraternal twins more alike than random mating would, which pushes the heritability estimate down and the shared-environment estimate up.

Cross-trait assortative mating creates genetic correlations. If taller people tend to partner with more educated people, alleles for height and alleles for education become correlated in the next generation, even if no variant affects both. Analyses suggest that a substantial part of the genetic correlations reported between some pairs of traits, particularly among psychiatric and behavioural traits, could arise this way and not from shared biology. Mendelian randomization inherits the same bias.

Within-family designs are again the remedy, since the alleles a child receives are random draws from the parents whatever the reason the parents came together.

Two more biases worth knowing

Who takes part. Biobank participants are volunteers. In UK Biobank they are, on average, healthier, better educated and less deprived than the population they were drawn from. Participation is itself partly heritable. When both an exposure and an outcome affect the chance of taking part, they become correlated among participants (collider bias). Effects on genetic associations are generally modest but measurable, and they matter most for traits linked to health behaviour and social position.

Who survives. Studies of older adults, or of disease progression among people who already have a disease, are restricted to those who lived long enough and, in the second case, who developed the disease. A variant that raises the risk of getting a disease can appear to protect against progressing with it, purely because of that selection.

Questions to ask of any genetic finding

The book's argument can be compressed into a checklist. For any claim that a variant, gene or score is linked to a trait, ask:

  1. Is the association real? Was the threshold appropriate for the number of tests, and has it replicated? (Chapter 14)
  2. Is it confounded? How was ancestry handled? What is the LD score intercept? Is there a within-family estimate? (Chapters 15, 17, 23)
  3. Which variant? Is the reported variant the lead SNP or a member of a credible set? How large is the set? (Chapter 18)
  4. Which gene, in which tissue? Is the assignment based on proximity, or on colocalization, coding variants, or rare-variant evidence? (Chapters 19, 20)
  5. Is it causal for the outcome of interest? A genetic correlation is not a causal effect. Do MR estimates from several methods agree, and are the instruments plausible? (Chapters 17, 22)
  6. In whom? Which populations were studied? Does the result, especially a polygenic score, transfer to others? (Chapters 9, 10, 21)
  7. How big is it? Statistical significance in half a million people is compatible with an effect of no practical consequence. What is the effect in absolute terms? (Chapters 14, 21)
  8. What does it not show? Heritability is not destiny, an association is not a mechanism, and a difference between individuals within a group says nothing about differences between groups. (Chapters 13, 21)

Where the field is going

Several directions are clear enough to name. Whole-genome sequencing of very large cohorts is bringing rare and structural variants into routine analysis. Studies are slowly becoming more diverse, which improves fine-mapping and makes prediction fairer. Single-cell and proteomic data are filling in the middle of the path from variant to trait. Genealogies inferred for hundreds of thousands of genomes are joining population genetics and association analysis into one framework, in which a variant's age, history and effect are studied together. And family-based designs, once limited by sample size, are becoming large enough to give estimates of direct genetic effects for many traits.

What ties these together is the theme of this book. The data analysed by statistical genetics were produced by mutation, drift, recombination, selection, migration and mate choice. Methods that take that history into account work, and the ones that ignore it fail in ways the population genetics predicts.

Key points

  • A population GWAS effect combines direct effects, indirect effects of relatives, and confounding.
  • Mendelian segregation is random within families, so sibling and trio designs isolate direct effects, at a cost in power.
  • Population and within-family estimates agree for most biomarkers and diverge for socially patterned traits.
  • Assortative mating correlates trait-increasing alleles across the genome, inflating genetic variance and creating spurious genetic correlations between traits.
  • Any genetic finding should be questioned on its reality, confounding, causal variant, causal gene, causal interpretation, generalisability and size.

Why genetics finds drug targets

Most drugs that enter human trials fail, and the usual reason is that the molecule they act on turns out not to matter for the disease. Human genetics offers evidence about that question in people, before any drug exists. This chapter explains what kind of evidence it is, why it raises the odds of success, and where it stops.

What a drug target is

A drug works by binding a particular molecule in the body and changing what it does. That molecule is the drug's target. Most targets are proteins: enzymes, receptors, ion channels, transporters, or proteins secreted into the blood. Newer kinds of medicine act one step earlier. Antisense oligonucleotides and small interfering RNAs destroy a gene's messenger RNA, and gene editing changes the DNA itself, but the logic is the same: the drug changes the amount or activity of one gene product.

A drug's mechanism is its target together with the direction of change: inhibiting an enzyme, blocking a receptor, activating one, replacing a missing protein. Target discovery is the choice of mechanism and of the patients it is for. It is made at the start of a programme, years before anyone knows whether the drug works, and almost everything spent afterwards depends on it.

Why most programmes fail

Of the drugs that enter the first human trials, only about one in ten is eventually approved. Most of the losses happen in phase II, the first test of whether the drug helps patients, and in phase III, the large confirmatory trials. Surveys of these failures find that the commonest single reason is lack of efficacy, followed by safety.

A failure of efficacy in phase II is usually not a failure of chemistry. By then the drug is known to reach its target and to change it. The problem is that changing the target does not change the disease in people. The evidence that led to the target came from cells, from animals, or from observational associations in patients, and each of those can mislead. Cells lack the rest of the body. Animal diseases are imitations. And an observational association between a protein's level and a disease can arise because the disease changes the protein, or because both depend on something else (Chapter 22).

Genetic support and success

Human genetics avoids the first two problems because it is about humans, and largely avoids the third because genotypes are fixed at conception. A variant that changes a gene's activity and is associated with a disease is evidence that changing that gene changes the disease, in the species and the condition that matter.

Whether this matters in practice has been tested retrospectively. In 2015, Matthew Nelson and colleagues at GlaxoSmithKline matched the targets and indications of drugs in development against genetic associations between the same genes and similar diseases. The share of drug mechanisms with genetic support rose at every stage of development, from about 2% of preclinical programmes to about 8% of approved drugs. They estimated that choosing genetically supported targets could double the chance of success.

Later analyses with more data have reached similar conclusions. The largest, by Eric Minikel and colleagues in 2024, estimated that programmes with human genetic support are about 2.6 times as likely to progress from phase I to approval. The advantage was larger when the gene responsible for the genetic association was identified with more confidence, and it varied between therapeutic areas. It was largely unaffected by the size of the genetic effect or the frequency of the variant, which is consistent with the point made below that a small association can mark a good target.

What these studies do and do not show

The analyses are observational. "Genetic support" in them usually means that the target gene has been associated with the disease in a GWAS or a Mendelian disorder. The larger estimates apply to cases where the causal gene is clear, such as Mendelian traits and associations driven by coding variants. It does not mean that a full Mendelian randomization or experimental programme was done. The finding is that such support predicts success, not that it guarantees it, and some of the effect may come from genetic support tending to mark biology that is better understood in general. The direction of the result has nonetheless been consistent across companies, datasets and methods.

The single most useful fact genetics can supply, after the identity of the gene, is the direction of effect. It decides what kind of drug is needed.

Making a molecule that blocks a protein is far easier than making one that increases its activity, so protective loss of function is the most valuable kind of genetic finding. The PCSK9 case in Chapter 20 is the model. Activators can be made for some targets, notably receptors whose natural ligands can be imitated. Common variants in the receptor for the hormone GLP-1, for example, show that genetically higher receptor activity is associated with lower blood glucose and less type 2 diabetes, consistent with the benefit of the GLP-1 receptor agonists now widely used.

The modality also depends on where the target is. Secreted and cell-surface proteins can be reached by antibodies. Proteins made in the liver can be silenced by oligonucleotides, which liver cells take up readily. Intracellular enzymes generally need small molecules. A genetically compelling target that cannot be reached by any modality is not yet a drug target.

Dose and the allelic series

A drug rarely abolishes its target. It reduces activity by some fraction, for some hours of the day, from some age. To predict what a drug will do, one wants to know how the disease responds across a range of reductions.

Genetics can supply that through an allelic series: several variants in the same gene with different effects on its function. Common regulatory variants change activity slightly. Missense variants change it more. A heterozygous loss-of-function variant halves it. Rare people with two loss-of-function alleles have none. If the effects on a biomarker or a disease line up with the effects on the gene, the result is a dose–response curve obtained in humans.

An allelic series does three things. It strengthens the case that the gene is causal, because a graded response is hard to explain otherwise. It predicts the size of effect for a given degree of inhibition. And it shows whether there is a threshold or a plateau: whether, for instance, halving activity achieves most of the benefit.

The size of a GWAS effect is not the size of a drug effect

Common variants in HMGCR, the target of statins, have small effects on LDL cholesterol, a few percent at most. Statins lower it by a third or more. A common variant typically changes its gene's activity slightly, so its small effect on disease says that the gene matters, not that it matters only a little. What a drug can achieve depends on how strongly it changes the target. This is why a modest GWAS signal at a well-chosen gene can be worth more than a large one at a gene that cannot be drugged.

Safety: what carriers show

A person who has carried a loss-of-function variant for their whole life is a long-term safety study of the corresponding inhibitor. Two kinds of observation are useful.

Healthy knockouts. Heterozygous loss-of-function carriers are common enough to study in biobanks. People with loss of both copies are rare, except in populations where parents are often related, which makes long stretches of the genome homozygous (Chapter 10). Sequencing studies in such populations, notably in Pakistan and among British people of Pakistani heritage, have found adults with complete loss of hundreds of genes. If people lacking a gene entirely are healthy, a drug that inhibits it is unlikely to cause harm through that gene. One such study, of about 10,500 people in Pakistan, found complete knockouts of more than 1,300 genes. Participants with no APOC3 were recruited and given a fatty meal; the usual rise in blood triglycerides afterwards was blunted, which is the effect a drug against that target should have.

Effects on other traits. A target's genetic variants can be tested against every disease and measurement recorded in a biobank, a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS). Associations other than the intended one are candidate on-target side effects. The next chapter shows how this is done with Mendelian randomization, and how it anticipated the small increase in diabetes risk seen with statins.

Constraint is a weak guide to safety

Chapter 20 introduced constraint: genes in which loss-of-function variants are much rarer than expected, because losing one copy reduces fitness. It is tempting to treat a constrained gene as unsafe to inhibit. In practice the relationship is weak. Many established drug targets are constrained. Fitness costs can come from effects in development that a drug given in adulthood would not reproduce, and drugs usually reduce activity partly and reversibly. Constraint is a reason to look closely at what loss of the gene does, not a reason to abandon it.

Where genetic evidence stops

  • Lifelong is not late. A variant acts from conception. A drug starts in adulthood, often after disease has begun. For LDL cholesterol the lifelong effect per unit is about three times the effect of five years of treatment. For some exposures, late intervention may do little: the first outcome trial of lipoprotein(a) lowering, reported in 2026, found no benefit despite strong genetic evidence for lifelong exposure.
  • Onset is not progression. Most GWAS compare people with and without a disease. Many drugs are given to people who already have it, to slow it. The genes that influence whether a disease starts need not be those that influence how it progresses, and studying progression among cases introduces its own selection bias.
  • Development is not maintenance. A gene may matter during growth and not afterwards, or the body may compensate for lifelong loss in ways it cannot for sudden inhibition.
  • Some targets are invisible. Genes so constrained that damaging variants are vanishingly rare, or genes with redundant partners, may show no genetic signal even if a drug against them would work.
  • Evidence comes mostly from European-ancestry cohorts. Variants that would reveal a target may be common only in populations that have been studied little, as the PCSK9 variants found in African Americans showed.

The next four chapters follow a target from a GWAS signal to a decision. Chapters 26 and 27 turn to experimental data: Perturb-seq, which measures what a gene does to the expression of every other gene in a cell, and the ways such maps are joined to GWAS to find the pathways that loci act through. Key points

  • A target is the molecule a drug changes; a mechanism is the target plus the direction of change. Most clinical failures are failures of efficacy, which usually means the wrong target.
  • Drug programmes with human genetic support are roughly twice as likely, or more, to reach approval, especially when the causal gene is confidently identified.
  • Genetics can indicate the causal gene, the direction of effect, the dose–response (allelic series), safety (healthy knockouts, PheWAS), and the tissue.
  • Protective loss of function is the most useful finding because inhibitors are the easiest drugs to make.
  • A small common-variant effect does not imply a small drug effect.
  • Genetic evidence reflects lifelong exposure and disease onset; drugs act late and often on progression.

Testing a target with Mendelian randomization

Chapter 22 used Mendelian randomization to ask whether an exposure causes a disease. A drug developer asks something narrower: if this one protein is changed, does the disease change, and what else changes with it? Using variants in the target's own gene answers that question, provided some particular traps are avoided.

From exposures to mechanisms

Genome-wide MR asks, for example, whether LDL cholesterol causes coronary disease. It uses variants from many genes that all affect LDL, and treats them as interchangeable. A drug does not lower "LDL"; it inhibits one protein. Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, ezetimibe blocks the intestinal transporter NPC1L1, and the PCSK9 antibodies block PCSK9. Each lowers LDL, and each could in principle have other effects that depend on its mechanism.

Drug-target MR, also called cis-MR, uses only variants in or near the gene that encodes the target. Those variants act, in the first instance, on that gene's product. They therefore model the drug's mechanism, not the biomarker in general, and they bring two advantages. The biology of the instrument is clear, which makes the exclusion restriction (Chapter 22) easier to defend. And their associations with other traits are candidate effects of the drug itself, which is what a safety assessment needs.

What should the instrument be associated with?

A cis-MR analysis needs variants that change the target and a measure of how much they change it. There are three common choices, at successive steps along the chain from gene to disease.

A downstream biomarker. If the target's main effect is known and measurable, use variants in the gene that are associated with that biomarker, weighted by their effect on it. Variants in HMGCR associated with LDL cholesterol are the standard example. The answer comes in clinical units, such as risk per mmol/L of LDL, which can be compared directly with trials. The approach needs a biomarker that captures the mechanism, which exists for few targets.

Protein level. Variants associated with the amount of the target protein in blood, cis-pQTLs, measure the target itself. Plasma proteomics now provides them for thousands of proteins (below). Their limitation is that plasma is not where most proteins act, and the level of a protein is not its activity.

Gene expression. cis-eQTLs exist for nearly every gene. But the relevant tissue or cell state may not have been sampled, and Chapter 19 explained why eQTLs at important disease genes are often small or absent.

A fourth source of evidence is not a ratio estimate at all: the direction and size of effect in carriers of rare loss-of-function variants (Chapter 20). It is the most direct model of an inhibitor, and agreement between it and a cis-MR estimate is strong support.

Plasma proteomics

Measuring thousands of proteins in tens of thousands of blood samples became possible with affinity-based assays. One platform uses aptamers, short modified DNA molecules selected to bind a protein. Another uses pairs of antibodies that, when both bind the same protein, bring two DNA tags together so that they can be amplified and counted. Large biobank studies using these platforms have mapped cis-pQTLs for a large share of the proteins measured, and their summary statistics are public. They are the main source of instruments for drug-target MR today.

Two problems are specific to these data.

  • Epitope effects. An aptamer or antibody binds a particular surface of the protein. A missense variant that changes that surface can reduce binding without changing how much protein there is. The assay reports a lower level, and the variant looks like a strong pQTL. Warning signs are a pQTL driven by a protein-altering variant, and disagreement between platforms that use different reagents.
  • Platform disagreement. The same protein measured by the two main platforms sometimes gives cis-pQTLs that differ in strength or even direction. For some proteins the two methods may be detecting different forms or complexes. An MR result that depends on one platform's instrument and is absent with the other deserves suspicion until checked by an independent method such as mass spectrometry.

Instruments in one gene are correlated

Genome-wide MR uses many independent variants. A cis analysis uses one region, where variants are in linkage disequilibrium with one another. There are three ways to handle this.

  1. Use only the lead variant and compute a single Wald ratio. Simple, but it wastes information and gives no check of heterogeneity.
  2. Prune to variants with low mutual correlation. Common, but residual correlation makes the standard errors too small if it is ignored.
  3. Keep several correlated variants and model their correlation. The inverse-variance weighted estimator of Chapter 22 generalises to
\[ \hat\theta = \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_X^{\top}\,\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_Y}{\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_X^{\top}\,\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_X}, \qquad \Omega_{jk} = \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_{Y,j})\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_{Y,k})\,\rho_{jk}, \]

where \(\rho_{jk}\) is the LD correlation between variants \(j\) and \(k\). When the variants are independent, \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) is diagonal and this reduces to the ordinary IVW estimate. The correlations come from an LD reference panel, and, as with fine-mapping (Chapter 18), a panel that does not match the GWAS sample can give confident, wrong answers.

Cis instruments for protein levels are often very strong, with F-statistics in the hundreds or thousands, so weak-instrument bias is less of a concern than in genome-wide MR. The bigger danger lies elsewhere.

Confounding by linkage disequilibrium

Suppose the variant that changes the protein and a variant that changes disease risk are different, but lie near each other and are partly correlated. The disease variant might act through a neighbouring gene. The lead pQTL variant will then show an association with disease through LD alone, and its Wald ratio will suggest a causal effect of the protein that does not exist. This is the most common way for cis-MR to mislead, and gene-dense regions are especially prone to it.

The figure shows the two defences. The first is colocalization (Chapter 18; compare ): test whether the protein signal and the disease signal share a causal variant. A cis-MR result is credible only with good evidence for a shared variant, and a high posterior probability of distinct variants is a reason to discard it. The second is heterogeneity: if several variants in the region change the protein by different amounts, they should all give the same ratio. Where a gene has more than one independent pQTL signal, each is an independent test, a small allelic series inside the MR.

A further check is to look at the neighbours. If the instruments are also eQTLs for adjacent genes in a relevant tissue, the MR cannot by itself say which gene is responsible.

Positive controls

Before trusting a genetic proxy for a new target, one can ask whether the same approach recovers what is already known about established drugs. Proxies for HMG-CoA reductase and PCSK9 inhibition should lower LDL cholesterol and coronary risk. Proxies for blood-pressure-lowering drug targets should lower blood pressure and stroke risk. A proxy for IL-6 receptor blockade should raise circulating IL-6 and lower C-reactive protein, as the antibody tocilizumab does. If a method cannot reproduce such known effects, its results for an unknown target carry little weight. If it can, the same pipeline applied to the new target has earned some trust.

From a ratio to a drug

An MR estimate is an effect per unit of the exposure. To predict a drug's effect, multiply by how much the drug changes the exposure. If the estimate is 0.4 log odds per standard deviation of a protein, and a drug lowers the protein by one standard deviation, the predicted odds ratio is \(e^{-0.4} \approx 0.67\).

That prediction carries every caveat about lifelong exposure from Chapter 24. Its more reliable use is comparative: two candidate targets can be compared on the same scale, the reduction in a protein needed to match an existing therapy can be calculated, and a trial can be sized. The Lp(a) calculation that suggested about 100 mg/dL of lowering would be needed to match 1 mmol/L of LDL lowering is an example of this kind of reasoning.

Side effects and new uses: phenome-wide MR

The instrument for a target can be tested against every outcome in a biobank, giving a phenome-wide MR. The results fall into three groups: the intended effect, candidate adverse effects, and candidate new indications.

The best-known example is HMGCR. Variants that mimic statins are associated with slightly higher body weight and a slightly higher risk of type 2 diabetes. Statin trials found a small increase in new diabetes. The genetic finding showed that this is a consequence of inhibiting the target, not of particular molecules, and that it is small compared with the reduction in heart attacks. Phenome-wide scans of PCSK9 variants found a similar, smaller association with diabetes.

Each signal from such a scan needs the same care as the primary result. The number of tests is large, so a multiple-testing correction is required. Each association should colocalize with the protein signal, for the reason shown in . And a genetic association with lifelong lower activity can reflect development, not something a drug started in adulthood would cause.

Pitfalls particular to drug-target MR

  • Level is not activity. A variant may raise the amount of a protein while reducing its function, or alter its form. A drug that inhibits activity may not be modelled by variants that change level.
  • The sampled tissue may be the wrong one. A protein measured in plasma may be made in many tissues, and the one relevant to disease may contribute little to the plasma level.
  • Feedback. Some proteins are regulated by their own pathway. Statins raise plasma PCSK9, for instance, because both are switched on when liver cells are low in cholesterol.
  • Gain and loss of function are not symmetrical. A missense variant that makes a protein hyperactive does not necessarily mirror what an inhibitor does in reverse.
  • Population and LD. Instruments chosen in one ancestry may tag the causal variant poorly in another, and LD panels must match.
  • Sample overlap and selection apply as in any MR (Chapter 22), and studies of disease progression in patients add collider bias.

Published guidance for drug-target MR asks for a clear statement of which gene region and exposure were used, evidence of colocalization, positive controls, and analyses of the outcomes that matter for safety.

Key points

  • Drug-target (cis) MR uses variants in the target's own gene to model a specific mechanism, not a biomarker in general.
  • The exposure can be a downstream biomarker, the protein level (pQTL) or gene expression (eQTL), each with different strengths.
  • Plasma proteomics supplies cis-pQTLs for thousands of proteins; epitope effects and platform disagreement can create false instruments.
  • Variants in one region are correlated; generalised IVW models the LD, and needs an accurate LD reference.
  • The main danger is a distinct but linked disease variant. Colocalization and consistency across variants are the defences.
  • Positive controls test the pipeline; phenome-wide MR anticipates side effects and new uses.

Perturb-seq: what a gene does in a cell

Human genetics can show that a gene affects a disease. It seldom shows what the gene does inside a cell to bring that about. Perturb-seq supplies a different kind of data: the expression of every gene, measured in single cells in which one chosen gene has been turned down. This chapter describes the structure of those data and the statistics used to read them.

The layer GWAS does not see

By the end of Chapter 25, a locus has become a gene, with a direction of effect and an estimate of how much the disease changes when the gene's product changes. For the lipid genes that is nearly a complete account, because their biochemistry was already known. For most genes found by GWAS it is not. Many are transcription factors, signalling proteins, or genes with no described function. Knowing that such a gene raises disease risk says nothing about the pathway it works through, and nothing about whether two genes at two different loci are parts of the same mechanism.

What is missing is a map of downstream effects: when the activity of gene A changes, which other genes change their expression? Natural variation contains that information in principle. A variant that changes the expression of gene A in cis will also shift, slightly, every gene that A regulates, and those shifts are trans-eQTLs (Chapter 19). In practice trans-eQTLs are very hard to find. The cis effect of a common variant is small, the downstream effects are a fraction of it, and tens of thousands of samples of one tissue are needed to detect even the strongest. The largest study in blood, of 31,684 people, found a cis-eQTL for 88% of genes, but a trans-eQTL for only 37% of the roughly 10,000 trait-associated variants it tested. For genes under selective constraint, common variants with useful cis effects may not exist at all.

The alternative is to stop waiting for natural variants and change the gene deliberately, by a large amount, in cells grown in the laboratory. Perturb-seq does this for thousands of genes in one experiment.

The design in outline

This book is about analysis, so the laboratory side is described only as far as is needed to understand the data. The reviews listed at the end cover it properly.

  • The experiment starts with a large population of cells of one type.
  • Each cell receives a guide: a short sequence that directs a perturbation to one chosen gene. In most large experiments the perturbation is a knockdown, which lowers the expression of the target gene. Designs that inactivate a gene completely, or raise its expression, are also used.
  • Guides are distributed at random, so which gene is perturbed in which cell is a matter of chance. A share of cells receive non-targeting control guides that perturb nothing.
  • After several days, each cell is profiled by single-cell RNA sequencing. The sequencing captures two things from the same cell: the number of transcripts of every gene, and the identity of the guide the cell carries.

The result is that every cell comes with a label saying which gene was perturbed in it, and all perturbations were made in one pooled culture under identical conditions. Because the guide was assigned at random, the comparison between cells carrying a given guide and control cells has the logic of a randomised trial carried out among cells. The parallel with Mendelian randomization is close: there, nature assigns alleles at random; here, the experimenter assigns perturbations at random.

Scale

The approach was described in 2016 and 2017 in a group of papers, under the names Perturb-seq, CRISP-seq and CROP-seq. One of the first profiled about 200,000 cells and concentrated on transcription factors in an immune response. By 2022 a single study had perturbed every expressed gene in cultured human cells and profiled more than 2.5 million of them. In 2026 the same was reported for primary human T cells from four blood donors, at rest and after stimulation, with about 22 million cells. The data from the large experiments are public, and a growing share of the work in this area is re-analysis.

Estimating a perturbation's effect

For a perturbed gene \(g\) and a measured gene \(j\), the basic quantity is a log fold change,

\[ \hat\beta_{gj} = \log \frac{\text{mean expression of } j \text{ in cells where } g \text{ is perturbed}}{\text{mean expression of } j \text{ in control cells}}, \]

with a standard error and a test statistic. Collected over all pairs these form the matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) of . In practice the estimate comes from a regression model for counts, often after adding up the cells of each perturbation within each replicate (a "pseudobulk" analysis). A reader who has followed the GWAS chapters will recognise each of the issues that arise.

Did the perturbation work? The first check is the effect of each guide on its own target, the cis effect. Guides differ in efficiency, and some cells that carry a guide show little or no knockdown. If those cells are left in, every downstream effect is diluted. Analyses therefore report knockdown efficiency per guide, and some fit a mixture model to separate the cells that responded from those that did not.

Calibration. Single-cell counts are sparse and more variable than simple models assume, and cells differ in how deeply they were sequenced. Sequencing depth is a particular problem, because it affects both the measured expression of a cell and the chance that its guide is detected, which makes it a confounder. Naive tests can be badly miscalibrated in either direction. The non-targeting control guides provide a check that has no equivalent in GWAS: split the control cells into arbitrary groups, test them against one another, and every result is a null by construction. A quantile–quantile plot of those tests (Chapter 15) shows directly whether the method is calibrated, and resampling-based tests have been developed to restore calibration where it fails.

Covariates. Cell-cycle stage, sequencing batch, the donor a cell came from, and the number of guides in a cell all affect expression. They play the part that ancestry and batch play in a GWAS. Random assignment of guides protects against confounding on average, but adjusting for these covariates improves precision.

Multiple testing. Ten thousand perturbations tested against ten thousand genes is \(10^8\) tests. Control of the false discovery rate is standard, and so is restricting attention to summaries with fewer dimensions, as described below.

Agreement between guides. A guide can occasionally affect a gene other than its intended target. Using two or more guides per gene and requiring that their effects agree is the usual protection, and it is the analogue of replication.

Power

How small an effect can be seen? Put each gene's expression on a scale where its cell-to-cell standard deviation is 1. With \(n\) perturbed cells and a much larger number of control cells, the standard error of the difference in means is about \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so the smallest shift detectable with 80% power at significance threshold \(\alpha\) is roughly

\[ \delta_{\min} \approx \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2} + 0.84}{\sqrt{n}} . \]

With around a hundred cells per perturbation, which is typical, only shifts of about half a standard deviation in a single gene are detectable. Many true effects are smaller. This is the single-cell version of the lesson of Chapter 14: most effects are small, and a study of fixed size sees the largest. Two consequences follow. A perturbation with "no significant effects" has not been shown to do nothing. One analysis that modelled the distribution of true effects, instead of counting significant ones, estimated that a typical perturbation changes the expression of about 45 genes, and a typical essential gene more than 500. And averaging over groups of genes that respond together recovers much of the lost power, which leads to the idea of a program.

From a hundred million numbers to programs

The matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) is large and noisy, and also highly structured. Genes do not respond one at a time. Genes for ribosomal proteins move together, as do the enzymes of cholesterol synthesis, the genes switched on by interferon, and the genes of each phase of the cell cycle. A gene program is a set of genes whose expression rises and falls together, usually because they share regulators.

Programs are found by matrix factorisation. The expression data, or the effect matrix, are approximated as a product of two smaller matrices,

\[ \mathbf{B} \approx \mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{H}, \]

where \(\mathbf{H}\) has one row per program, giving the weight of each gene in it, and \(\mathbf{W}\) has one row per perturbation, giving its effect on each program. Principal components analysis (Chapter 10) is one such factorisation. Non-negative matrix factorisation is often preferred because its programs are easier to interpret as sets of genes that are "on" together. A perturbation is then described by its effect on a few dozen or a few hundred programs instead of on ten thousand genes.

The figure shows two things that are read off such a map.

Regulators of a program are the perturbations with a large effect on it: the rows that light up a given block of columns. Their signs matter. If turning a gene down lowers a program, the gene normally activates it. If turning it down raises the program, the gene normally represses it.

Similar perturbations imply shared function. Two genes whose knockdown produces the same pattern across all measured genes very probably work together, as subunits of one complex or steps in one pathway. Clustering the rows of \(\mathbf{B}\) recovers known complexes with striking accuracy, and places genes of unknown function next to partners whose role is understood. For a GWAS gene with no described function, this is often the first real clue about what it does.

What the data do not capture

  • One cell type, in a dish, in one state. A gene's downstream effects depend on the cell type and on whether the cells are resting or stimulated. A map made in a convenient cell line may say little about a hepatocyte or a neuron.
  • RNA only. Changes in protein level, protein modification or metabolism are invisible unless they feed back on transcription. Versions of the method that also measure surface proteins or chromatin exist, at smaller scale.
  • Large perturbations. A knockdown removes most of a gene's expression. A common regulatory variant changes it by a few percent. If the response is not proportional to dose, the large perturbation misrepresents the small one.
  • Effects within the cell only. Whatever a gene does through other cell types, hormones or whole-body physiology is outside the experiment.
  • Essential genes. Cells in which a gene required for growth is strongly turned down are lost from the culture, so the cells that remain are those in which the perturbation worked least well.
  • Cost. The number of cells needed grows with the number of perturbations and with the smallness of the effects sought. Designs that pool several perturbations per measurement and separate them statistically have been reported to cut the cost by about an order of magnitude.

Key points

Any comparison among control cells is a true null. Testing arbitrary groups of control cells against each other should give uniformly distributed P-values, so a QQ plot of those tests shows whether the method is calibrated.

Selection removes common variants that change a constrained gene's expression much, so there is little natural variation to use. A perturbation is imposed by the experimenter and does not depend on such variants existing.

Joining perturbation data to GWAS

A perturbation map says what each gene does to a cell. A GWAS says which genes matter to a person. Neither answers the question a drug developer asks, which is through what mechanism a gene changes disease risk. Put together they can, and this chapter covers the three main ways of combining them, each with the assumptions it rests on.

A model with three layers

The working picture is a chain. A variant changes the expression of a nearby gene. That gene, directly or through others, changes the activity of one or more gene programs in a particular cell type. The activity of those programs changes the trait.

The model is a simplification. Genes act through several programs at once, programs differ between cell types, and some effects do not pass through transcription at all. It is useful because each link can be measured, and because it makes clear what has to be assumed when a link is not.

Use 1: from variant to gene

Chapter 19 inferred the target gene of a non-coding variant from indirect evidence: eQTLs, chromatin marks, physical contact with a promoter. A perturbation experiment can test the link directly. Instead of turning down a gene, the perturbation is aimed at a non-coding element, and the single-cell readout shows which nearby gene changes its expression as a result.

The first large experiment of this kind, by Molly Gasperini, Jay Shendure and colleagues, perturbed 5,920 candidate enhancers in about 250,000 cells and identified 664 enhancer–gene pairs. Experiments like it serve two purposes.

They provide ground truth for predictive models. Testing every element near every gene in every cell type is not feasible, so the experimental pairs are used to build and check rules that can be applied anywhere. The best known is the activity-by-contact (ABC) model. It scores the effect of element \(E\) on gene \(G\) as the element's activity (from chromatin accessibility and histone marks) multiplied by how often it touches the gene's promoter, as a share of the same quantity summed over all elements near the gene:

\[ \mathrm{ABC}_{E,G} = \frac{A_E \times C_{E,G}}{\sum_{e} A_e \times C_{e,G}} . \]

Only activity and contact maps are needed for a new cell type. ABC maps built for 131 cell types and tissues were used to connect about 5,000 GWAS signals to about 2,200 genes across 72 diseases and traits.

They can be aimed at GWAS variants themselves. One study perturbed non-coding GWAS loci for blood-cell traits and found target genes at 91 of them, 124 genes in all. Because the readout is the whole transcriptome, the same experiment also shows what changes further downstream. Where the target gene encoded a transcription factor, the network of genes it regulates was itself enriched for blood-trait variants.

Two cautions apply. Effects of single enhancers on their target genes are usually modest, so these experiments are short of power for the same reason eQTL studies are, and a negative result is weak evidence. And an element is active only in certain cell types, so the experiment has to be done in the right one.

Use 2: which programs does a trait act through?

Suppose the genes for a trait have been assigned at several hundred loci. The simplest use of a perturbation map is to ask whether those genes are concentrated in particular parts of it. Two kinds of gene set can be made for each program:

  • the program's member genes, those with high weight in it; and
  • its regulators, the genes whose perturbation changes it.

The test is whether the trait's genes, or more generally its heritability, fall in a set more often than expected. Gene-set tests compare gene-level association statistics inside and outside the set. Stratified LD score regression (Chapter 17) does the same at the level of SNPs, by treating the SNPs near a program's genes as an annotation and estimating the heritability they carry.

A study of coronary artery disease shows what this can deliver. Most loci for the disease are not explained by lipids or other known risk factors, and are thought to act in the artery wall. Researchers linked variants to candidate genes with epigenomic maps, then perturbed those genes in cultured endothelial cells and read out the effects on expression programs. Genes from 43 separate GWAS signals converged on one signalling pathway, known from a different disease, cerebral cavernous malformation, and previously unconnected to coronary disease genetics. No single locus pointed to that pathway. The convergence across loci did.

Enrichment has clear limits. It is a statement about sets, and says nothing certain about any one gene in the set. It inherits every error in the assignment of genes to loci. It depends on the comparison set: the right background is the genes expressed in that cell type, not all genes. And many programs are tested, so correction for multiple testing matters. Above all, enrichment in a program does not give the direction of the program's effect on the trait. For that, the signs of the effects have to be used.

Use 3: direction and size, by regression

The third approach uses the numbers and their signs, and has the same structure as Mendelian randomization.

Two quantities are available for many genes. From the perturbation map, \(\beta_{g,p}\) is the effect of turning down gene \(g\) on the activity of program \(p\). From rare-variant burden tests (Chapter 20), \(\gamma_g\) is the effect on the trait of carrying a loss-of-function variant in gene \(g\). Both describe what happens when the gene's activity is reduced. If a gene influences the trait by changing the activity of programs, then

\[ \gamma_g = \sum_{p} \beta_{g,p}\,\theta_p + \varepsilon_g , \]

where \(\theta_p\) is the effect of program \(p\) on the trait. The \(\gamma\)s and \(\beta\)s are measured. Regressing one on the other across genes estimates the \(\theta\)s.

Compare the inverse-variance weighted estimator of Chapter 22. There, each variant changed an exposure by \(\beta_{GX}\) and the outcome by \(\beta_{GY}\), and the slope of one on the other across variants estimated the causal effect of the exposure. Here each gene plays the part of an instrument, the exposure is the activity of a program, and the slope is the program's effect on the trait. With several programs fitted together the analysis corresponds to multivariable MR.

Mineto Ota, Jonathan Pritchard and colleagues applied this reasoning to three blood traits, joining loss-of-function burden estimates with Perturb-seq data from a relevant cell type. Their analysis links upstream regulators to programs and programs to traits in one causal graph, and so connects an association to a mechanism with a direction.

Rare-variant burden effects suit this purpose better than common-variant GWAS effects for two reasons. The gene is known, with no variant-to-gene step. And the direction is known: a loss-of-function variant reduces the gene's activity, as a knockdown does. For a common non-coding variant, neither is known without further data.

Choosing the cell type

All three uses depend on doing the experiment in cells where the trait's genetics act. That choice can itself be guided by genetics. Partitioned heritability (Chapter 17) identifies the cell types whose active regulatory regions carry a trait's heritability: T cells for many autoimmune diseases, liver for LDL cholesterol, particular neuronal types for psychiatric traits. A perturbation map is informative for a trait to the extent that it was made in, or in a good model of, that cell type and state.

This is why the move from cell lines to primary human cells matters. Cell lines grow without limit and are easy to perturb, but they are abnormal cells that may lack the programs of interest. Genome-scale maps in primary immune cells from several donors, under resting and stimulated conditions, are much closer to the cells in which immune-disease variants act. The first such map found that regulators and programs change strongly when the cells are stimulated, and that some of the pathways implicated in autoimmune disease risk appear only in one of the two states.

Alongside other kinds of study

eQTLs and colocalization. A colocalized cis-eQTL names a gene and the direction in which its expression moves with risk (Chapter 19). The perturbation map then shows what that change in expression does downstream. Where large blood cohorts have found trans-eQTLs for the same gene, they can be compared with the perturbation effects as a check that the cultured cells behave like cells in people.

Drug-target MR. Chapter 25 estimates how much a disease changes when a target changes. Perturbation data add what else changes in the cell. That helps in three ways: it gives a molecular signature that can be measured in early trials to confirm a drug is acting as expected, it flags programs whose disturbance might cause on-target side effects, and it shows when two candidate targets act on the same program and are therefore unlikely to add to each other.

Programs from tissue atlases. The enrichment test of use 2 does not need a perturbation experiment. Programs can also be defined from single-cell RNA-seq of human tissue and tested for heritability in the same way, which has pointed to specific cell types and processes for a range of diseases. Such programs come from the right cells in the right setting, but without perturbations they have no known regulators and no causal direction.

Single-cell atlases of patient tissue. A program defined in cultured cells can be scored in cells from diseased human tissue. If it is present there, in the expected cell type, and differs between patients and controls, the laboratory finding gains a great deal of weight. If it cannot be found, the map may not transfer.

Mendelian disease genes. A gene whose perturbation profile closely resembles that of a gene known to cause a single-gene disorder is a candidate for the same pathway, and sometimes for the same disorder.

Prediction of unseen perturbations. Machine-learning models are being trained on these maps to predict the effect of perturbations that have not been done, or of the same perturbation in another cell type. A 2025 benchmark of seven deep-learning models, five of them large foundation models, found that none outperformed deliberately simple baselines, so predictions of this kind are best treated as hypotheses to test.

Reading a claim that joins GWAS to perturbation data

Four questions sort strong claims from weak ones.

  1. How were genes assigned to loci? If by proximity alone, a share of the "GWAS genes" are wrong, and any enrichment is diluted or distorted.
  2. Is the cell type supported by the genetics? Was it chosen because heritability is enriched there, or because it was available?
  3. Is the result an enrichment or a signed effect? An enrichment says a program is involved. Only a signed analysis says which way to push it.
  4. Does it hold up outside the dish? Is the program seen in human tissue, and do independent data such as rare-variant effects or trans-eQTLs agree?

Key points

  • The working model is variant → gene → program → trait. GWAS and burden tests measure the total effect of a gene; perturbation data supply the gene-to-program link.
  • Perturbing non-coding elements tests variant-to-gene links directly and provides the ground truth for predictive models such as activity-by-contact.
  • Enrichment of a trait's genes or heritability in a program's members or regulators identifies the pathways involved, without a direction.
  • Regressing loss-of-function burden effects on perturbation effects across genes estimates each program's signed effect on the trait. It is Mendelian randomization with genes as instruments, and shares its assumptions.
  • Everything depends on using the cell type and state in which the trait's genetics act.

Genes from 40 GWAS loci for a disease all turn out to regulate one expression program in a relevant cell type. What does this add to what the GWAS showed?

The GWAS showed 40 separate regions. The perturbation map shows that they act on a common mechanism, which no single locus could reveal, and it identifies that mechanism as a candidate point of intervention.

Knocking down gene A lowers a program by 1 SD; loss of function in A raises a trait by 0.3 SD. Knocking down gene B raises the same program by 0.5 SD. If the program is what drives the trait, what should loss of function in B do?

The implied effect of the program on the trait is \(0.3/(-1) = -0.3\) SD per SD. For B, \(0.5 \times (-0.3) = -0.15\): loss of function in B should lower the trait by about 0.15 SD.

In the regression of burden effects on perturbation effects, what is the counterpart of horizontal pleiotropy?

A gene affecting the trait by a route other than the programs in the model, such as another cell type or a mechanism that does not change transcription. It biases the estimated program effects as pleiotropy biases an MR estimate.

Why are burden-test effects easier to combine with knockdown effects than common-variant GWAS effects are?

The gene is known and the direction is known: a loss-of-function variant reduces the gene's activity as a knockdown does. For a common non-coding variant, both the target gene and the direction of its effect on expression have to be inferred.

A perturbation map made in a leukaemia cell line shows no programs enriched for the heritability of a psychiatric trait. What do you conclude?

Nothing about the trait. The programs through which its genes act are probably not present in that cell type. The cell type should be chosen where the trait's heritability is enriched.

Putting the evidence together

No single analysis identifies a drug target. A decision rests on several lines of evidence that have to agree, and on knowing which disagreements matter. This chapter sets out the sequence, the ways it goes wrong, and four cases in which the answer was later checked by a trial.

The sequence

The steps below run roughly in order, though in practice they loop: an unexpected result at step 5 sends you back to step 2.

  1. Define the phenotype. What exactly is the disease, and in whom? A diagnosis that pools distinct mechanisms dilutes every signal that follows. If a drug is meant for progression rather than onset, the ideal study would be of progression, with the selection problems that implies.
  2. Find the loci. A GWAS, ideally across several ancestries, which improves fine-mapping because LD blocks are shorter in some populations (Chapter 16).
  3. Fine-map. Reduce each locus to a credible set (Chapter 18). A set of one or two variants is a tractable result; a set of two hundred is not.
  4. Assign genes. Combine coding variants, colocalized eQTLs and pQTLs, chromatin contacts, enhancer–gene maps, rare-variant burden and pathway membership (Chapters 19–20, 27). Keep the confidence of the assignment attached to the gene; it predicts whether the programme will succeed.
  5. Establish direction and dose. Which way does the disease move when the gene's activity falls? Is there an allelic series (Chapter 24)?
  6. Estimate the causal effect. Drug-target MR with colocalization, positive controls and the checks of Chapter 25.
  7. Assess safety. Phenome-wide scans of the instrument; the health of heterozygous and, where they exist, homozygous loss-of-function carriers.
  8. Check tractability. Is the protein secreted, on the cell surface, intracellular? Which modality could reach it, and in which tissue?
  9. Find the mechanism. In a cell type supported by the genetics, what does the gene do? Perturbation maps (Chapters 26–27) show which programs it regulates, whether genes from other loci converge on the same programs, and in which direction those programs move the trait.
  10. Design the trial. Which patients, which endpoint, how long, and how much target engagement is needed? Steps 5 and 6 inform all four.

The strongest case arises when independent kinds of data point to the same gene and the same direction. The forms of evidence differ in what they can be fooled by, so agreement between them is informative in a way that any one of them is not.

Four cases

PCSK9: the clean case

A Mendelian form of high cholesterol pointed to the gene; people with loss-of-function variants had low LDL and far less coronary disease; one person with no functional copies was healthy; an allelic series ran from mild missense variants to complete loss; the protein is secreted and so reachable by an antibody. Every criterion in the list above was met before any drug existed, and the drugs worked. The case is the template, and also a warning: the evidence is rarely this complete.

Lp(a): right about cause, wrong about the trial

Variants in LPA determine the level of lipoprotein(a), and those raising it raise the risk of coronary disease and aortic stenosis. The causal inference was as strong as any in cardiovascular genetics, and drugs that lower the particle by most of its level were developed. The first large outcome trial, reported in 2026, found no reduction in events over nearly five years in patients with established disease. Genetics had established that lifelong exposure matters; it could not establish that removing the exposure late reverses the risk. The genetic estimate also implied that a very large absolute reduction would be required, which was known in advance.

Lp-PLA2: a negative result that was available first

The enzyme's activity in blood predicted cardiovascular events, and it is present in plaque. A loss-of-function variant in its gene is common in East Asia, and carriers were not protected from vascular disease. Two large trials of an inhibitor found no benefit. Here the genetic experiment and the pharmacological one agreed, and the genetic one was available years earlier and at a tiny fraction of the cost.

CETP: the target was not the biomarker

Inhibiting CETP raises HDL cholesterol substantially. Chapter 22 described why that was the wrong thing to aim at. Variants in CETP are associated with lower coronary risk, but in proportion to their effect on ApoB-containing particles rather than on HDL. Three of four outcome trials failed; the one that succeeded also lowered LDL, by about the amount its benefit would predict. The lesson is about which exposure a target's variants should be scaled against.

A recurring pattern

In each failure, the genetics was not wrong; it was read as answering a question it had not been asked. HDL cholesterol variants were read as evidence about HDL-raising drugs. Lifelong Lp(a) exposure was read as evidence about late Lp(a) lowering. Being precise about what the instrument is, which exposure it is scaled against, and over what period it acts prevents most such errors.

How the evidence is scored

Because the inputs are numerous and partly redundant, they are often combined into a score for each gene at each locus. Some approaches train a model on loci where the causal gene is known from coding or Mendelian evidence, then apply it genome-wide. Others score genes by how similar they are, across many features, to genes already implicated elsewhere in the same trait. Public platforms publish such scores for most traits, with the underlying evidence shown alongside.

These scores are useful for ranking and unreliable as proof. Their training sets are biased towards loci that were easy to solve, which tend to contain coding variants and well-studied genes. They can propagate the same mistake across many loci. The sensible use is to triage: let a score order the candidates, then examine the top ones with the evidence in hand.

What makes a target convincing

A reasonable checklist before committing to a programme:

  • The causal gene is identified by more than proximity, ideally including coding or rare-variant evidence.
  • The direction of effect is consistent across variant classes.
  • There is some evidence on dose: an allelic series, or at least variants of differing strength that agree.
  • A cis-MR estimate exists, with colocalization supporting a shared causal variant and positive controls behaving as expected.
  • Carriers of reduced function show no alarming associations across the phenome.
  • The effect is seen in more than one ancestry, or the reason it is not is understood.
  • The program the target acts through is known, in a cell type where the trait’s heritability is enriched.
  • The target can be reached by a modality in the tissue where it acts.
  • The disease stage and endpoint for the trial match what the genetic evidence actually supports.

Few targets satisfy all of these. The value of listing them is that it makes explicit which are missing, so that the remaining risk is stated rather than assumed away.

How long it takes

One review traced the germline genetic discoveries that have led to an approved drug. It counted about forty, most of them for rare conditions and only a handful for common ones, with a median of roughly twenty-five years between the genetic discovery and the approval. The PCSK9 story, from the French families in 2003 to approved antibodies in 2015, is one of the fastest. The lag is a reason to be cautious about judging the value of genetic target discovery from the drugs approved so far: most of the loci in this book are too recent to have produced one yet.

Beyond cardiovascular genetics

The examples in this part have been mostly lipid genes, because they are the best-worked examples. The same logic has been applied across medicine: IL23R and inflammatory bowel disease, where protective coding variants preceded the antibodies now used; TYK2, where a loss-of-function allele protects against several autoimmune diseases and has guided selective inhibitors; SLC30A8, where loss-of-function variants protect against type 2 diabetes although the gene's Mendelian biology had suggested the opposite direction. The last is a reminder that the direction of effect has to be measured, not assumed from what a gene does.

Key points

  • Target discovery is a sequence: phenotype, loci, fine-mapping, gene assignment, direction and dose, causal estimate, safety, tractability, mechanism, trial design.
  • Different evidence types fail in different ways, so convergence is informative and disagreement is a clue about context.
  • Gene-prioritisation scores are useful for ranking and should not be treated as evidence of cause.
  • The failures in this chapter came from reading genetic evidence as answering a question it was not asked, usually about which exposure or over what period.
  • A target is convincing when the gene, direction, dose, causal estimate, safety and tractability all hold together, and the trial is designed for what the genetics supports.

A gene has a strong colocalized eQTL in liver and a rare-variant burden pointing the other way. What are the possible explanations?

The common signal may act on a different gene; the burden may be driven by annotation errors or LD with a nearby common variant; or complete loss and small changes in expression may genuinely differ in effect. Each can be checked, and the disagreement itself indicates where to look.

Why was the Lp-PLA2 programme avoidable?

A common loss-of-function variant existed in a studied population and carriers were not protected. That is the human equivalent of the trial, and it was available before the trials were run.

Why can a gene-prioritisation score be systematically wrong rather than just noisy?

It is trained on loci that were solvable, which over-represents coding variants and well-studied genes, and it applies the same learned rules everywhere, so a bias in the training set repeats across many loci.

Genetic evidence for a target comes entirely from disease onset in healthy people. What limits does that place on the trial?

It supports prevention in people without the disease better than treatment of established disease. A trial in advanced patients may fail even if the target is real, as the Lp(a) case suggests.

Posterior inclusion probability (PIP)
The probability, given the data and model, that a variant is causal. Ch. 18
Power
The probability that a study detects an effect that is really present. Ch. 2, 14
pQTL
A variant associated with the level of a protein; cis if near that protein’s own gene. Ch. 19, 25
Principal components
The leading axes of genetic variation in a sample, used to summarise and adjust for ancestry. Ch. 10, 15
Purifying selection
Removal of harmful alleles by selection. Ch. 8
Recombination fraction (c)
The probability that a transmitted chromosome is recombinant between two sites. Ch. 1
Selection coefficient (s)
The fitness difference between genotypes. Ch. 8
Selective sweep
The rapid rise of a beneficial allele, removing nearby variation. Ch. 8, 11
Site frequency spectrum (SFS)
The count of variable sites at each derived-allele count in a sample. Ch. 7
SKAT
A rare-variant test that sums squared per-variant statistics, allowing effects in both directions. Ch. 20
SNP heritability
The share of trait variance explained jointly by a given set of SNPs. Ch. 17
Summary statistics
The per-variant effect estimates, standard errors and p-values from a GWAS. Ch. 14
Tajima's D
A statistic comparing two estimates of θ to detect a skew in the frequency spectrum. Ch. 7
θ (theta)
The population-scaled mutation rate, \(4N_e\mu\). Ch. 5
TWAS
A test of association between genetically predicted gene expression and a trait. Ch. 19
Wald ratio
The Mendelian randomization estimate from one variant: its effect on the outcome divided by its effect on the exposure. Ch. 22
Winner's curse
Overestimation of effect sizes for variants selected because they passed a significance threshold. Ch. 14
Wright–Fisher model
The standard model of drift: each generation is formed by sampling alleles with replacement from the previous one. Ch. 4
