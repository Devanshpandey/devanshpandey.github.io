Arteries and Alleles

What human genetics has revealed about the biology of coronary artery disease: which molecules and cells cause it, how we know, and how that knowledge became medicines.

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of death worldwide. It is also one of the diseases that human genetics has explained best. Half a century ago, families with inherited high cholesterol led to the discovery of the LDL receptor. Since 2007, genome-wide association studies have found several hundred regions of the genome where common variants change coronary risk. Sequencing studies have found people who naturally lack a gene and are protected. Each of these findings is an experiment that nature has already run in humans, and together they give a mechanistic account of the disease that no other source of evidence could have provided.

This book tells that account in order. It starts with the disease itself: what an artery is, how a plaque forms, and why it kills. It then goes through the biology one pathway at a time. For each pathway it states what the genetic evidence is, which genes are involved, in which cells they act, and what happened when drugs were aimed at them.

It is a companion to Genes in Populations, which explains the methods (GWAS, fine-mapping, colocalization, rare-variant tests, polygenic scores, Mendelian randomization) from the ground up. Here those methods are used and only briefly recalled. If a term is unfamiliar, the gene index and glossary at the end will point you to the right place.

The disease in one picture

A coronary plaque takes decades to develop and passes through a sequence of steps. Genetics has found causal genes at every step. The map below is the outline of the book.

Three ideas that run through the book

Genetics gives causal direction. A blood test can show that people with a given biomarker have more heart attacks. It cannot show that the biomarker causes them. An inherited variant is assigned at conception, before disease and before lifestyle, so an association between a variant and disease is much harder to explain away by confounding or reverse causation. This is why genetics could settle arguments that decades of observation could not: that LDL is a cause, that HDL cholesterol is not, that C-reactive protein is a bystander while the interleukin-6 pathway is a participant.

Most of the genetic risk is not about cholesterol. The best-understood genes are the lipid genes, because their effects can be read in a blood test. But most of the regions found by association studies show no effect on lipids, blood pressure or any other standard risk factor. They act inside the artery wall, in smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells and immune cells. This is the part of the disease that current medicines do not treat.

A gene is not a mechanism until the experiment is done. An association points to a region. Turning it into a statement about a gene, a cell type and a direction of effect takes fine-mapping, functional genomics and laboratory work. Throughout, the book separates what has been shown from what is inferred, and says so when the evidence is disputed.

The artery and the plaque

Coronary artery disease is a disease of the artery wall. Before asking what the genes do, we need the anatomy of the wall, the cells in it, and the sequence of events that turns a healthy artery into one that can close without warning.

The coronary arteries

The heart pumps about five litres of blood a minute and never rests, so its own muscle needs a constant supply of oxygen. That supply comes through the coronary arteries, two vessels a few millimetres wide that leave the aorta just above the aortic valve and branch over the surface of the heart. Heart muscle extracts most of the oxygen from the blood it receives even at rest. It has almost no reserve, so when a coronary artery is blocked, the muscle it supplies begins to die within about twenty minutes.

The wall has three layers

  • The intima is the inner layer. It is lined by a single sheet of endothelial cells, which are in direct contact with blood. In a healthy young artery the intima is thin. It is where plaques form.
  • The media is the middle layer, made of vascular smooth muscle cells arranged in rings and embedded in elastin and collagen. By contracting and relaxing, these cells set the width of the artery.
  • The adventitia is the outer layer of connective tissue, with fibroblasts, nerves and small vessels that feed the wall itself.

Three cell types do most of the work in this book. Endothelial cells form the barrier and sense blood flow. Smooth muscle cells give the wall its structure and, as we shall see, can change their identity. Macrophages, immune cells derived from blood monocytes, enter the wall in disease and take up lipid.

How a plaque forms

Atherosclerosis is the slow build-up of lipid, cells and scar tissue in the intima. It unfolds over decades. The steps are as follows.

  1. Lipoproteins enter and are retained. Cholesterol travels in blood inside particles called lipoproteins. Particles that carry the protein ApoB, chiefly LDL, cross the endothelium and become trapped by binding to the proteoglycans of the intima. The more particles in the blood and the longer the exposure, the more are retained. This is the initiating event, and nothing else in the sequence happens without it.
  2. The retained lipid is modified and provokes a response. Trapped particles are oxidised and aggregate. Endothelial cells above them become activated and display adhesion molecules that capture passing monocytes.
  3. Monocytes enter and become foam cells. Inside the wall, monocytes mature into macrophages and engulf the modified lipoproteins. Unlike the regulated uptake of normal LDL, this uptake has no off switch. The cells fill with cholesterol droplets and are then called foam cells. A collection of foam cells is visible to the naked eye as a fatty streak, and fatty streaks are present in the arteries of many teenagers.
  4. Smooth muscle cells move in. Smooth muscle cells from the media migrate into the intima, multiply, and lay down collagen. Some also take up lipid. They build a fibrous cap over the lesion.
  5. A necrotic core develops. Foam cells die. If the dead cells are not cleared quickly (a process called efferocytosis), their contents spill out and form a pool of lipid and debris, the necrotic core. The mature plaque is a lipid core walled off from the blood by a fibrous cap.
  6. Calcification. Calcium deposits form in older plaques. They can be seen on a CT scan and are used clinically as a measure of total plaque burden.

How a plaque causes harm

A plaque causes disease in two different ways, and the distinction matters for interpreting the genetics.

Narrowing. A plaque that grows inward reduces the lumen. When demand for oxygen rises during exertion, flow cannot keep up, and the person feels chest pain that settles with rest. This is stable angina. It limits life but is seldom immediately fatal.

Sudden blockage. Most heart attacks happen in a different way. The fibrous cap of a plaque tears (rupture), or the endothelium over it is lost (erosion). Blood then meets the contents of the plaque, which strongly activate clotting. Platelets stick, the coagulation cascade produces fibrin, and a clot (thrombus) forms within minutes. If it blocks the artery, the result is a myocardial infarction. The plaques responsible are often not the ones that were causing the tightest narrowing beforehand.

Plaques differ in how likely they are to rupture. A plaque with a large lipid core, many inflammatory cells and a thin cap is fragile. One with a thick, collagen-rich cap and plenty of smooth muscle cells is stable. So the amount of plaque is one determinant of risk and the composition of plaque is another, and different genes affect each.

Why the phenotype definition matters

Genetic studies of "CAD" combine people with heart attacks, angina, bypass surgery, stents and narrowed arteries on angiography. That is sensible, since all of these share atherosclerosis as the underlying cause. But a variant that promotes plaque growth and a variant that promotes clotting on an existing plaque will both appear as CAD risk variants. Chapter 11 describes a study that separated the two and found that they map to different genes.

The classical risk factors

Long-term cohort studies, beginning with Framingham in 1948, identified the measurable traits that predict coronary disease: high LDL cholesterol, high blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, obesity, older age, male sex, and a family history of early disease. These remain the basis of clinical risk prediction.

Two questions were left open by that work. First, which of these factors are causes and which are only markers? Observational studies cannot say, because risk factors travel together. Second, why do many people have heart attacks with no risk factor that stands out? The first question is taken up in Chapter 12. The second is one reason the genetics of the artery wall, covered in Part III, is of such interest.

Related diseases with different biology

Not every heart attack is atherosclerotic. Spontaneous coronary artery dissection, a tear within the wall of an artery that is otherwise free of plaque, mainly affects younger women. As Chapter 8 will show, it shares a genetic locus with CAD, but the risk allele points the opposite way. Finding that kind of pattern is one of the ways genetics separates conditions that look alike in the clinic.

Key points

  • Atherosclerosis begins when ApoB-containing lipoproteins are retained in the intima. Macrophages take them up and become foam cells; smooth muscle cells build a fibrous cap; a necrotic core forms.
  • Endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells and macrophages are the main cell types, and each has its own set of risk genes.
  • Narrowing causes angina. Most heart attacks are caused by rupture or erosion of a plaque followed by a clot.
  • Risk depends on both the amount of plaque and its composition.
  • Genetic studies of CAD pool several clinical presentations, so risk variants can act on plaque formation, plaque stability or clotting.

How genetics studies coronary disease

Coronary disease runs in families, and the reasons range from a single broken gene to millions of small effects. Each kind of genetic evidence answers a different question. This chapter sets out the kinds, what each can and cannot show, and how the catalogue of risk loci was built.

Coronary disease is heritable

A parent or sibling with early coronary disease roughly doubles a person's own risk, and that has been known since the first cohort studies. Twin studies put a number on it. In the Swedish Twin Registry, the heritability of death from coronary heart disease was estimated at about one half, somewhat higher in men than in women, and higher for deaths at younger ages. Genes matter most for early disease. By old age, decades of accumulated exposure have had more time to act.

Heritability says that genetic differences matter. It does not say which genes, or how. For that there are four main sources of evidence.

1. Mendelian disorders: one gene, large effect

Some families carry a single variant that causes very high cholesterol and heart attacks in early adulthood, or even in childhood. The best known condition is familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH). Studying such families identified the LDL receptor in the 1970s and PCSK9 in 2003. Mendelian disorders are rare, but they give the clearest possible view of what a gene does, because the effect is large and segregates visibly through a pedigree.

2. Genome-wide association studies: many loci, small effects

A genome-wide association study (GWAS) compares the frequency of millions of common variants between people with and without disease. The first GWAS of coronary disease were published in 2007, and several groups independently found the same signal on chromosome 9p21, the subject of Chapter 7. A typical risk allele found this way is common and raises risk by 3–10%.

Small effects need large samples. Progress came from pooling studies into consortia, first CARDIoGRAM and C4D, then their merger, and later the addition of large biobanks such as UK Biobank, the Million Veteran Program and Biobank Japan.

Three general features of the results are worth stating at the outset.

  • Nearly all the signals are in non-coding DNA. They change how much of a gene is made, in which cell and when. They do not change the protein. Working out which gene a signal acts on is a research project for each locus.
  • Only a minority of loci act through known risk factors. Some risk alleles are also associated with LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, blood pressure or diabetes. Most are associated with none of these, which means they act through pathways that a clinic does not measure.
  • The effects are spread thinly. The genome-wide significant loci explain only part of the heritability. Much of the rest lies in thousands of variants whose effects are too small to detect individually.

3. Rare-variant studies: natural knockouts

Sequencing the protein-coding genes of large numbers of people finds rare variants that inactivate one copy of a gene. If carriers of such loss-of-function variants have less coronary disease, three things follow at once. The gene is causal. Reducing its activity is protective. And partial lifelong loss is compatible with health, since the carriers were found among ordinary adults. This is the closest human equivalent of a drug trial, and it is the form of genetic evidence that drug developers trust most. PCSK9, NPC1L1, APOC3, ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL4 have all been validated in this way.

4. Mendelian randomization: testing a biomarker

Suppose a set of variants raises a biomarker such as LDL cholesterol. If the biomarker causes disease, then people who inherit those variants should have more disease, in proportion to how much their biomarker is raised. Mendelian randomization (MR) tests exactly this. Because variants are allocated at conception, the comparison resembles a randomised trial of lifelong exposure. MR has been used to judge nearly every proposed coronary risk factor, and Chapter 12 collects the verdicts.

MR depends on assumptions. The most important is that the variants affect disease only through the biomarker being tested. When a variant has other effects (pleiotropy), the conclusion can be wrong. The strongest MR studies use variants in the gene that encodes the biomarker itself, where the biology is clear.

From a locus to a mechanism

A GWAS locus is a set of correlated variants. Getting from there to biology means answering four questions in turn.

  1. Which variant? Statistical fine-mapping narrows the set to the few variants most likely to be causal.
  2. Which gene? The evidence includes whether the variant changes a protein, whether it is associated with expression of a nearby gene (an eQTL) or with the level of a protein in blood (a pQTL), whether the two signals colocalize, whether the variant physically contacts a gene's promoter, and whether rare coding variants in a nearby gene are also associated with disease. The nearest gene is right more often than not, but far from always.
  3. Which cell type? Regulatory variants act where the regulatory element is active. Maps of open chromatin in single cells from human coronary arteries now show whether a variant lies in an element used by smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, macrophages or hepatocytes.
  4. Which direction, and what does the gene do? This needs experiments: editing the variant in cultured human cells, or altering the gene in mice and measuring plaque.

For lipid genes, all four questions have been answered many times over. For most artery-wall loci, the work is unfinished, and the chapters in Part III say plainly how far it has gone for each.

A caution about mouse models

Mice do not develop atherosclerosis naturally. Their main lipoprotein is HDL. Experiments use mice lacking Apoe or Ldlr, fed a high-fat diet, which produces plaques in the aorta within weeks. These models have been very useful for studying how plaques grow. They rarely rupture or cause a heart attack, so they say little about the final clinical event, and a gene's effect in mice does not always match its effect in people.

Key points

  • Coronary disease is about half heritable, more so at younger ages.
  • Mendelian families identify genes of large effect; GWAS identify hundreds of regions of small effect; rare loss-of-function variants show the direction of effect; Mendelian randomization tests whether a biomarker is causal.
  • Most GWAS signals are regulatory and most are unrelated to standard risk factors.
  • Going from locus to mechanism means identifying the variant, the gene, the cell type and the direction, and for most artery-wall loci that is incomplete.

LDL and ApoB

The best-established fact about coronary disease is that LDL particles cause it. The proof came from genetics, beginning with children who had heart attacks, and it produced the most widely used drugs in medicine.

What a lipoprotein is

Cholesterol and triglycerides do not dissolve in water, so the body moves them through blood inside particles. A lipoprotein has a core of fat (triglyceride and cholesterol ester) and a shell of phospholipid and protein. The proteins, called apolipoproteins, hold the particle together and direct it to receptors.

The liver secretes triglyceride-rich particles called VLDL. Each carries exactly one molecule of apolipoprotein B (ApoB), which stays with it for life. As the particle circulates, its triglyceride is removed for use by muscle and fat tissue. The particle shrinks, becoming a remnant and then a cholesterol-rich LDL particle. LDL circulates for a few days until it is taken up, mostly by the liver.

The standard blood test reports LDL-C, the mass of cholesterol carried in LDL. A measurement of ApoB instead counts the particles. The two usually agree, but when they do not, the particle count tracks risk better, because it is particles that enter the artery wall.

The LDL receptor pathway

In the early 1970s, Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein studied skin cells from children with homozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia, who have LDL cholesterol four to six times normal and heart attacks in childhood. Normal cells took up LDL from the surrounding fluid and responded by shutting down their own cholesterol synthesis. The patients' cells did neither. From this they deduced the existence of a cell-surface LDL receptor, described how it carries LDL into the cell, and showed that the patients lacked it. They received the Nobel Prize in 1985.

The pathway they uncovered has a simple logic. A liver cell senses its own cholesterol content. When cholesterol is low, the transcription factor SREBP2 switches on the genes for cholesterol synthesis, including HMGCR, and the gene for the LDL receptor, LDLR. More receptors on the cell surface pull more LDL out of the blood. So anything that lowers cholesterol inside the liver cell lowers LDL in the blood. That is how statins work: they inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, the liver cell makes more LDL receptors to compensate, and blood LDL falls.

Familial hypercholesterolaemia

FH is caused by variants in three genes, all in this pathway:

  • LDLR, the receptor. Loss-of-function variants account for most cases.
  • APOB, the ligand. Certain variants in the part of ApoB that binds the receptor prevent LDL from docking.
  • PCSK9. Gain-of-function variants make the protein more active, so more receptors are destroyed.

A rare recessive form is caused by loss of LDLRAP1, an adaptor the receptor needs in order to be taken into liver cells. Heterozygous FH affects about 1 in 250 people. Untreated, LDL cholesterol is roughly double the normal level from birth, and coronary disease typically appears in the forties or fifties in men and a decade later in women.

FH carries a lesson that goes beyond the rare disease. For a given LDL level measured in adulthood, a person with an FH variant has a higher coronary risk than a person without one. A single measurement in middle age understates the exposure of someone whose LDL has been high since birth. What damages the artery is the cumulative exposure: concentration multiplied by time.

PCSK9: from a French family to a drug

In 2003, Marianne Abifadel, Catherine Boileau and colleagues traced a form of FH in French families to variants in a little-studied gene, PCSK9. The variants turned out to increase the protein's activity. That raised an obvious question: what happens to people with too little?

Helen Hobbs and Jonathan Cohen answered it by sequencing PCSK9 in people from the Dallas Heart Study who had very low LDL. They found two nonsense variants, carried by about 2–3% of Black participants. In a separate cohort followed for 15 years, carriers had LDL cholesterol 28% lower than non-carriers and a risk of coronary heart disease 88% lower. A milder missense variant found in white participants lowered LDL by 15% and risk by about half. One woman who had inherited inactivating variants on both chromosomes had almost no circulating PCSK9, very low LDL, and was healthy.

These observations were a complete argument for a drug. Inhibiting PCSK9 should lower LDL and prevent coronary disease, and should be safe. Antibodies against PCSK9 were approved in 2015. Later came a small interfering RNA that silences the gene in the liver with two injections a year, and in 2022 the first trial in which a single infusion of a base editor permanently switched the gene off.

SORT1: a GWAS locus solved

The strongest common-variant association with LDL cholesterol lies on chromosome 1p13, in a non-coding region between several genes. It is also a CAD locus. In 2010, Kiran Musunuru, Daniel Rader and colleagues showed how it works. A single-base change creates a binding site for the transcription factor C/EBP in liver cells. That raises expression of the nearby gene SORT1 many-fold in the liver. Sortilin, the protein, reduces secretion of VLDL and increases breakdown of LDL. The result is lower LDL and fewer heart attacks. This was one of the first cases in which a non-coding GWAS signal was followed all the way to a variant, a gene, a tissue and a mechanism, and it set the pattern for later work.

Lifelong exposure and the size of the benefit

Statin trials show that lowering LDL cholesterol by 1 mmol/L (39 mg/dL) for about five years cuts coronary events by roughly a fifth. Genetic variants that lower LDL by the same amount from birth are associated with a reduction about three times larger. The comparison was first set out by Brian Ference and colleagues in 2012.

The explanation is the cumulative-exposure idea again. Plaque builds over decades in proportion to the number of ApoB particles in the blood. Lowering LDL at 60 slows the process from that point on. Lowering it from birth prevents the plaque from forming. This has changed clinical thinking towards treating earlier and for longer, and it is the rationale for one-time treatments that lower LDL permanently.

It is the particle

Different genes lower LDL in different ways, and some change the number of particles more than the cholesterol in each. When variants in many genes are compared, the reduction in coronary risk is proportional to the reduction in ApoB, the particle count, whatever the mechanism. The next chapter shows that this also holds for triglyceride-rich particles. The unifying statement is that risk depends on the number of ApoB-containing particles in the blood and on how long the artery wall has been exposed to them.

Key points

  • Every LDL particle carries one ApoB. The liver removes LDL through the LDL receptor, and makes more receptors when its own cholesterol is low.
  • FH is caused by variants in LDLR, APOB and PCSK9, all in the receptor pathway.
  • PCSK9 loss-of-function carriers have lower LDL, far less coronary disease and no apparent harm. Drugs followed within about a decade.
  • Variants in many genes that lower LDL by different mechanisms all lower coronary risk, in proportion to the fall in ApoB.
  • Risk follows cumulative exposure, so lifelong lowering gives about three times the benefit per mmol/L seen in five-year trials.

Triglyceride-rich lipoproteins

For decades it was unclear whether high triglycerides cause coronary disease or merely accompany it. Human knockouts of a handful of genes that control one enzyme settled the question, and showed that the harm comes from the particles that carry triglyceride.

The pathway: delivering fat to tissues

Triglyceride is the body's fuel in transit. After a meal, the intestine packages dietary fat into large particles called chylomicrons. Between meals, the liver packages its own triglyceride into VLDL. Both kinds of particle travel to muscle and fat tissue, where an enzyme anchored to the wall of small blood vessels, lipoprotein lipase (LPL), breaks down the triglyceride so that the tissue can take up the fatty acids.

As a particle loses triglyceride it shrinks into a remnant. Remnants still carry ApoB and are relatively rich in cholesterol. The liver clears most of them; the rest become LDL. Remnants are small enough to enter the artery wall, and macrophages can take them up without any prior modification.

People with high triglycerides have more coronary disease. Trials of older triglyceride-lowering drugs gave mixed results. By the 2000s many cardiologists regarded triglycerides as a marker.

An antibody against it, evinacumab, was approved in 2021 for homozygous FH.

It is still the particle

Does triglyceride itself damage arteries? Probably not. People with complete LPL deficiency have extremely high triglycerides carried in huge chylomicrons, which are too large to cross the endothelium. Their main problem is pancreatitis, and they have less atherosclerosis than their triglyceride level would suggest.

What matters is the number of remnant particles and the cholesterol they deliver. A direct test of this compared triglyceride-lowering variants in LPL with LDL-lowering variants in LDLR. Scaled by their effect on LDL cholesterol or triglycerides, the two looked different. Scaled by their effect on ApoB, they had the same effect on coronary risk: about 23% lower for every 10 mg/dL less ApoB in both cases. A triglyceride-rich particle and an LDL particle appear to be about equally harmful, one for one.

Consequence for drugs

This explains a pattern in clinical trials. Drugs that lower triglycerides a great deal but ApoB only slightly, such as fibrates and fish-oil preparations in several large trials, have shown little or no reduction in coronary events. On the genetic evidence, a triglyceride-lowering drug should prevent coronary disease to the extent that it lowers the number of ApoB particles.

The drugs that followed

ApoC-III and ANGPTL3 are made in the liver, which is the organ most easily reached by antisense oligonucleotides and small interfering RNAs. Agents that silence APOC3 lower triglycerides by half or more and have been approved for familial chylomicronaemia syndrome, where the aim is to prevent pancreatitis. Whether they prevent coronary events in the far larger group of people with moderately raised triglycerides is being tested in outcome trials.

ANGPTL4 has been harder. In animals, antibodies against it were reported to cause lipid-laden inflammation in abdominal lymph nodes, which halted early programmes. Human carriers of inactivating variants do not show an obvious equivalent problem, and liver-targeted approaches are being developed on that basis. This is a case where genetics and animal toxicology gave different signals and the question is still open.

Key points

  • LPL removes triglyceride from VLDL and chylomicrons. ApoC-III, ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL4 inhibit it; ApoA-V supports it.
  • Variants that increase LPL activity lower triglycerides and coronary risk; variants that reduce it do the opposite. This holds for every gene in the pathway.
  • The harm comes from cholesterol-rich remnant particles. Per ApoB particle, the effect on risk matches that of LDL.
  • Drugs silencing APOC3 and ANGPTL3 came directly from human loss-of-function carriers.

HDL: the hypothesis that failed

High HDL cholesterol predicts a low risk of heart attack as reliably as any measurement in medicine. For thirty years it was assumed that raising it would protect the heart. Genetics showed that the assumption was wrong, and the story is the clearest lesson in the difference between a marker and a cause.

The "good cholesterol" idea

HDL particles do not carry ApoB. Their main protein is ApoA-I. They collect excess cholesterol from tissues, including from macrophages in the artery wall, and return it to the liver, a process called reverse cholesterol transport. The biology made a protective role plausible, and the epidemiology seemed to confirm it: in the Framingham study and every cohort since, people with higher HDL cholesterol had fewer heart attacks, independently of LDL.

Raising HDL became a major goal of drug development.

The genetic test

If HDL cholesterol protects, then people who inherit variants that raise it should have fewer heart attacks. In 2012, Benjamin Voight, Sekar Kathiresan and colleagues tested this in two ways.

First they used a single variant in the endothelial lipase gene, LIPG Asn396Ser. Carriers have HDL cholesterol about 0.14 mmol/L higher, with no change in LDL or triglycerides. From the epidemiology, that difference should lower the risk of heart attack by about 13%. In more than 20,000 cases and 95,000 controls, the observed odds ratio was 0.99. There was no protection.

Second, they built a score from 14 common variants associated only with HDL cholesterol. A higher score meant higher HDL and no difference in risk. The same analysis using LDL-raising variants gave the expected increase in risk.

Other genetic findings agreed. Variants that lower HDL through ABCA1 or LCAT do not raise coronary risk in proportion. Most strikingly, a rare variant that abolishes the liver's HDL receptor, SCARB1 P376L, raises HDL cholesterol substantially and also raises coronary risk, with an odds ratio of about 1.8. Cholesterol piles up in HDL because it cannot be delivered to the liver. A high HDL cholesterol can therefore mean that reverse transport is failing.

The drug trials

The trials ran in parallel with the genetics and reached the same answer at far greater cost. Niacin raised HDL and did not reduce events when added to a statin. The CETP inhibitors were the main test. CETP moves cholesterol from HDL to ApoB particles, so blocking it raises HDL cholesterol by 30% to over 100%.

Why the epidemiology misled

HDL cholesterol is tightly and inversely linked to triglyceride-rich particles, because CETP exchanges lipid between the two. A low HDL cholesterol is a sign of many remnant particles, insulin resistance and obesity. It marks a harmful state without causing the harm. Statistical adjustment could not separate them because the measurements are so strongly correlated and each is measured with error.

What is still open

The genetic evidence rules out HDL cholesterol concentration as a cause. It does not rule out a role for what HDL particles do. The capacity of a person's HDL to accept cholesterol from macrophages predicts coronary events in cohort studies. No genetic or trial evidence yet shows that improving that function prevents disease.

The general lesson

The HDL story is why Mendelian randomization became a routine step before investing in a target. An association can be strong, consistent across populations, graded, biologically plausible and still not causal. Genetic evidence, available years before the trial results, gave the right answer for HDL cholesterol. Chapter 12 applies the same test to the other proposed risk factors.

Key points

  • High HDL cholesterol predicts low coronary risk, but variants that raise HDL cholesterol alone do not lower risk.
  • Loss of the HDL receptor SCARB1 raises both HDL cholesterol and coronary risk.
  • Drugs that raised HDL cholesterol failed. The benefit of potent CETP inhibition is explained by lower ApoB.
  • Low HDL cholesterol is a marker of excess triglyceride-rich particles and metabolic disease.

Lipoprotein(a)

Lipoprotein(a) is the most heritable common risk factor for coronary disease. One gene sets its level, the genetic evidence that it is causal is strong, and the first large trial of lowering it did not reduce events. It is the best current example of how much, and how little, genetic evidence can promise.

The particle

Lipoprotein(a), written Lp(a) and said "L-P-little-a", is an LDL particle with one extra protein, apolipoprotein(a), attached to its ApoB by a chemical bond. Apolipoprotein(a) is encoded by the LPA gene, which arose by duplication of the gene for plasminogen, the precursor of the enzyme that dissolves clots. It is found only in humans, apes and Old World monkeys.

Three properties have been proposed to make the particle harmful. It is an ApoB particle and can deposit cholesterol in the wall like LDL. It is the main carrier in blood of oxidised phospholipids, which provoke inflammation. And because it resembles plasminogen, it might interfere with the breakdown of clots, though the evidence for that in people is weak.

One gene, a huge range

Lp(a) concentration varies more than a thousand-fold between people, and a person's level is set in early childhood and hardly changes afterwards. Diet, exercise and statins do little to it. The reason is that the level is controlled almost entirely by the LPA gene itself.

The gene contains a block of sequence, called kringle IV type 2, that is repeated a variable number of times, from a few copies to more than forty. More repeats make a larger protein. Large versions are secreted poorly by the liver, so people with many repeats have low Lp(a) and people with few have high Lp(a). Single-nucleotide variants in the gene modify the level further.

Levels also differ between populations. On average they are highest in people of African ancestry, intermediate in South Asians and Europeans, and lowest in East Asians, largely because the distribution of LPA variants differs.

The genetic evidence

Because one gene determines the level, LPA variants are a clean instrument for Mendelian randomization. In 2009, two studies used them. Robert Clarke and colleagues found two variants strongly associated with high Lp(a). Carriers of one had 1.7 times the odds of coronary disease and carriers of the other 1.9 times, and the excess risk was explained by the Lp(a) level. Pia Kamstrup and colleagues in Copenhagen showed the same using the repeat number directly. In genome-wide studies LPA is consistently among the strongest CAD loci.

The same variants are associated with calcific aortic valve stenosis, a narrowing of the heart's outlet valve, which made Lp(a) the first identified causal factor for that condition. People with rare variants that abolish LPA have almost no Lp(a), appear healthy, and have less coronary disease.

How much lowering would be needed

Genetics also gave a warning about the size of the effect. Per unit of mass, Lp(a) lowering is associated with much less benefit than LDL lowering. Stephen Burgess and colleagues estimated that Lp(a) would have to fall by about 100 mg/dL to give the same reduction in coronary risk as a 1 mmol/L (38.7 mg/dL) fall in LDL cholesterol. The implication was that only people with very high levels could benefit much, and only from drugs that lower Lp(a) nearly to zero.

The drugs and the first outcome trial

Apolipoprotein(a) is made in the liver, so it can be silenced with antisense oligonucleotides or small interfering RNAs. These lower Lp(a) by 70% to more than 95%, far more than any earlier treatment. Several large trials were started to find out whether that prevents heart attacks.

The first to report was Lp(a)HORIZON, published in November 2026. It randomised 8,350 patients who already had cardiovascular disease and an Lp(a) of at least 70 mg/dL to monthly pelacarsen, an antisense oligonucleotide, or placebo. The drug worked on its target: at one year the median Lp(a) was 64 nmol/L on treatment against 233 nmol/L on placebo. After a median of 4.7 years, a major cardiovascular event had occurred in 12.3% of the treated group and 12.1% of the placebo group (hazard ratio 1.02, 95% confidence interval 0.90 to 1.15). There was no benefit, including among patients with the highest starting levels.

How to read a null trial against positive genetics

The two results are not measuring the same thing, and several explanations are possible. They are hypotheses, not conclusions.

  • Lifelong versus late exposure. Genetic variants act from birth. The trial lowered Lp(a) for under five years in people who already had advanced disease. If Lp(a) mainly drives the early build-up of plaque, late lowering may do little. The same gap exists for LDL (Chapter 3), but there the short-term effect is still clearly visible.
  • The size of the reduction. The absolute fall was large but, on the genetic estimate, equivalent to well under 1 mmol/L of LDL lowering, and the confidence interval of the trial does not exclude a small benefit.
  • The genetic estimate may not transfer. Variants in LPA change the size of the protein as well as its amount, and the two are hard to separate. If small isoforms are more harmful particle for particle, lowering concentration alone may not reproduce the genetic effect.
  • The measured exposure may be imperfect. Assays for Lp(a) are affected by isoform size.

Trials of more potent agents, and in different patient groups, were still running when this was written. Check the current literature before drawing a conclusion about the target.

Whatever the final verdict, the episode is instructive. Genetic evidence established that people who inherit high Lp(a) have more coronary disease, and nothing in the trial overturns that. What genetics cannot say by itself is when in life the exposure does its damage, or whether removing it late reverses the risk. Those are questions only a trial can answer.

What this means for patients now

Lp(a) remains useful for assessing risk. Guidelines in several countries recommend measuring it once in adult life, since it does not change. A high level identifies a person, and often a family, at increased inherited risk, for whom other risk factors can be treated more intensively.

Key points

  • Lp(a) is an LDL particle carrying apolipoprotein(a). Its level is set almost entirely by the LPA gene, chiefly by the number of kringle IV type 2 repeats.
  • LPA variants that raise Lp(a) raise the risk of coronary disease and aortic valve stenosis.
  • Genetics predicted that a large absolute reduction would be needed for clinical benefit.
  • The first outcome trial of Lp(a) lowering, in patients with established disease, showed no reduction in events over 4.7 years.
  • Genetic evidence shows that lifelong exposure matters. It does not guarantee that lowering the exposure late in the disease will help.

9p21: the first locus

The first region of the genome linked to common coronary disease is still the strongest. It contains no protein-coding gene, it has nothing to do with cholesterol, and after nearly twenty years its mechanism is only partly understood. It opened the study of the artery wall.

The discovery

In 2007, four groups working independently reported the same finding within months of one another. Common variants in a short stretch of chromosome 9, band p21, were associated with heart attack and coronary disease. The signal replicated in every population tested.

The numbers are unusual for a GWAS locus. The risk allele has a frequency near 50% in Europeans, so about three-quarters of people carry at least one copy and about a quarter carry two. Each copy raises the odds of coronary disease by about 25–30%. Because the allele is so common, it accounts for more cases at the population level than any other single locus.

The risk allele is not associated with LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, diabetes, obesity or smoking. Whatever it does, it does through something that the standard risk factors do not capture.

What lies in the region

CDKN2A and CDKN2B encode p16, p14ARF and p15, proteins that stop the cell cycle and promote senescence. They are among the most frequently deleted genes in cancer. That made them obvious candidates: a variant that weakened these brakes might let cells in the artery wall multiply too freely.

The experimental evidence

The mouse deletion. In 2010, Axel Visel, Len Pennacchio and colleagues deleted the equivalent 70,000-base region from the mouse genome. Expression of Cdkn2a and Cdkn2b in the heart and aorta fell sharply. Smooth muscle cells cultured from the aortas of these mice divided faster and were slower to become senescent. This showed that the region contains regulatory elements that control the neighbouring genes at a distance, and that the affected cell behaviour is proliferation.

Enhancers and inflammation. The human interval is dense with enhancers. Some risk variants alter a binding site for STAT1, a transcription factor activated by interferon-γ, which suggests that the region changes how vascular cells respond to inflammatory signals.

Human cells with and without the risk haplotype. In 2018, Valentina Lo Sardo, Kristin Baldwin and colleagues made induced pluripotent stem cells from people homozygous for the risk or the non-risk haplotype, turned them into vascular smooth muscle cells, and used genome editing to delete the haplotype. Cells with the risk haplotype had widespread changes in gene expression and showed abnormal adhesion, contraction and proliferation. Deleting the risk haplotype returned them to the normal state. Deleting the non-risk haplotype had little effect. Expressing ANRIL in normal cells produced the risk-like behaviour.

What can and cannot be said

Put together, the most supported account is this. The risk haplotype changes regulatory activity in vascular smooth muscle cells, at least partly through the non-coding RNA ANRIL and the cell-cycle genes CDKN2A and CDKN2B. The cells proliferate more and hold their normal contractile state less well.

Several things remain unsettled:

  • Which of the dozens of tightly linked variants are causal. There may be several.
  • How much of the effect runs through ANRIL and how much through direct regulation of CDKN2A/B. ANRIL has many splice forms, including circular ones, and they may act differently.
  • Whether smooth muscle is the only relevant cell type. Effects in macrophages and endothelial cells have been reported.
  • Why mice with the deletion did not develop more atherosclerosis in the standard models.

Clues from other diseases

The same risk allele is associated with abdominal aortic aneurysm and intracranial aneurysm. Aneurysms are a weakening and widening of the artery wall, with no necessary involvement of lipid. That shared association supports the view that 9p21 acts on the structure and cellular make-up of the wall itself.

There is also a clinical clue. In people who already have coronary disease, the 9p21 allele is associated with how much plaque they have, and much less clearly with whether they go on to have a heart attack. It seems to affect the development of atherosclerosis more than the final event.

Why it matters beyond the locus itself

9p21 established three facts that shaped the field. Common variants with real effects on heart disease exist. They can lie far from any gene and act by regulation. And an important share of coronary risk has nothing to do with the classical risk factors. Every chapter in this part follows from the third point.

Key points

  • 9p21 was the first GWAS locus for coronary disease and remains the strongest common one, with a risk allele carried by most people.
  • It is independent of lipids, blood pressure and diabetes.
  • The risk interval contains no protein-coding gene. It overlaps ANRIL and regulates the cell-cycle inhibitors CDKN2A and CDKN2B.
  • Mouse deletion and edited human stem-cell models both point to altered proliferation and function of vascular smooth muscle cells.
  • The causal variants and the full mechanism are not established.

Smooth muscle cells and the plaque

More CAD risk loci act in smooth muscle cells than in any other cell of the artery wall. These cells can change what they are, and which way they change decides whether a plaque is stable or dangerous.

A cell that changes identity

In a healthy artery, smooth muscle cells are contractile. They are packed with actin and myosin, they divide rarely, and their job is to hold the vessel's tone. They are not fixed in that state. In response to injury or to the lipid and inflammatory signals of a plaque, they switch off their contractile genes, start to divide and migrate, and take on other characters. This is called phenotypic modulation.

For a long time the cells in a plaque were identified by a few marker proteins, and most cells carrying macrophage markers were assumed to have come from the blood. Experiments in mice that permanently label smooth muscle cells and all their descendants overturned this. A large share of the cells in a plaque, including many that look like macrophages or foam cells, descend from smooth muscle cells. Single-cell RNA sequencing of mouse and human plaques then showed the range of states these cells can enter.

The fibrous cap is built by smooth muscle-derived cells. A plaque in which these cells move in and make collagen has a thick cap and is less likely to rupture. A plaque in which they die, fail to arrive, or turn inflammatory has a thin one. So a gene that alters smooth muscle behaviour can change the risk of a heart attack without changing the size of a plaque at all.

TCF21: a gene that protects by promoting change

TCF21 is a transcription factor needed in the embryo to form the cells that become coronary smooth muscle and fibroblasts. A CAD locus lies next to it. In 2019, Robert Wirka, Thomas Quertermous and colleagues combined lineage tracing with single-cell sequencing in mice and in human coronary arteries. They found that modulated smooth muscle cells mostly become a fibroblast-like cell, which they named the fibromyocyte, and that these cells populate the fibrous cap. Deleting Tcf21 in smooth muscle cells reduced the number of fibromyocytes and left fewer cells in the cap. In human data, alleles that increase TCF21 expression are associated with lower coronary risk.

This reversed an older assumption. Loss of the contractile state had been seen as harmful in itself. The TCF21 work shows that one form of modulation is protective, because it supplies the cells that stabilise the plaque.

ADAMTS7: plaque growth without an effect on rupture

ADAMTS7 encodes a secreted enzyme that cuts proteins of the extracellular matrix. It was found in 2011 by a study with an unusual design. Muredach Reilly and colleagues compared people with coronary plaque on angiography against people with clean arteries, and separately compared, among people who all had plaque, those who had suffered a heart attack against those who had not. ADAMTS7 was associated with having plaque. It was not associated with heart attack among those with plaque.

The biology fits. ADAMTS7 breaks down matrix proteins that restrain smooth muscle cells, allowing them to migrate. Mice lacking the gene develop less atherosclerosis and less thickening of the wall after injury. A coding variant in humans that impairs maturation of the enzyme is associated with lower risk. The direction is consistent throughout: less ADAMTS7 activity, less disease. Since the protein is an enzyme acting outside the cell, it is a candidate drug target, and inhibitors and vaccines against it have been explored in animals.

PHACTR1 and EDN1: one variant, five diseases

A common variant in an intron of PHACTR1 on chromosome 6, rs9349379, is one of the strongest CAD signals. The same variant is associated with migraine, cervical artery dissection, fibromuscular dysplasia and hypertension. The allele that raises coronary risk lowers the risk of the other conditions. For spontaneous coronary artery dissection the pattern is the same: the allele that protects against coronary disease raises the risk of dissection.

In 2017, Rajat Gupta, Sekar Kathiresan and colleagues reported that the variant lies in an enhancer that regulates, from a distance of about 600,000 bases, the gene EDN1. Its product, endothelin-1, is a powerful constrictor of blood vessels made by endothelial cells and promotes smooth muscle proliferation. More endothelin-1 would plausibly favour atherosclerosis and protect against diseases of a fragile or over-dilated wall.

The assignment is disputed. Other groups editing the variant in stem-cell-derived endothelial cells found a change in PHACTR1 expression and none in EDN1. PHACTR1 itself regulates the actin cytoskeleton and could act in endothelial or smooth muscle cells. At present the locus is best described as certainly vascular, with the responsible gene unresolved.

Opposite directions are informative

When one allele raises the risk of an occlusive disease and lowers the risk of a dissecting one, it suggests a single underlying property of the wall, such as tone or stiffness, with a different optimum for each disease. It also warns that a drug acting on that property could trade one risk for another.

The endothelium and nitric oxide

The endothelium is the single layer of cells between blood and the artery wall. It decides what gets in, senses flow, and sends a signal, nitric oxide, that keeps the artery relaxed and platelets quiet. Several CAD genes lie along that one signalling route.

What the endothelium does

A healthy endothelium does four things that matter here. It is a selective barrier, limiting the passage of lipoproteins into the intima. It presents a surface to which blood cells do not stick. It produces substances that relax the smooth muscle beneath it. And it senses the mechanical force of flowing blood.

That last function explains where plaques form. Where flow is fast and steady, endothelial cells switch on a protective programme of genes. At branch points and inner curves, flow is slow and changes direction. There the protective programme is off, the cells are more permeable and more inflammatory, and that is where plaques appear first. The coronary arteries, which branch repeatedly and are squeezed with every heartbeat, have many such sites.

When the endothelium is activated, by disturbed flow, retained lipoproteins or inflammatory signals, it displays adhesion molecules, captures monocytes and lets them through. "Endothelial dysfunction" is the general term for this loss of the normal protective state.

The nitric oxide pathway

The main relaxing signal is nitric oxide (NO), a gas made in endothelial cells by the enzyme endothelial nitric oxide synthase, encoded by NOS3. NO diffuses into neighbouring smooth muscle cells and into platelets passing in the blood. In both it activates soluble guanylyl cyclase, which makes the messenger molecule cGMP. In smooth muscle, cGMP causes relaxation and widens the artery. In platelets it suppresses activation. Enzymes called phosphodiesterases then break cGMP down and end the signal.

The genetic evidence

A family. In 2013, Jeanette Erdmann, Heribert Schunkert and colleagues studied a large family in which many members had heart attacks. Sequencing found that affected members had inherited two rare variants together: one that truncates GUCY1A1 (then called GUCY1A3), the gene for a subunit of soluble guanylyl cyclase, and one in CCT7, a chaperone that helps the enzyme fold. Either alone lowered the amount of enzyme. Together they lowered it further, and platelets from mice lacking the gene formed clots more readily.

Common variants. GWAS have found independent signals at NOS3, at GUCY1A1, and at PDE5A. These are three steps of the same pathway, which is strong evidence that the pathway itself, not some unrelated function of one gene, is what matters.

A combined test. Connor Emdin and colleagues built a genetic score from variants in NOS3 and GUCY1A1 that increase NO signalling. People with a higher score had lower blood pressure and lower risks of coronary disease, peripheral artery disease and stroke. The reduction in coronary disease was larger than the fall in blood pressure would explain, which suggests that NO signalling protects in other ways too, plausibly through its effects on platelets and on the wall.

Drugs already exist for this pathway

Nitrates, used for angina since the nineteenth century, release NO. Stimulators of soluble guanylyl cyclase are used for pulmonary hypertension and heart failure. PDE5 inhibitors such as sildenafil prolong the cGMP signal. The genetics predicts that enhancing this pathway over the long term should prevent coronary events. No trial has yet tested that directly.

A hint at tailored treatment

The common risk variant at GUCY1A1 lowers expression of the enzyme in platelets, making them easier to activate. Analyses of two large aspirin trials found that people who carried the risk genotype appeared to benefit from aspirin, while people without it had more harm than benefit. This was a retrospective finding and needs confirmation, but it shows the kind of use to which a mechanistic understanding of a locus could be put.

Many loci, one endothelial programme

Testing genes one locus at a time is slow. In 2024, Gavin Schnitzler, Jesse Engreitz and colleagues took a different approach. They switched off, one by one, every gene near every CAD locus in cultured human endothelial cells and read out the effect on gene expression in single cells. Genes from 43 separate CAD signals turned out to affect the same set of expression programmes, centred on the cerebral cavernous malformation (CCM) signalling pathway, which controls how endothelial cells respond to flow and maintain their barrier. The study identified a previously unstudied gene, TLNRD1, as a regulator in that pathway. It suggests that many endothelial loci of small effect add up to changes in one cellular function.

Blood pressure

High blood pressure damages the endothelium and drives lipoproteins into the wall. Many of the loci shared between blood pressure and coronary disease act in the vessel wall or the kidney. Mendelian randomization using blood pressure variants shows a clear causal effect on coronary disease, consistent with the trials of blood-pressure-lowering drugs.

  • Genetically stronger NO signalling lowers blood pressure and coronary risk.

Inflammation and clonal haematopoiesis

A plaque is full of immune cells, and markers of inflammation in the blood predict heart attacks. Genetics identified which part of the inflammatory cascade is causal, and uncovered a new risk factor that is genetic but not inherited: mutations acquired in blood stem cells with age.

Inflammation in the plaque

Macrophages in a plaque take up modified lipoproteins and cholesterol crystals. These activate an intracellular sensor, the NLRP3 inflammasome, which releases the active form of the signalling protein interleukin-1β (IL-1β). IL-1β stimulates many cell types to make interleukin-6 (IL-6). IL-6 travels in the blood to the liver, where it switches on production of C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen and other proteins of the acute-phase response.

CRP is easy to measure and people with higher levels have more heart attacks. For years the open question was where in this chain, if anywhere, the causal step lies.

CRP is a marker

Several variants in the CRP gene change the blood level of CRP and nothing else. If CRP caused coronary disease, carriers of CRP-raising variants would have more of it. Large studies in Copenhagen and in an international collaboration of over 190,000 people found no such effect. CRP rises because the cascade upstream is active.

The IL-6 receptor is causal

A common missense variant in IL6R, Asp358Ala, causes the receptor to be shed from the cell surface more readily. Cells with fewer receptors respond less to IL-6. Carriers have lower CRP and fibrinogen, and higher circulating IL-6 because less of it is taken up. This is the same pattern produced by tocilizumab, an antibody against the receptor used for rheumatoid arthritis.

In 2012, two consortia published analyses of this variant side by side. Each copy of the 358Ala allele was associated with a 3–5% lower risk of coronary heart disease. The effect is small, as expected for a variant that only slightly reduces signalling, but it was consistent across dozens of studies, and it established the IL-6 pathway as a cause.

The trials

CANTOS (2017) tested the hypothesis directly. About 10,000 people with a previous heart attack and raised CRP received canakinumab, an antibody against IL-1β, or placebo. The drug did not change lipids at all. At the 150 mg dose, cardiovascular events fell by 15%. The benefit tracked the reduction in IL-6 and CRP. Fatal infections were more frequent, the expected cost of suppressing a defence pathway.

CANTOS was the first proof that reducing inflammation alone prevents coronary events. Not all anti-inflammatory drugs work: low-dose methotrexate, which did not lower IL-6 or CRP, had no effect in a large trial. Colchicine, an old and inexpensive drug that interferes with inflammasome activation and white cell function, reduced events in two trials and is now approved for cardiovascular prevention. Antibodies against IL-6 itself are in outcome trials.

Other immune loci

GWAS signals also implicate CXCL12, a chemokine that governs the movement of blood progenitor and immune cells, and SH2B3, a brake on blood cell production whose risk variant is also associated with higher blood counts, blood pressure and several autoimmune diseases. Loci near LIPA and TRIB1 affect how macrophages and liver cells process lipid. Compared with the smooth muscle and lipid loci, the immune signals in CAD GWAS are fewer than the abundance of immune cells in plaque might lead one to expect.

Clonal haematopoiesis: acquired mutations in blood

Blood is renewed throughout life from a pool of stem cells in the bone marrow. Each stem cell accumulates mutations as it ages. Occasionally a mutation gives one stem cell a growth advantage, and its descendants come to make up a noticeable fraction of all blood cells. When this happens in a person with normal blood counts and no blood cancer, it is called clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP). Most cases involve one of a few genes: DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1 or JAK2.

CHIP was first studied as a precursor of leukaemia. In 2014, Siddhartha Jaiswal, Benjamin Ebert and colleagues noticed that carriers died more often than non-carriers and that the excess was mostly cardiovascular. A follow-up study in 2017 reported that CHIP carriers had 1.9 times the risk of coronary heart disease and 4 times the risk of a heart attack before 50.

The mechanism was demonstrated in mice. When a fraction of the bone marrow of atherosclerosis-prone mice was replaced with cells lacking Tet2, plaques grew faster. Macrophages without Tet2 produced more IL-1β and IL-6, and blocking the NLRP3 inflammasome removed the extra plaque. So the mutant clone supplies the plaque with overactive inflammatory cells.

How strong is the effect in people?

Later and larger studies have given smaller estimates than the first reports. In one analysis of about 35,000 people the hazard ratio for cardiovascular events was about 1.3 for CHIP overall and about 1.6 for large clones, and risk appears to differ by gene, being clearer for TET2 and JAK2 than for DNMT3A. That same analysis found that carriers of large clones who also carried the IL6R 358Ala allele were protected, which would point to IL-6 blockade as a treatment for this group. A larger study in UK Biobank did not reproduce that interaction. The association of CHIP with cardiovascular disease is real, but its size, and whether it can be treated through IL-6, are not settled.

CHIP matters conceptually for another reason. It is a genetic cause of disease that is not inherited and so is invisible to studies of the genome a person was born with. Risk is shaped by the genome's changes over a lifetime as well as by its starting sequence.

Key points

  • Lipid in the plaque activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to IL-1β, then IL-6, then CRP.
  • Variants that alter CRP alone do not change risk. A variant that dampens IL-6 receptor signalling lowers it.
  • Blocking IL-1β reduced cardiovascular events without changing lipids; colchicine also reduces events.
  • CHIP, the age-related expansion of mutated blood stem cell clones, is associated with coronary disease, and Tet2-deficient macrophages accelerate plaque in mice through the inflammasome.
  • The size of the CHIP effect in humans and its interaction with IL-6 signalling remain uncertain.

Thrombosis

A plaque can sit in an artery for decades. What turns it into a heart attack is a clot. A small set of genes affects that final step, and they are different from the genes that build the plaque.

How a clot forms on a plaque

When a plaque ruptures or its surface erodes, blood meets material it never normally touches. Two systems respond within seconds.

Platelets stick to exposed collagen. The large protein von Willebrand factor (VWF) acts as the glue, especially where blood flows fast, as it does through a narrowed coronary artery. Stuck platelets become activated and recruit more.

The coagulation cascade is triggered by tissue factor, which is abundant in the plaque's core. A chain of clotting factors activates one another and ends with thrombin converting fibrinogen into strands of fibrin, which bind the platelets into a solid mass.

Opposing systems limit the clot. Protein C, activated on the endothelial surface, switches off clotting factors. Plasmin dissolves fibrin. Nitric oxide and prostacyclin from healthy endothelium keep platelets quiet. Whether a ruptured plaque produces a small sealed-over clot or one that blocks the artery depends on the balance.

Separating plaque from clot

The study by Reilly and colleagues described in Chapter 8 is the key piece of evidence. Among people who all had coronary plaque on angiography, those who had suffered a heart attack differed from those who had not at one locus: the ABO blood group gene. ABO was not associated with having plaque in the first place. This is direct evidence that the genetics of clotting on a plaque is distinct from the genetics of forming one.

ABO blood group

The ABO gene encodes an enzyme that attaches sugars to proteins on cell surfaces and in blood. The A and B forms attach different sugars. The O form is inactive. One of the proteins modified in this way is VWF. In people with blood group O, VWF is cleared from the blood faster, and their VWF levels are about a quarter lower than in people of other groups. Factor VIII, a clotting factor that VWF carries and protects, is lower too.

People with non-O blood groups have a modestly higher risk of heart attack and a larger increase in the risk of venous thrombosis. The ABO locus also affects the levels of many other blood proteins, including adhesion molecules, and has a small effect on LDL cholesterol, so VWF may not be the whole explanation.

Clotting factor variants

Two well-known variants raise the tendency to clot. Factor V Leiden makes factor V resistant to being switched off by activated protein C. The prothrombin G20210A variant raises the level of prothrombin. Each is carried by a few percent of Europeans. Both increase the risk of venous thrombosis several-fold. Their effect on coronary disease is much smaller, an increase in risk of the order of 10–30%.

The contrast is informative. Clots in veins form in slow-moving blood and are made largely of fibrin and red cells, so they depend heavily on the coagulation cascade. Clots in arteries form at high flow on a ruptured plaque and are rich in platelets. That is why arterial thrombosis is prevented mainly with antiplatelet drugs and venous thrombosis with anticoagulants.

A CAD locus at PROCR, the gene for the endothelial receptor that helps activate protein C, links the natural anticoagulant system to coronary risk. The risk variant has opposite effects on venous thrombosis and on coronary disease, which suggests that the receptor's anti-inflammatory signalling matters in arteries as well as its effect on clotting.

Platelets

The nitric oxide pathway of Chapter 9 acts in platelets as well as in smooth muscle. The GUCY1A1 risk variant lowers the amount of soluble guanylyl cyclase in platelets, so the normal inhibitory signal from the endothelium is weaker and platelets are more easily activated. This is the best-characterised platelet mechanism among the CAD loci. Other loci influence platelet count and size, and some of those are associated with coronary disease.

Why few clotting genes appear in CAD GWAS

The clinical evidence that clotting matters is overwhelming: aspirin and other antiplatelet drugs prevent heart attacks. Yet thrombosis loci are a small minority of CAD signals. There are two likely reasons. Studies define cases broadly, and many cases have plaque without having had a clot, which dilutes the signal for genes that act only on the final step. And strongly pro-thrombotic variants may be kept rare by selection. Studies that separate heart attack from stable disease are better placed to find these genes.

The genetic view of antithrombotic drugs

Every drug that prevents clotting also causes bleeding. Genetics can indicate where the balance is favourable. People with low levels of factor XI, through inherited deficiency or common variants, have less venous thrombosis and less ischaemic stroke with little spontaneous bleeding. That observation led to the development of factor XI inhibitors as anticoagulants intended to carry a lower bleeding risk. Their value in coronary disease specifically is still under study.

Key points

  • A heart attack happens when platelets and the coagulation cascade build a clot on a ruptured or eroded plaque.
  • ABO is associated with heart attack among people who already have plaque, not with plaque itself. Non-O groups have higher VWF and factor VIII.
  • Factor V Leiden and prothrombin G20210A strongly raise venous thrombosis risk and only modestly raise coronary risk, reflecting the platelet-rich nature of arterial clots.
  • The GUCY1A1 variant makes platelets easier to activate.
  • Thrombosis genes are under-represented in CAD GWAS partly because of how cases are defined.

Which risk factors are causal

Hundreds of measurements predict coronary disease. Only some of them cause it. Mendelian randomization has now been applied to nearly all the candidates, and its verdicts have matched the results of drug trials closely enough to be used to decide which trials to run.

The test

For each proposed risk factor, find genetic variants that change it. Ask whether people who carry those variants have more or less coronary disease, in proportion to the change in the factor. If they do, and if the variants do not act on disease by another route, the factor is causal. The method and its assumptions are described in Genes in Populations. Here are the results.

Cases worth knowing in detail

Homocysteine

People with high blood homocysteine have more vascular disease, and folic acid lowers homocysteine cheaply. A common variant in MTHFR raises homocysteine for life. In populations with adequate folate, carriers have no more coronary disease. Large trials of folate and B vitamins lowered homocysteine and did not reduce events.

Lp-PLA2

Lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2 is an enzyme found in plaques, and its activity in blood predicts events. A loss-of-function variant in its gene, PLA2G7, is common in East Asia; some people have no enzyme activity at all. In about 90,000 Chinese adults, carriers had no reduction in vascular disease. Two outcome trials of darapladib, an inhibitor of the enzyme, enrolled about 29,000 patients and found no benefit. Here the genetic experiment and the pharmacological one gave the same answer, and the genetic one was far cheaper.

Alcohol

Moderate drinkers have fewer heart attacks than non-drinkers in observational studies. But the group of non-drinkers includes people who stopped because of illness, and moderate drinkers differ from others in many ways. Variants in the alcohol-metabolising genes ADH1B and ALDH2 cause unpleasant reactions to alcohol, and carriers drink less throughout life. They do not have more cardiovascular disease, as the protective hypothesis would predict. If anything they have lower blood pressure and less stroke.

Height

Shorter people have more coronary disease. A genetic analysis found that each standard deviation (about 6.5 cm) of genetically shorter height was associated with a 13.5% higher risk. Only part of this is explained by lipids. Shared growth pathways acting on the vessel wall, and the smaller calibre of the arteries, have been suggested. It is a reminder that a causal factor need not be something one could or would treat.

Obesity and diabetes

Both are causal, and both act partly through other factors on this list: blood pressure, triglyceride-rich particles, inflammation. Genetics has also separated different kinds of adiposity. Variants that favour fat storage under the skin of the hips and thighs are associated with better metabolic health and lower coronary risk than variants that favour fat in the abdomen and liver, for the same body mass index.

Drug side effects predicted the same way

The same logic applies to unwanted effects. Variants in HMGCR that mimic statins are associated with slightly higher body weight and a slightly higher risk of type 2 diabetes. Statin trials show the same small increase in diabetes. So that effect is a consequence of inhibiting the target, not a property of particular molecules. Variants that lower LDL through PCSK9 show a similar small association with diabetes.

Reading the scoreboard with care

  • "Causal" means lifelong. Genetic estimates reflect exposure from birth. They say that the factor contributes to disease, and are usually larger than what a few years of treatment achieves. The Lp(a) trial is a warning that late intervention may not reproduce the effect.
  • "Not causal" refers to the measured quantity. HDL cholesterol concentration is not causal. That does not exclude a role for some aspect of HDL function that the genetic instruments do not capture.
  • Instruments vary in quality. Verdicts based on variants in the gene encoding the biomarker (CRP, LPA, IL6R, PLA2G7) are the most reliable. Verdicts based on hundreds of variants scattered across the genome, as for body mass index, are more exposed to pleiotropy.
  • Correlated factors need to be modelled together. LDL cholesterol, ApoB and triglycerides share many variants. Analyses that include several at once (multivariable MR) are needed to say which one carries the effect, and for the lipid traits they point to ApoB.

Key points

  • Genetically supported causes of coronary disease include ApoB-containing lipoproteins, Lp(a), blood pressure, adiposity, type 2 diabetes, smoking and IL-6 signalling.
  • HDL cholesterol, CRP, homocysteine, Lp-PLA2 and vitamin D are markers, and drugs aimed at them failed.
  • Agreement between genetic verdicts and trial results is high, which is why genetic support is now sought before a target is taken into development.
  • Genetic estimates describe lifelong exposure and do not guarantee the effect of late treatment.

From gene to medicine

Coronary disease is the field in which human genetics has most clearly paid for itself in new drugs. The route from a family or a loss-of-function carrier to an approved medicine is now well enough travelled to describe as a method, with known strengths and one recent failure.

Why genetic evidence predicts drug success

Most drugs that enter clinical trials fail, and the commonest reason is that the target turns out not to matter for the disease. A human genetic association is evidence, obtained before any drug is made, that changing the target changes the disease in people. Analyses across the pharmaceutical industry have found that drug programmes with genetic support for the target are about twice as likely to reach approval as those without.

The ideal genetic evidence has three parts:

  1. Direction. Loss of function lowers risk, so an inhibitor is wanted. Inhibitors are much easier to make than activators.
  2. Dose–response. Variants with a range of effects on the gene, from mild common ones to complete loss, show a graded effect on disease. This is called an allelic series, and it predicts how much inhibition gives how much benefit.
  3. Safety. People with complete loss of the gene are healthy, and a scan of carriers' medical records across hundreds of diseases shows no unexpected harm.

PCSK9 had all three. That is why it moved so fast.

The newer kinds of medicine

Many of these targets are proteins made in the liver and secreted into blood. That suits three technologies that act on the gene's message or on the gene.

Antisense oligonucleotides are short strands of modified DNA that bind a messenger RNA and cause it to be destroyed. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) do the same through the cell's own RNA interference machinery. Both can be tagged with a sugar, GalNAc, that liver cells take up avidly, so small doses given every few weeks or months are enough. Inclisiran, against PCSK9, is given twice a year.

Gene editing goes a step further. A base editor delivered to the liver in a lipid nanoparticle can change a single letter in PCSK9 and switch the gene off permanently. The first patients, with familial hypercholesterolaemia, were treated in 2022, and LDL cholesterol fell substantially after one infusion. The rationale comes straight from genetics: people born without PCSK9 are healthy, so permanent loss should be safe, and Chapter 3 showed that lifelong lowering gives the largest benefit. The open questions are about delivery, editing at unintended sites and long-term safety, and they will take years of follow-up to answer.

What genetics predicts about magnitude

A recurring mistake is to expect a trial to reproduce the genetic effect size. It will not, for two reasons already met.

First, a genetic effect accumulates over a lifetime. A trial lasts a few years. For LDL the ratio is about three to one.

Second, the variant and the drug may differ in how strongly they act. The IL6R variant lowers risk by a few percent because it reduces signalling slightly. A drug that blocks the pathway almost completely could do much more. The size of a genetic association says how strong the variant is. It does not put a ceiling on the drug.

The useful quantity is the ratio: how much disease risk changes per unit change in the biomarker. That ratio, taken from genetics, can be used to predict what a drug with a known effect on the biomarker will achieve, as was done for Lp(a).

When it does not work

The pelacarsen trial is the first clear case in this field of a target with strong genetic support and a potent, well-tolerated drug that gave no benefit. Chapter 6 lists the possible reasons. The general lesson is about what genetic evidence covers. It establishes that the target is on the causal path to disease over a lifetime. It does not establish that the damage is reversible, or at what stage of disease intervention must begin. For the artery-wall targets in Part III, where the biology concerns how plaques are built over decades, that question of timing may be even more important.

Predicting harm

Genetics has also stopped programmes or shaped them. Loss of APOB or MTTP causes fatty liver, and so do the drugs. Statin-mimicking variants predicted the small increase in diabetes. The PHACTR1/EDN1 allele that protects against coronary disease raises the risk of arterial dissection. Looking up what else a protective variant is associated with, across all recorded diagnoses in a biobank, has become a standard early check on a target.

The untreated part of the disease

Every approved drug in the table acts on lipoproteins, clotting, blood pressure or, recently, inflammation. None acts on smooth muscle cells or the endothelium to change how a plaque is built. Yet that is where most of the genetic signal lies. People who are treated to very low LDL levels still have heart attacks, a problem known as residual risk. Targets such as ADAMTS7 and the nitric oxide pathway are attempts to address it. Turning the artery-wall loci into medicines is the main unfinished task that genetics has set for the field.

Key points

  • Genetic support roughly doubles a drug target's chance of success. The strongest evidence shows direction, dose–response and safety.
  • Statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, bempedoic acid, ANGPTL3 and APOC3 inhibitors all act on targets validated by human genetics.
  • Liver-made targets can be silenced with antisense, siRNA or permanent gene editing.
  • Trials should be expected to show smaller effects than lifelong genetic exposure, and genetic support does not guarantee that late treatment works.
  • No current drug targets the artery wall itself, where most CAD loci act.

Genetic risk in the individual

A person's inherited risk of coronary disease comes in two forms: rarely, one variant of large effect, and for everyone, the sum of many small ones. Both can now be measured. What to do with the measurement is a harder question.

Monogenic risk

About 1 in 250 people carries a variant causing familial hypercholesterolaemia. Finding one changes management: treatment starts earlier and is more intensive, and relatives can be tested, since each first-degree relative has a one in two chance of carrying it.

Sequencing has shown that the variant carries information beyond the cholesterol level. In a study of people with LDL cholesterol of 190 mg/dL or more, those with an FH variant had about 22 times the coronary risk of people with normal LDL, while those with the same high LDL and no variant had about 6 times the risk. The explanation is cumulative exposure: the variant marks an LDL level that has been high since birth. The same study found that under 2% of people with LDL this high carried an FH variant. Most severe hypercholesterolaemia is polygenic.

Polygenic risk

A polygenic risk score adds up a person's risk alleles across the genome, each weighted by its effect in a GWAS. Modern scores for coronary disease use millions of variants. The score is fixed at conception, costs little to measure, and is largely independent of the standard risk factors and of family history as usually recorded.

In 2018, Amit Khera, Sekar Kathiresan and colleagues showed that 8% of people in UK Biobank had a polygenic score high enough to give them at least three times the average risk. That is the level of risk conferred by an FH variant, and it applies to twenty times as many people. Most of them have unremarkable cholesterol and no other sign of their risk.

Genetic risk is not fixed risk

A high score raises the starting point. It does not remove the effect of everything else. In three cohorts followed for many years, people in the top fifth of polygenic risk had about 90% more coronary events than those in the bottom fifth. Within every level of genetic risk, those with a favourable lifestyle (not smoking, not obese, physically active, a healthy diet) had about half the risk of those with an unfavourable one.

The same holds for drugs. Reanalysis of statin trials by genetic risk found that people in the highest genetic risk group had the largest reduction in events, in relative as well as absolute terms. A high polygenic score identifies people who have the most to gain from prevention.

How the two kinds of risk combine

Polygenic background modifies monogenic risk. Among carriers of an FH variant, those with a low polygenic score have a much lower chance of developing coronary disease than those with a high one. This explains part of the old observation that the same variant can cause early heart attacks in one relative and little disease in another.

Limits

Modest gain over existing tools. Clinical risk calculators use age, sex, cholesterol, blood pressure, smoking and diabetes. Adding a polygenic score improves their discrimination a little: in one large evaluation the C statistic rose from 0.76 to 0.78. Age dominates any such calculation in middle-aged and older people. The score is most informative early in life, before risk factors have appeared and before age takes over, and that is also when prevention has the longest time to act.

Unequal accuracy across ancestries. Scores are built from GWAS, and most GWAS participants have been of European ancestry. Because of differences in allele frequency and linkage disequilibrium, a score trained in one population predicts less well in others, and worst in people of African ancestry. Multi-ancestry studies such as the Million Veteran Program and Biobank Japan have reduced the gap. They have not closed it.

A probability, not a diagnosis. Most people with a high score will not have a heart attack in the next ten years, and many people with an average score will. The score shifts the odds.

No statement about mechanism. Two people with the same total score may have reached it by different routes, one mainly through lipid variants and another through variants acting in the artery wall. A single number hides that.

Scores by pathway

That last limitation is also an opportunity. The variants in a score can be grouped by the biology they act on, using the kind of evidence described in earlier chapters, to give separate scores for LDL, triglyceride-rich particles, Lp(a), blood pressure, inflammation, smooth muscle biology and so on. In principle this would describe a person's disease by mechanism and might indicate which treatment fits them. The idea is under active study. So far the evidence that pathway scores predict response to a specific therapy is limited, with the partial exception of LDL-related risk and lipid lowering. Showing that mechanistic subtypes of coronary disease exist and matter for treatment is one of the open problems taken up in the final chapter.

Key points

  • FH variants carry more risk than the LDL level alone indicates, because they mark lifelong exposure.
  • About 8% of people have a polygenic score giving at least threefold risk, comparable to a monogenic variant.
  • High genetic risk is offset by lifestyle and by statins, and those at highest genetic risk gain the most.
  • Polygenic scores add modestly to clinical risk calculators, work less well outside European ancestry, and are most useful early in life.
  • Splitting scores by biological pathway may reveal mechanistic subtypes, but this is not yet established.

Seeing the disease: coronary CT

Genetics describes risk a person is born with. A CT scan shows what has actually happened in their arteries. Three measurements taken from the same scan, plaque composition, the pressure drop across a narrowing, and the state of the fat around the artery, each report on a different part of the biology in this book.

What a coronary CT scan shows

CT measures how strongly tissue absorbs X-rays, in Hounsfield units (HU). Water is 0, air is −1000, fat is around −100, and calcium is several hundred or more. Two kinds of scan are used for the coronary arteries.

A calcium scan uses no contrast agent. It detects calcified plaque and adds it up into the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score. The score is a measure of how much atherosclerosis a person has accumulated. A score of zero in middle age indicates a low risk over the following decade.

Coronary CT angiography (CCTA) is done after injecting iodine contrast, which makes blood bright. The lumen can then be seen, and so can the wall around it, including plaque that contains no calcium. For decades the only routine way to look at coronary arteries was invasive angiography, which shows the lumen and nothing of the wall. CCTA shows the disease itself.

That it matters for patients was shown in the SCOT-HEART trial. About 4,100 people with stable chest pain were randomly assigned to standard care or to standard care plus CCTA. After five years, death from coronary disease or non-fatal heart attack had occurred in 2.3% of the CCTA group and 3.9% of the standard-care group. Rates of invasive procedures were similar. The benefit came mainly from starting preventive drugs in people whose scans showed plaque.

Plaque composition and AI quantification

Chapter 1 explained that risk depends on what a plaque is made of. On CCTA, the components differ in attenuation, so they can be told apart voxel by voxel.

Radiologists also recognise high-risk plaque features by eye: low-attenuation plaque, outward expansion of the vessel (positive remodelling), small flecks of calcium, and a ring of higher attenuation around a dark centre known as the napkin-ring sign. These correspond to the thin-capped, lipid-rich plaque that pathologists find at the site of ruptures.

Measuring plaque volume by hand means tracing the inner and outer boundaries of the wall along every artery. It takes an expert about half an hour per patient and varies between readers, so it was confined to research. Deep learning changed that. A network trained on expert-traced scans segments the lumen and wall and classifies every voxel of plaque.

In an international study, such a system was trained on 921 patients. Its total plaque volumes agreed closely with expert readers and with intravascular ultrasound, the invasive reference method (intraclass correlations of about 0.95). The analysis took under six seconds per patient against about 26 minutes for an expert. Applied to SCOT-HEART participants, a total plaque volume above about 240 mm³ was associated with five times the risk of heart attack, after accounting for the clinical risk score and for obstructive narrowing.

An earlier analysis of the same trial, using semi-automated software, looked at the lipid-rich component specifically. Low-attenuation plaque burden was the strongest predictor of heart attack, ahead of the clinical risk score, the calcium score and the severity of narrowing. Patients in whom low-attenuation plaque made up more than 4% of vessel volume were nearly five times as likely to have a heart attack.

Connection to the biology

Low-attenuation plaque is the necrotic core of Chapter 1 made visible. Calcified plaque is older, more stable disease. Serial CT studies report that statins shrink the low-attenuation component while calcification increases, which is consistent with plaques becoming more stable as lipid is removed. Imaging is therefore measuring the plaque stability that the smooth muscle genes of Chapter 8 are thought to influence.

CT-derived fractional flow reserve

A narrowing matters for symptoms only if it limits blood flow, and anatomy predicts this poorly. Many narrowings that look severe do not limit flow, and some that look moderate do.

The invasive test of this is fractional flow reserve (FFR). A pressure-sensing wire is passed beyond the narrowing, a drug is given to dilate the small vessels of the heart maximally, and the pressure beyond the narrowing is divided by the pressure in the aorta:

\[ \mathrm{FFR} = \frac{P_{\text{distal}}}{P_{\text{aortic}}} \quad \text{during maximal flow}. \]

A value of 1 means no obstruction. A value of 0.80 or below is taken to mean that the narrowing limits flow enough to cause ischaemia. Trials showed that placing stents only in arteries with a low FFR gives better outcomes than stenting everything that looks narrow.

CT-derived FFR (FFR-CT or CT-FFR) estimates the same number without a wire and without the drug. The CCTA images are turned into a three-dimensional model of the coronary tree. The equations of fluid flow are then solved in that model by computational fluid dynamics. The inflow and outflow conditions are set from physiological rules: total coronary flow at rest scales with the mass of heart muscle, the flow each branch carries scales with its size, and the fall in resistance during maximal dilation is assumed from known values. The output is an FFR value at every point in every artery. Newer versions replace the full simulation with machine-learning models trained to reproduce it, which run in minutes on site.

The practical value is in avoiding unnecessary invasive tests. In one prospective study of patients already scheduled for invasive angiography, 73% of those managed in the usual way turned out to have no obstructive disease. In the group assessed first by CCTA and CT-FFR, the invasive test was cancelled in 61%, and only 12% of those who went ahead had no obstruction.

The method has limits. It depends on image quality and is less reliable with heavy calcification, stents or motion. Values close to the 0.80 threshold are uncertain. And it answers a question about narrowing, which Chapter 1 distinguished from the question of which plaque will rupture.

The fat attenuation index

The third measurement looks outside the artery. Coronary arteries are surrounded by fat, the perivascular adipose tissue. In 2017, Charalambos Antoniades and colleagues in Oxford showed that an inflamed artery wall changes this fat. Inflammatory signals released by the wall diffuse outward and stop the nearby precursor fat cells from maturing and filling with lipid. Fat next to an inflamed artery therefore contains smaller cells with less lipid and relatively more water.

Lipid has a very low CT attenuation and water a higher one. So fat around an inflamed artery is slightly less negative on CT than fat around a healthy one. The fat attenuation index (FAI) is the average attenuation of the fat voxels in a defined shell around a segment of coronary artery. The group validated it against tissue taken at heart surgery and against PET imaging of inflammation, and found that it rose around the culprit lesion in patients with acute coronary syndromes and fell afterwards.

Does it predict outcomes?

The CRISP-CT study measured FAI in about 3,900 people who had undergone CCTA in Germany and the United States and had been followed for several years. A high FAI around the right coronary artery predicted death from cardiac causes after adjustment for risk factors, the extent of coronary disease and high-risk plaque features. Above a cutoff of −70.1 HU, the risk of cardiac death was about nine times higher in the first cohort and about five to six times higher in the second.

The ORFAN study extended this to about 40,000 people having routine CCTA in eight British hospitals. Two findings stand out. First, 81% of patients had no obstructive narrowing, and they accounted for about two-thirds of the heart attacks, new heart failure and cardiac deaths that followed. Most events occur in people whom a scan read only for narrowing would have reassured. Second, an FAI-based score, standardised for age, sex and scan settings, predicted cardiac death and events independently of risk factors and of the amount of plaque. A model combining the score with plaque measurements and clinical factors was well calibrated against the events that occurred.

What is established and what is not
  • Established: perivascular fat attenuation is associated with later cardiac events in several large cohorts, in addition to what plaque and risk factors predict.
  • Technical dependence: the raw value shifts with tube voltage, scanner, reconstruction method and the artery measured. Values from different centres cannot be compared without standardisation, which is why a proprietary adjusted score is used clinically.
  • Not established: that changing treatment on the basis of FAI improves outcomes. The evidence is observational. Smaller studies report that FAI falls after statins and after anti-inflammatory biological drugs, but no randomised trial has tested an FAI-guided strategy.

Where imaging meets genetics

Chapter 2 noted that a GWAS of "coronary disease" mixes plaque formation, plaque stability and clotting. Imaging measurements are a way to take that apart, because each is closer to one biological process than the clinical diagnosis is.

Calcium. The calcium score has been studied by GWAS for over a decade. The first loci found were ones already known for coronary disease, including 9p21 and PHACTR1, which showed that those loci act on the build-up of plaque. A multi-ancestry study of about 36,000 people identified 11 loci. Five of them had not been reported for coronary disease, and several pointed to genes of bone mineralisation and phosphate metabolism that regulate calcification by smooth muscle cells. This is the bone-like cell fate of Chapter 8 seen through genetics.

Plaque composition, flow and perivascular fat. Genetic studies of non-calcified plaque volume, low-attenuation plaque, CT-FFR and FAI are far smaller, because until recently these could not be measured in tens of thousands of people. Automated analysis removes that obstacle. Biobanks that link CT scans to genotypes can now treat each measurement as a trait in its own right.

Several questions follow that have not been answered. Do the smooth muscle loci associate with plaque composition more than with plaque amount? Do the IL-6 pathway variants or clonal haematopoiesis associate with fat attenuation, as their biology would predict? Do people with the same polygenic score differ in what their scan shows, and does that difference indicate which treatment they need? These are hypotheses. Testing them is one of the more direct routes from the loci of Part III to a use in the clinic.

Key points

  • Coronary CT angiography shows the artery wall as well as the lumen, and its use improved outcomes in a randomised trial.
  • Deep learning measures plaque volume and composition in seconds with accuracy close to experts. Low-attenuation plaque, the lipid-rich core, is a strong predictor of heart attack.
  • CT-FFR computes the pressure drop across narrowings by simulating flow in a model built from the scan. It sharply reduces false positives from anatomy alone.
  • The fat attenuation index detects coronary inflammation through its effect on surrounding fat. It predicts cardiac death in large cohorts, mostly in people without obstructive disease, but has not been tested as a guide to treatment in a trial.
  • Imaging traits separate the biological processes that a clinical diagnosis combines, and are becoming available at the scale genetic studies need.

What is still unknown

Genetics has explained the lipid half of coronary disease in detail and has only outlined the rest. This closing chapter sets out the main open problems, so that the reader can tell settled results from work in progress.

1. Most loci have no confirmed gene

Of the several hundred CAD loci, the causal gene has been established with experimental support at a few dozen. For the remainder there is a list of candidates ranked by statistical evidence. The ranking uses proximity, coding variants, expression and protein associations, chromatin contacts and rare-variant burden, and when several of these agree the candidate is usually right. But "usually" hides many errors, and the PHACTR1/EDN1 dispute shows that even an intensively studied locus can remain unresolved.

The bottleneck is experimental. Each locus needs the right cell type, in the right state, with the right stimulus. Methods that perturb thousands of genes or variants at once in vascular cells and read out the effect in single cells are beginning to change the pace.

2. The right cells in the right state

Many regulatory variants act only under particular conditions: in smooth muscle cells that have begun to modulate, in endothelial cells under disturbed flow, in macrophages loaded with lipid. Expression data from resting tissue miss these effects. This is a likely reason why a large share of CAD signals do not colocalize with any known expression association. Single-cell atlases of human plaque, and of cells under stimulation, are filling in the gap.

3. Plaque formation versus the clinical event

Chapters 8 and 11 described one study that separated the genetics of having plaque from the genetics of having a heart attack. Few studies have followed, because they need detailed imaging or angiography in very large numbers. Genetic studies of coronary calcium, of plaque composition on CT, and of specific events such as plaque erosion are small by GWAS standards. A variant's effect on "CAD" does not say which step it acts on, and that matters for deciding who should receive a drug and when. Automated analysis of CT scans, described in Chapter 15, is beginning to supply these measurements at the scale genetics needs.

4. Timing and reversibility

Genetic effects are lifelong. The Lp(a) trial raised the question sharply: when a causal exposure is removed late, does the risk go away? For LDL, it partly does. For other pathways it is not known. If smooth muscle or endothelial variants act mainly while plaques are forming, drugs against those targets may need to be given early and for a long time, which makes them hard to test in conventional trials of a few years.

5. Populations that have not been studied

Most participants in CAD genetic studies have been of European ancestry, with East Asian populations second. People of South Asian ancestry have high rates of early coronary disease and are under-represented. So are people of African ancestry, in whom fine-mapping is more precise because linkage disequilibrium extends over shorter distances, and in whom variants exist that are absent elsewhere. The PCSK9 loss-of-function variants were found in African Americans. Wider sampling is a matter of fairness and also the most direct way to find new biology.

6. Women

Coronary disease appears about a decade later in women, erosion is a more frequent cause of heart attack than rupture, and conditions such as spontaneous coronary artery dissection are far more common. Most loci have similar effects in both sexes, but a few differ, and the X chromosome was left out of most early analyses. Sex-specific analyses with adequate power are recent.

7. Missing heritability and rare variants

Common variants account for part of the heritability of coronary disease. Sequencing of exomes at biobank scale has confirmed the known lipid genes by rare-variant burden and added few genes outside lipid metabolism. That may be because artery-wall genes are intolerant of loss, so that damaging variants are too rare to detect, or because their effects are specific to a cell type and are diluted when a variant affects the whole body. Whole-genome sequencing of very large cohorts will show how much rare non-coding variation contributes.

8. Acquired mutations

Clonal haematopoiesis showed that mutations acquired in blood affect coronary risk. Mutations also accumulate in the cells of the artery wall, and plaques contain clones of smooth muscle cells descended from one or a few cells. Whether acquired mutations in the wall drive disease is an open question.

9. Are there mechanistic subtypes?

If risk variants fall into distinct pathways, patients may differ in which pathway dominates: one person's disease driven mainly by ApoB particles, another's by the vessel wall, another's by inflammation or clotting. Grouping variants by pathway and scoring people on each is straightforward. Showing that the resulting groups are stable, that they differ in plaque characteristics or outcomes, and above all that they respond differently to treatment, has not yet been done convincingly. Without that last step a subtype is a description and not a guide to care.

10. Residual risk

People treated to very low LDL cholesterol still have events. Inflammation accounts for some of that, as the canakinumab and colchicine trials showed. The rest is presumably the biology of Part III, for which there are no drugs. Closing that gap is where the genetics of the past two decades will be tested.

How to keep up

The fastest-moving areas are single-cell and perturbation studies of vascular cells, multi-ancestry GWAS and sequencing, and outcome trials of RNA-silencing and gene-editing therapies. For any locus, a good first question is which of the four steps from Chapter 2 (variant, gene, cell type, direction) has experimental support, and which is inference.

Key points

  • For most CAD loci the causal gene, cell type and direction are inferred, not demonstrated.
  • Regulatory effects often appear only in specific cell states, which standard expression data miss.
  • The genetics of the final clinical event is far less studied than the genetics of "CAD" as a whole.
  • Whether removing a lifelong exposure late in disease reverses risk must be tested pathway by pathway.
  • Diverse populations, sex differences, rare non-coding variants and acquired mutations are all under-explored.
  • Mechanistic subtypes of coronary disease are a hypothesis awaiting evidence that they change treatment.

Clearance of dead cells by macrophages. When it fails in a plaque, a necrotic core forms. Ch. 1
Endothelium
The single layer of cells lining the inside of blood vessels. Ch. 1, 9
eQTL, pQTL
A variant associated with the expression level of a gene, or with the blood level of a protein. Ch. 2
Fat attenuation index (FAI)
Average CT attenuation of the fat around a coronary artery; higher (less negative) values indicate inflammation of the wall. Ch. 15
Fractional flow reserve (FFR)
Pressure beyond a narrowing divided by aortic pressure at maximal flow; 0.80 or below indicates a flow-limiting narrowing. CT-FFR computes it from a scan. Ch. 15
Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH)
Inherited high LDL cholesterol caused by variants in LDLR, APOB or PCSK9. Ch. 3
Fibromyocyte
A fibroblast-like cell derived from a smooth muscle cell, which contributes to the fibrous cap. Ch. 8
Fibrous cap
The layer of smooth muscle-derived cells and collagen separating a plaque's core from the blood. Ch. 1, 8
Foam cell
A macrophage or smooth muscle-derived cell filled with cholesterol droplets. Ch. 1
Hounsfield unit (HU)
The unit of CT attenuation: water is 0, air −1000, fat about −100. Ch. 15
Intima, media, adventitia
The inner, middle and outer layers of the artery wall. Ch. 1
Kringle IV type 2 repeat
The repeated segment of the LPA gene whose copy number largely determines Lp(a) level. Ch. 6
Lipoprotein lipase (LPL)
The enzyme on capillary walls that removes triglyceride from circulating lipoproteins. Ch. 4
Loss of function
A variant that abolishes the activity of one copy of a gene. Ch. 2
Low-attenuation plaque
Plaque with CT attenuation below about 30 HU, corresponding to lipid-rich necrotic core. Ch. 15
Mendelian randomization
Use of genetic variants that change a biomarker to test whether the biomarker causes a disease. Ch. 2, 12
Myocardial infarction
Death of heart muscle from loss of blood supply; a heart attack. Ch. 1
Phenotypic modulation
The change of a smooth muscle cell from its contractile state into another cell state. Ch. 8
Plaque rupture, erosion
Tearing of the fibrous cap, or loss of the endothelium over a plaque. Either exposes the plaque to blood and triggers a clot. Ch. 1
Polygenic risk score
A weighted sum of a person's risk alleles across the genome. Ch. 14
Remnant
A triglyceride-rich lipoprotein after partial removal of its triglyceride; cholesterol-rich and able to enter the artery wall. Ch. 4
Residual risk
The risk of events that remains in people whose LDL cholesterol has been lowered. Ch. 13, 16
siRNA
Small interfering RNA: a short double-stranded RNA that silences a gene through the cell's RNA interference pathway. Ch. 13
Thrombus
A blood clot formed inside a vessel. Ch. 11
